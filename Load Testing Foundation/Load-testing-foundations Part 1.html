Before you load test, understand what you're actually testing on.

You've watched teammates run load tests against services in ECS, tweaking config like ecs_task_cpu: 2048 and ecs_task_memory: 8192. Before those numbers mean anything, you need to know what a CPU, a core, a vCPU, a CPU unit, and a worker actually are — and how they all physically relate to each other.

01 — THE PHYSICAL LAYER

What a CPU and a core actually are

Think of the CPU as a small factory, and each core as an independent production line inside it.

A CPU (Central Processing Unit) is the physical chip that executes instructions — every calculation, every comparison, every "if this, do that" in your code eventually becomes a stream of instructions the CPU runs.

Early CPUs had exactly one production line: one core, running one instruction stream at a time. Modern CPUs pack many cores onto a single chip — 4, 8, 16, sometimes 64+. Each core can independently execute its own stream of instructions at the same time as the others. That's real, physical parallelism.

02 — THE CLOUD ABSTRACTION

What is a vCPU, really?

Cloud providers don't hand you a physical core. They hand you a vCPU — a virtual unit that represents your share of compute time on the underlying physical hardware.

Here's the bridge, step by step. Picture a physical core as a single highway lane, and an instruction stream as traffic moving down it. If you owned the lane outright, your code would run on it 100% of the time. But most applications don't need a lane to themselves every second — so instead of handing you the whole lane, the cloud gives you a scheduled slot on it.

A vCPU is that scheduled slot: the operating system lets your code run on the physical core for a tiny fraction of a second, pauses it, lets something else run, then brings your code back — over and over, thousands of times a second. To your app, it feels like owning a dedicated core. In reality, you're sharing execution time on a real one.

Worth knowing — hyperthreading Many physical cores also use hyperthreading (SMT), presenting themselves to the OS as two logical threads instead of one. For sizing your app, it's safe to treat 1 vCPU = 1 core in your calculations — that's exactly what the worker formula later in this article does. Just know that, under the hood, that 1 vCPU is really one logical thread's share of a physical core, not always the full physical silicon to yourself.
03 — ECS'S OWN CURRENCY

What is a "CPU unit," really?

It's normal for this to feel abstract — a CPU unit isn't a physical thing, it's an invented accounting system. Two analogies make it click.

Analogy 1 — The Pizza

1 vCPU is a pizza cut into 1,024 slices

  • A CPU unit is 1 slice.
  • 1024 units = the whole pizza = 1 full vCPU.
  • 512 units = half the pizza = 0.5 vCPU.
  • 256 units = a quarter = 0.25 vCPU.
Analogy 2 — Your internet cafe ✓

1 vCPU is 1 hour of PC time

  • You paid ₹15 for 1 hour (1024 units) of computer access.
  • Split that hour with friends using tickets: 512 units = 30 min, 256 units = 15 min.
  • In hardware, that sharing happens in milliseconds instead of minutes — millions of times a second.

Both analogies land on the same idea: CPU units are a fractional counting system, invented so cloud systems can slice up one physical core's time without forcing everyone to work in messy decimals like 0.25 or 0.5.

Why 1024, specifically?

Computers think in binary, and 2¹⁰ = 1024 — a clean power of two, close to 1,000. When Linux and Docker designed how containers share CPU time, they picked 1024 as the baseline "one full share." AWS ECS simply adopted that same exchange rate:

04 — WHO ACTUALLY HANDS OUT THE UNITS

How your app "consumes" 1024 CPU units

Think of the Linux kernel as the internet cafe manager, holding a stopwatch.

When you set ecs_task_cpu: 1024, you're telling the manager: "this task has paid for 1024 tickets out of 1024." Every few milliseconds (a window Linux calls a CPU period, commonly 100ms), the scheduler looks at every running task and hands out execution time in proportion to its ticket count. A task with 1024 units gets twice the guaranteed time of a task with 512.

An application doesn't "consume" units like fuel — it uses CPU clock cycles during its assigned slice of time. What varies is how much of that slice it actually needs:

What the app is doingUnit usageWhat happens in hardware
Idle / waiting on a DB callLow (~100–200)The core spends most of the slice waiting, mostly idle
Normal trafficMedium (~400–700)The core smoothly runs your code within its slot
Heavy computationMaxed (1024)The core executes your instructions for the full slice
Overloaded — needs 2048, only has 1024Wants >1024Throttled — paused until the next window
What throttling actually feels like A throttled task isn't killed and it isn't rejected. If a task needs 2048 units' worth of computation but only has 1024 configured, it simply uses its full allowance, gets paused, and finishes the remaining work in the next period. A job that should take 1 second now takes 2. To your users, that shows up as latency — not errors.
05 — THE METRIC YOUR TEAM ACTUALLY WATCHES

The throttle ratio, properly

"CPU Utilization: 45%" and "Throttle ratio: 30%" can both be true about the exact same five seconds. They are not measuring the same thing.

Section 04 told you the scheduler hands out CPU time in ~100ms periods and pauses you if you ask for more than you've paid for. The throttle ratio is how you measure how often that pausing actually happens — and it's worth understanding precisely, because it's easy to assume it's counted the same way as your request metrics. It isn't.

The quota is time, not requests

Linux's CFS bandwidth controller gives your task a hard compute budget every period, sized directly from the CPU units you configured:

That quota is one shared pool for the whole task — every worker or thread inside it draws from the same budget every period. It's not a separate quota per worker, which is exactly why the practical run animation throttles the moment all five workers are busy at once: five workers doing heavy work simultaneously drain the shared 200ms (2048-unit) budget far faster than one worker would alone.

Worked example — a request that needs more than its quota

1
Task is allocated 512 CPU units (0.5 vCPU) — a quota of 50ms of real CPU execution per 100ms period.
2
A request needs 140ms of actual CPU work to finish. Nothing about the 100ms period is a deadline for the request itself — it's just the accounting window.
3

4

5

What about 2 vCPU and 4 vCPU?

For this same single request, they behave identically to 1 vCPU. That's not a simplification — it's the actual mechanics, and it's worth sitting with.

A single request runs on a single thread, and a single thread can only ever execute on one core at a time. Whether the task's total quota is 100ms (1 vCPU), 200ms (2 vCPU), or 400ms (4 vCPU) per period, that one thread still can't consume more than 100ms of CPU-time in a 100ms period — there's only one core's worth of instructions it can physically issue. The extra quota from 2 or 4 vCPU sits completely unused by this request.

Where 2 vCPU and 4 vCPU actually pay off Not by making one request faster — by giving more concurrent workers room in the same shared pool. Five workers all doing heavy compute at once will exhaust a 100ms (1 vCPU) quota almost immediately; against a 400ms (4 vCPU) quota, those same five workers can all run flat-out without anyone getting frozen. That's exactly what section 09's worker formula and the practical run animation are sizing for — concurrent demand, not single-request speed.

Why "CPU Utilization: 45%" can still mean severe throttling

This isn't just about bursty traffic — it's built into how the quota is enforced. The CFS bandwidth controller only asks "did this task exceed its own quota within this specific 100ms window," full stop. It never asks whether the wider machine has spare physical capacity sitting idle right next to it. A task can be charged and frozen mid-period even while the box it's running on is mostly idle overall — because the limit is about your slice, not the machine's total headroom.

That's why a handful of worker threads doing short, sharp bursts of computation can rack up a real throttle ratio while the averaged CPU utilization graph — sampled every 30 or 60 seconds — shows something calm like 45%. The average smooths right over the burst that got frozen.

Where teams actually get stuck watching this On raw Linux or Kubernetes with cgroup access, you can read nr_throttled and nr_periods directly. On AWS Fargate, the task metadata endpoint documents a matching field — but it's a known, long-standing bug: that counter stays at 0 even while real throttling is happening. In practice, most ECS/Fargate teams can't read a clean throttle-ratio panel at all — they infer throttling indirectly, from CPU pinned near 100% plus climbing latency, exactly like the diagnostic table below. If your team watches CPU and latency instead of a direct throttle metric, that's not a gap in their setup — that's the actual state of the tooling.

Throttled vs. OOM-killed — two very different kinds of "it stopped"

Throttled (CPU)
The kernel pauses your worker mid-execution and resumes it, unchanged, next period. Nothing is lost — the request just takes longer. Recovers by itself the moment demand drops.
OOM-killed (memory)
The kernel kills the container outright. Any in-flight requests on it are lost. ECS has to notice and restart the task before it can serve anything again — a much harder failure.

This is exactly the difference the practical run animation shows you: the CPU gauge redlining just slows things down, but the memory gauge crossing its ceiling takes an entire worker out.

06 — WORKING MEMORY, NOT STORAGE

What "memory: 8192" is telling you

This is the one that trips almost everyone up.

Memory in ECS is RAM — the fast, temporary workspace your application uses while it's running: variables, loaded objects, request buffers, caches, your app's heap. It empties every time the task restarts.

ecs_task_memory: 8192 means 8,192 MiB (≈8 GB) is the ceiling your task's containers can use during execution. It has nothing to do with how much code or how many files you can "load onto" the vCPU — disk/storage is a completely separate, independently configured resource.

Myth "CPU memory" is a separate resource from regular memory, and 2048 vs 8192 are two flavors of the same thing.
Reality There is no such thing as "CPU memory." You are allocating two independent resources: compute (CPU units → vCPU) and working memory (MiB → RAM). They just sit next to each other in the same config file.

Exceed the memory ceiling, and the Linux OOM (out-of-memory) killer terminates the container — ECS reports the task as stopped, not "disk full." A memory breach and a CPU bottleneck look completely different in your metrics, which is exactly why load testing watches both separately.

08 — HOW MUCH DO YOU ACTUALLY NEED

How do you know how many CPU units your app needs?

Three steps: classify the workload, estimate with math, then confirm by benchmarking.

Step 1 — What kind of workload is it?

I/O-bound → light CPU
Mostly waiting on a database, an external API, or a file read. Doesn't need much raw compute. Start around 256–512 units.
CPU-bound → heavy CPU
Continuous computation with little waiting — image/video processing, PDF generation, ML inference, cryptography. Start at 1024+ units.

Step 2 — Estimate concurrency & required vCPUs

A two-part formula turns a traffic target into a concrete container and vCPU estimate:

Part A: Estimate in-flight concurrency with Little's Law

Little's Law (In-Flight Concurrency) Concurrency = Target RPS × Avg. Response Time

Worked calculation — in-flight requests

1
Target: 500 RPS, average total response time: 200 ms (0.2s).
2
Concurrency = 500 × 0.2 = 100 concurrent in-flight requests at any instant inside the system.

Part B: Turn concurrency into required vCPUs

Crucially, 100 in-flight requests does NOT mean you need 100 vCPUs. During a 200 ms request lifetime, code only actively runs instructions on silicon for a fraction of that time — the rest is spent waiting for a database, Redis, or an external API:

Active CPU time (Tcpu)
Active silicon compute: JSON serialization, parsing, business logic, cryptography. (e.g. 10 ms)
I/O wait time (Tio)
Zero CPU load: sleeping while waiting on database queries, network sockets, or disk. (e.g. 190 ms)
vCPU Demand Formula vCPUs Required = (Target RPS × CPU Time per Req (Tcpu)) ÷ Target Utilization

Worked calculation — vCPU provisioning

1
From APM profiling (e.g., Datadog/CloudWatch): of the 200 ms response time, Tcpu = 10 ms (0.01s), while 190 ms is idle I/O wait.
2
Raw compute demand = 500 RPS × 0.01s = 5.0 vCPUs running at 100% continuous load.
3
Factor in a 70% target utilization sweet spot for headroom (from Step 3): 5.0 ÷ 0.70 = 7.14 vCPUs → round up to 8 vCPUs total.
4
Map to ECS tasks: provision four 2-vCPU tasks (ecs_task_cpu: 2048) or two 4-vCPU tasks (ecs_task_cpu: 4096).
How runtime architecture affects your cluster sizing Async runtimes (Node.js, Go, FastAPI / asyncio) hold hundreds of waiting I/O connections per thread without blocking CPU, so they only need the raw compute allocation: ~8 vCPUs total.
Sync worker runtimes (Gunicorn sync, Puma) block an entire worker thread during the 190 ms I/O wait. To hold 100 concurrent requests without queue backpressure, you need 100 sync workers across your fleet (e.g., 20 tasks of 2 vCPU each, using the formula in Section 09).

Step 3 — Confirm with a real benchmark

Math gives you a starting point. Only a real load test tells you where your specific code actually breaks:

Observed CPU utilization at max loadVerdictAction
< 50%Over-provisionedScale down — save money
60% – 80%Sweet spotKeep as-is — headroom for spikes
~100% / throttling spikesCPU-starvedScale up — latency will climb otherwise

Deploy the same service at 1 vCPU and 2 vCPU, hit both with identical load, and record the RPS each sustains. Now you have a number that means something: "1 vCPU on our stack ≈ X sustained RPS for this endpoint" — specific to your code, not a generic spec sheet.
09 — THE PART THAT ACTUALLY USES THE CPU

Workers: software, not hardware

Easy to mentally place "inside" the CPU — but workers are pure software, sitting in your application's memory.

A worker is a process or thread your application code creates to handle requests. A worker cannot do anything on its own — it has to be scheduled onto a physical core before any of its instructions actually run. Nothing stops you from creating 100 workers on a single core; whether that's a good idea depends entirely on whether they're sync or async.

Sync worker
Handles exactly one request at a time and blocks while waiting on I/O (database calls, external APIs). Examples: Gunicorn's sync workers, Puma's default mode.
Async / event-loop worker
Juggles thousands of in-flight requests on one thread by never blocking on I/O wait. Examples: Node.js, Python asyncio, Netty.

Why more workers than cores can still help

If a sync worker is blocked waiting on a database, it isn't using CPU at all — so extra workers let the core stay busy serving someone else while others wait. This only helps for I/O-bound work. For CPU-bound work, extra workers beyond your core count just add context-switching overhead with no throughput gain.

Common starting rule of thumb — sync workers workers = (2 × cores) + 1

"Cores" here means vCPUs — convert your CPU units first: cores = CPU units ÷ 1024.

10 — THE WHOLE PICTURE, IN ONE MAP

Where does everything actually live?

Cores and vCPUs are hardware, boxed inside the physical CPU chip. Workers are software — they live in your application's memory, and only ever borrow vCPU time.

Dashed border / arrow = software or a scheduling relationship
vCPU (hardware time-slice)
Worker (application software)

Cores and vCPUs are physically boxed inside the CPU chip — that's the hardware domain. Workers live entirely in your application's memory and don't "sit inside" the CPU at all. The OS scheduler is the only thing that connects the two domains: it temporarily lends a worker some vCPU execution time, then takes it back and lends that same time to the next worker in line.

13 — WHAT "ACHIEVED THROUGHPUT" ACTUALLY MEANS

Is throughput just what the CPU can chew through — and is 5 workers a 5 RPS ceiling?

Two questions that sound like they have obvious answers. Neither answer is the obvious one.

Offered load vs. achieved throughput, one more time

Offered load is how many requests are being thrown at your app per second. Achieved throughput is how many of those it actually finishes per second. The gap between them doesn't vanish — it piles up as backlog:

Is the CPU always what caps it?

No — the CPU is just one possible bottleneck. Achieved throughput is capped by whatever resource runs out first, and it's rarely the same one every time:

So — is 5 workers a hard ceiling of 5 RPS?

No. Worker count sets your concurrency ceiling, not your throughput ceiling.

Little's Law, rearranged, gives you throughput directly:

Throughput, solved from Little's Law Throughput = Concurrency ÷ Avg. Response Time

5 workers is your concurrency — how many requests can be in flight at the same instant. How many requests-per-second that turns into depends entirely on how fast each one finishes:

Myth Worker count is your throughput ceiling — 5 workers means 5 RPS, max.
Reality Worker count is your concurrency ceiling. Throughput is concurrency divided by response time — so fewer, faster workers can out-throughput more, slower ones. This is exactly why the practical run animation's same 5 workers achieve very different numbers across one loop: during ramp-up, service time is 0.6s and achieved throughput sits near 8 RPS; at peak/soak, heavier compute pushes service time to 1.1s and throughput drops to around 4–5 RPS. Same concurrency, same worker count — the response time changed, so the throughput did too.

15 — WHY THIS EXISTS

Why a load test happens, and what you're actually hunting for

Nobody runs a load test because they have k6 installed. They run one because someone has a specific, falsifiable worry.

Before a single request fires, someone asks a real question: "Will this survive Black Friday at 3× normal traffic?" or "We just changed the DB connection pool — did we just create a new ceiling?" That question sets the target load and the pass/fail bar before the test plan exists. Without it, a load test is just noise with graphs attached.

The signals you hunt for, not the vibes

A load tester doesn't watch one number and call it done. They watch a small set of signals, each answering a different question about the same system — exactly what the CPU gauge, memory gauge, and queue depth in the practical run above were showing you all at once.

None of these tell the story alone. CPU at 100% with flat latency can be perfectly fine — an async worker pool doing exactly what it's designed to do. CPU at 40% with latency climbing means the bottleneck isn't the box you're staring at — it's a downstream database, a lock, or a slow dependency somewhere else in the chain.

Myth A load test that finds no problems is a successful load test.
Reality Finding nothing usually means you didn't push hard enough. A test that only confirms the happy path re-confirms something nobody doubted. The value is in what breaks — because that's the number that was never actually written down anywhere.

The mindset: hunting for where it breaks, not whether it works

A tester who stops the moment the target load passes hasn't learned anything new yet. The real work starts after that — deliberately pushing past the target until something gives, for two reasons.

Reason 1

You need the real ceiling

  • Not the one in the task definition's cpu / memory values — the one your specific code actually hits.
  • That's the only number that tells you how much margin you really have left.
Reason 2 ✓

You need to know how it fails

  • Graceful — latency climbs, no errors, recovers the moment load drops. Like ramp-down in the animation above.
  • Catastrophic — cascading OOM kills, connection pool exhaustion, a retry storm making things worse. Like the OOM kill in the animation above.
  • Each demands a completely different fix — so you have to actually see it happen, not assume it.

So the honest scorecard isn't "did it pass." Good is: "I found the ceiling, and it fails the way I'd want it to, with a margin I can defend." Bad isn't "the test found a problem" — bad is "the test didn't find the problem that's waiting in production."

CHEAT SHEET

Recap — reading your ECS config from now on

  • 1
    CPU — the physical chip; cores are its independent execution lanes.
    More cores = more true parallelism, not just faster single-thread work.
  • 2
    vCPU — your reserved share of one core's execution time. Safe to treat as 1 vCPU = 1 core when sizing workers.
    Under the hood, it's one hyperthread's slice of a physical core.
  • 3
    CPU units — ECS's own scale, where 1024 units = 1 vCPU (think: 1024 pizza slices, or your internet cafe's 60-minute hour).
    2048 → 2 vCPU. 512 → half a vCPU.
  • 4
    The scheduler hands out time in ~100ms periods, proportional to your ticket count.
    Need more than you've paid for? You get throttled — paused, not killed — and finish next period.
  • 5
    Throttle ratio — nr_throttled ÷ nr_periods, straight from the cgroup. Time-slices, not requests.
    A different metric from backlog/queue ratio — but throttling now causes backlog moments later.
  • 6
    Memory (MiB) — RAM available while the task runs, not disk or "CPU memory."
    8192 → 8 GB RAM ceiling. Exceed it and the container gets OOM-killed — a harder failure than throttling.
  • 7
    Workers — software, not hardware. They live in your app's memory and borrow vCPU time.
    Sync workers ≈ (2 × cores) + 1; async workers scale differently for I/O-bound work.
  • 8
    RPS vs. Throughput — RPS is a rate; throughput is what you can sustain without a growing backlog.
    Watch offered load vs. achieved throughput diverge to find your system's real ceiling.
  • 9
    Little's Law & vCPU Sizing — Concurrency = RPS × Total Response Time; Required vCPUs = (RPS × Tcpu) ÷ Target Utilization.
    Turns a target RPS and active CPU time into concrete container task and vCPU requirements.
  • 10
    Worker count ≠ throughput ceiling — Throughput = Concurrency ÷ Response Time.
    5 workers at 10ms each = 500 RPS; 5 workers at 1000ms each = 5 RPS. Same concurrency, very different throughput.
