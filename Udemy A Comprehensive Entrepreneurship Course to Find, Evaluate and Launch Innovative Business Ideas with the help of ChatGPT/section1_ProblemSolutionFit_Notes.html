1Introduction to the Course

Lecture 1

What this course covers

The course has two parts:

  • Part 1 — Evaluate: assess whether an idea has potential, using questions, brainstorming, and ChatGPT as a research/brainstorming partner.
  • Part 2 — Test: validate real demand in the real world, spending as little money/time as possible — actual data from actual people, not just assumptions.
Core philosophy Nobody — not you, not an investor, not ChatGPT — is qualified to judge an idea in advance. You can only lower the randomness by testing cheaply, then doubling down on whatever gets paying customers.

Who is this course for?

Innovative / startup ideas, or traditional businesses with a strong tech/innovation angle. A plain restaurant, shop, or hotel with no innovative twist is out of scope.

Important caveat repeated all through the course ChatGPT is a brainstorming tool, not an oracle. Its answers can be confidently wrong — always treat output as a hypothesis to verify, never as a fact.

2Introduction to ChatGPT and its Limitations

Lecture 2

Interface basics

  • GPT‑3.5 (free) vs. GPT‑4 (paid) — GPT‑4 is smarter and more accurate, but 3.5 is perfectly usable for brainstorming.
  • Past conversations persist — you can reopen a thread and ChatGPT keeps the full context, letting you refine iteratively instead of starting over.

Meta-trick: ask ChatGPT how to prompt itself

Instead of hunting for prompt-engineering guides online, just ask the model directly.

PROMPT → What is the best way to ask you to find business ideas?

ChatGPT will typically reply that it needs: industry/market, target audience, your resources/skills, budget, and business type — i.e., context.

Known limitation at time of recording Training data cut off around 2021 — no awareness of anything after that. Always sanity-check anything time-sensitive.
Missing ingredient spotted in this lecture The model's own suggested prompt list left out country — a reminder that even ChatGPT's advice on how to use itself is incomplete, so you still have to think critically about context.

3The Absolute Best Way to Use ChatGPT

Lecture 3

The 3-part prompt framework

STEP 1
Role
"Act as an industry expert / CEO / lawyer…"
STEP 2
Task
What you actually want produced
STEP 3
Context
Country, constraints, your resources, format…

Why Role matters: it primes the model to draw from the relevant "region" of its training data (e.g., "industry expert" pulls different associations than a generic answer).

Worked example prompt

Act as an industry expert. Generate 4 business ideas based on current unfulfilled demand and industries ripe for disruption in the United States. Then generate a basic business plan for each: name, one-liner, target customer persona, customer pain points, main value proposition, sales/marketing channels, revenue streams, cost structure, key activities, key resources, key partners, idea validation steps, estimated first-year cost, potential challenges. Write the result in a markdown table.

Iterative refinement — the real power move

Example flow from the lecture: after generating "Telehealth for Mental Health," the model flagged regulatory compliance as a challenge. Rather than restart, you refine inside the same conversation:

Come up with two more ideas, but which don't have regulatory compliance problems, licensing considerations, or security concerns.

This is what "context" really means — every follow-up question adds a constraint the model must keep satisfying, converging on something more useful with each turn.

Common mistake Thinking ChatGPT replaces domain knowledge. It augments — you still need to understand the underlying business theory (which is exactly why this course has "boring" theory lectures) to ask good questions and judge the answers.

5What Customers Really Want? — Introduction

Lecture 5

The scary stat

75% of venture-funded startups fail to return investor capital (Harvard Business School lecturer's study, 2,000+ companies funded in 2012). It's worse for non-venture-backed startups, which run out of cash even faster.

Why they actually fail

Per a 2014 CB Insights survey of 100+ founders:

RankReason for failure
#1No market need — customers simply didn't want the product
#2Ran out of cash
#3Wrong team
The cat-tower analogy You build an elaborate cat climbing tower... and the cat only wants the cardboard box it came in. Effort and cleverness spent building something ≠ something people actually want.

Product-out vs. Customer-in

🚀
Rocket approach
(traditional / product-first)
Build the whole thing, launch, hope it's right. Fine for big companies who already know their market. Risky for startups: no course-correction once launched.
🚗
Car approach
(customer-first)
Focus on customers → learn what they want → then build. You can steer, brake, or turn at any point. This is the Lean Startup approach (Blank / Ries / Maurya).

The core equation

A startup idea = Problem + Solution + Customer. Assessing an idea quickly means assessing:

  1. How big is the problem?
  2. How good is the solution?

(Customer persona is covered separately in lectures 11–12.)

6Evaluate the Problem You're Trying to Solve

Lecture 6

Three questions determine whether a problem is "big enough":

🔁
Frequent?
Ideally occurs ≥ once/day for the target user.
🌍
Common?
Shared by many people/businesses, not a niche few.
💳
Worth solving?
Serious enough that people will actually pay.

Win / Fail case studies

CriterionCompanyWhat happened
FrequentPlan (event-sharing app) People only share/look for events a couple of times a month — not often enough to build a habit-forming app around. FAIL
FrequentFacebook / Twitter / Instagram / Snapchat Status updates, news, photos, messages — used dozens of times per day. WIN
CommonPatient Communicator Built appointment reminders for doctors, but doctors are highly resistant to new tech in their workflow — after huge outreach effort, only 1 paying doctor signed up. FAIL
CommonZocDoc Charges doctors $300/mo to be listed; solves what doctors actually want (more patients), fully disconnected from their internal systems. 6M+ users/month, 2,000 cities. WIN
Worth solvingZomato "Cashless Payments" (Dubai, 2015) Let diners pay before eating (like Uber). Shut down — people are used to eating first, paying after; the problem wasn't actually painful enough to change that habit. FAIL
Worth solvingSquare Small merchants were paying exorbitant card fees with poor support. Square gave a simple iPad-based, user-friendly alternative — a real, expensive pain point. WIN
Recap — the ideal problem Frequent + Common + Worth solving. Score your own idea against these three before moving on.

7Use ChatGPT to Evaluate the Problem You're Solving

Lecture 7

Running example used for the rest of the course: an AI assistant for elderly people.

Step 1 — surface the problems

I am the CEO of a startup creating an AI assistant for elderly people. Describe the problem that this product solves.

ChatGPT returned five candidate problems: social isolation & loneliness, limited mobility/accessibility, safety & security concerns, health management, and simplified technology interaction.

Step 2 — score them against the 3 criteria

Rate these 5 problems from 1–10 based on: - How frequent is this problem? (per day/month/year) - How common is it to people? - Is it worth solving? (serious enough that people would pay) Organize the results in a table.
ProblemFrequencyCommonalityWorth solving
Social isolation & loneliness8/109/1010/10
Safety & security concerns6/10—10/10
Simplified technology interaction6/10——

(Only the scores explicitly discussed in the lecture are shown — the full table had all 5 rows filled in.)

How to read this table A perfect 10/10/10 problem essentially doesn't exist. Look for the highest combination — here, social isolation (8, 9, 10) stands out as the strongest candidate to build around.
Why the numbers make sense Safety incidents (falls, emergencies) don't happen often (6/10 frequency) — but when they do, the stakes are high, hence a 10/10 on "worth solving." Frequency and severity are independent dimensions.

9Evaluate the Solution (Your Business Idea)

Lecture 9

Same logic as the problem, but now applied to your proposed solution. Three questions:

✨
Unique?
Differentiated enough that competitors can't just swallow you.
🔒
Protectable?
Hard for others to copy once it works.
📈
Sustainable?
Economically viable long-term, not just in a launch spike.
CriterionCompanyWhat happened
UniqueMatch.com / eHarmony Won over 30+ Gen X singles, but Gen Y/Z hated the long profiles and 200-question compatibility quizzes. FAIL (with younger users)
UniqueTinder Ditched long quizzes for 3 simple signals — location, age, looks — and a swipe interface. Radically simpler than the crowded market. WIN
ProtectableGroupon Innovative group-buying model (2008) was trivially copyable — hundreds of clones appeared worldwide; Groupon had to acquire many just to keep its position. FAIL
ProtectablePremise Network of 25,000 people worldwide collecting retail price data — protection comes from unique data access + network, not a patent. WIN
SustainableHomejoy Homeowners & cleaners took the relationship offline to skip fees; Groupon discounts attracted one-time bargain hunters instead of repeat customers → ran out of cash, shut down. FAIL
SustainableTaskRabbit / Elance Diversified into many recurring services (moving, delivery, parties / programming, design, translation) — recurring, higher-value transactions sustain the business. WIN
Pattern spotted in "sustainable" wins On-demand platforms do better when the work is intellectual/skilled, pay is higher, and transactions repeat — the opposite of a one-off, price-sensitive, easily-disintermediated service.

10Use ChatGPT to Evaluate and Improve Your Business

Lecture 10

Reality check first No solution is ever perfectly unique, protectable, and sustainable all at once — that combination doesn't exist. The goal is to score as well as possible, and to keep improving even if the current score is weak.

Asking too broadly doesn't work

I am the CEO of a company that creates an AI assistant for elderly people. How unique do you think my solution is compared to competitors and substitutes?

ChatGPT correctly declined to give a real assessment — it has no specifics about your product's actual features, so it can't judge uniqueness. Lesson: vague input → vague or evasive output. The model needs the specific feature-level detail that differentiates your product.

What ChatGPT is genuinely good for here: generating differentiation ideas

Instead of asking it to judge uniqueness, ask it to suggest ways to build uniqueness: e.g., specialized features (medication reminders, fall detection, telehealth integration), adaptive personalization, accessibility, device integration, support network, and data privacy/security.

Ten ways to protect a solution (from the prompt in this lecture)

List 10 ways I can protect such a solution from competitors, so it's not easy to just create and sell the same product.
  • Patents, trademarks, copyrights
  • Trade secrets (algorithms, data, methods) + confidentiality agreements
  • Proprietary technology — continuous innovation
  • Strong partnerships (e.g., healthcare providers, senior living communities)
  • Regular updates
  • Strong brand identity & reputation
  • Customer loyalty programs
  • Network effects
  • Diversification
  • Legal action against infringers

Notice patents/IP are only one of ten levers — for early-stage startups, data access, partnerships, and brand often matter more because formal IP protection is slow and expensive.

11Draw Your Customer Persona

Lecture 11

The customer is whoever pays — not necessarily whoever uses the product. (E.g., an ad-based product has both a free user and a paying advertiser — you must address both.)

Exercise

Literally take paper and draw your ideal customer. First decide: B2C (mass market) or B2B (companies)?

B2C — think aboutB2B — think about
Gender · Age · Marital status (married/single/divorced) · Occupation · Interests · Typical day · Education level · Origin Company size (small/medium/large) · Industry · Headcount · Which department needs this most
The goal — Problem-Solution Fit You've reached it when the customer is genuinely ready to pay because the solution addresses their biggest problem. That's the target this entire section builds toward.

12Use ChatGPT to Help Define Your Customer Persona

Lecture 12

Step 1 — brainstorm broad segments

Describe 20 possible customer segments for an assistant for elderly people, and separate them into B2B and B2C (10 each).

Result included: elderly living alone, elderly with disabilities/memory issues, caregivers of elderly people, elderly wanting to stay active, those needing medical assistance — plus a parallel B2B list.

A key realization from this exercise The user (elderly person) and the customer (who pays) are often different — here, the actual paying customer is more likely the caregiver, not the elderly user themselves.

Step 2 — zoom into one segment (B2C)

Describe the ideal family/caregiver customer: age, gender, marital status, occupation, hobbies, education level, origin.

Output: wide age range (young adult to elderly), likely skews female, marital status/occupation/hobbies/education don't strongly matter.

Stay skeptical The "likely predominantly female" claim is a plausible-sounding assumption, not verified fact — exactly the kind of claim to double- and triple-check with real research, not accept at face value.

Step 3 — zoom into one segment (B2B)

Describe the ideal home healthcare provider B2B customer — company size, industry, number of employees, and department.

Output: mid-to-large healthcare companies with multiple locations (more resources to adopt new tech); healthcare industry; dozens to thousands of employees; buyer is likely IT (implementation) + senior management (financial/operational case).

Bonus move You can go one step further and ask: "List 10 companies like this in the US" — giving you real, named candidates to research as potential first customers or design partners (always verify — don't assume the list is accurate).
