20 Introduction to the Lean Canvas

What is the Lean Canvas and why do we need it?

What is it?

The Lean Canvas is a free, one-page tool to write down all the assumptions you are making about your startup idea. It is inspired by the famous Business Model Canvas, but simplified for early-stage startups.

Core idea — "known unknowns": There are things you know, things you know you don't know, and things you don't even know you don't know. The Lean Canvas is about writing down the "known unknowns" — things you assume are true but haven't tested yet. Later, you go and test each assumption to see if it's really true or false.

Lean Canvas vs Business Model Canvas

  • Both have 9 blocks.
  • 5 of the 9 blocks are different between the two.
  • Business Model Canvas → built for established, running businesses.
  • Lean Canvas → built for early-stage startups that have nothing yet (no key resources, no partners, no real customers yet).

Recommended order to fill it out

1. Problem 2. Customer Segments 3. Unique Value Proposition 4. Solution 5. Channels 6. Revenue Streams 7. Cost Structure 8. Key Metrics 9. Unfair Advantage

You don't need to invent things you don't have yet (like "key resources" on the old canvas). That's exactly why the Lean Canvas replaces those blocks with things a new startup can actually answer.

Running example used in this section: Snapchat

Example Founded in 2011 by Evan Spiegel and Bobby Murphy (a third co-founder, Reggie Brown, left early). The course uses Snapchat throughout to show how each block of the canvas is filled in real life.
Action: Download the Lean Canvas template (Word/PDF), and fill it block-by-block as you watch the next lectures.

21 Part 1: Problem, Customer Segments, Unique Value Proposition

The first three blocks of the canvas

Block 1 — Problem

List the top 3 customer problems. Keep them specific. Remember the rule from earlier sections: a good problem is frequent, common, and worth solving. It also helps to list existing alternatives — how people solve this problem today without your product — so you can see what's missing.

Snapchat's 3 problems
  1. People worry that social networks (like Facebook) misuse their data and don't respect privacy.
  2. People regret sharing things online that can't be taken back.
  3. Teenagers don't like being watched by parents, teachers, or future employers.

Block 2 — Customer Segments

Steps to fill this block:

  1. Split your broad audience into smaller, specific sub-segments.
  2. Pick the strongest segment to focus on first.
  3. Separate customers from users.
Customers vs Users: Customers are the people who pay you. Users are the people who use the product but may not pay anything. Both go in this block, because in many business models there are no customers without users first.
Analogy — The Simpsons Parents = customers (they have the money). Kids = users (they use the product but don't pay).
Snapchat Users: mostly teenagers (early adopters, mostly free users).
Customers: some teenagers who buy virtual goods (like "Snap Replays"), but the real money comes from advertisers (Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Universal) — because teenagers are a highly attractive audience for ads.

Block 3 — Unique Value Proposition (UVP)

This is basically a marketing catchphrase — a single, clear sentence that hooks people's attention. It is not the same as your solution: it doesn't list features, just the one thing that makes you stand out. You usually see it on the homepage of a company's website.

Snapchat's real UVP "Experience a totally new way to share today."

Quick recap

  • Problem: Top 3 problems, must be frequent + common + worth solving.
  • Customer Segments: Narrow down → pick strongest segment → split customers vs users.
  • UVP: One catchy sentence that hooks attention — not a feature list.

22 Part 2: Solution, Channels, Revenue Streams

The middle three blocks of the canvas

Block 4 — Solution

Best practice: outline a solution for each problem you listed earlier. It doesn't need to be a totally different product each time — it can just be a different feature.

Snapchat: problem → solution mapping
  • Privacy worry → Snaps are deleted from Snapchat's servers once they disappear.
  • Regret sharing → Content automatically vanishes after a few seconds (though screenshots are still a loophole).
  • Fear of being watched → Vanishing "notes" feel private; also, parents weren't yet on Snapchat the way they were on Facebook.

Block 5 — Channels

Channels are all about how you reach your customers. There are two types:

TypeWhat it meansExamples
Sales channelsWhere the product is actually purchased/downloaded App Store, Google Play, e-commerce sites, phone sales
Marketing channelsHow people find out about the product Google Ads, Facebook Ads, affiliate marketing (e.g. Rakuten), blog promotions (e.g. Reddit)
Snapchat's growth channel: word of mouth Snapchat wasn't popular at launch (2011). It took off after Evan Spiegel's mom told her niece about it at Orange County High School — the word spread through the school. Teenagers hang out in groups, so once one person had the app, friends followed. This is how it went viral organically.

Block 6 — Revenue Streams

This block is about how the business actually makes money.

Common pattern for ad-based startups: Many startups (especially ones betting on advertising) don't make money for years. They first need to grow to millions of users and show "hockey-stick growth" so advertisers see a big enough audience worth paying for. Until then, they burn cash.
Snapchat's revenue streams (started making real money in 2015)
  • Sponsored lenses — brands pay (up to $750,000 on peak days like Halloween) to have a fun filter people use and share with friends — essentially free viral marketing for the brand.
  • Sponsored Geofilters — location-based branded stickers (e.g. a burger brand's sticker near their restaurants).
  • Selling virtual goods — e.g. paying $0.99 for 3 "replays" of a snap (interesting: this slightly contradicts their "disappearing forever" promise, showing how startups test monetization boundaries).
  • Snapcash — send money to friends in-app; a first step toward mobile payments and possibly taking a cut on purchases later.

Quick recap

  • Solution: One idea per problem — doesn't need to be a new product each time.
  • Channels: Sales channels (where they buy) + Marketing channels (how they hear about you).
  • Revenue Streams: How you make money — and expect it may take time if you rely on ads.

23 Part 3: Cost Structure, Key Metrics, Unfair Advantage

The final three blocks of the canvas

Block 7 — Cost Structure

List your costs, split into two types:

TypeMeaningExamples
Fixed costsStay the same no matter how much you sell Office rent, electricity, insurance
Variable costsGo up or down depending on usage/sales Servers (more users = more cost), communication, travel

Block 8 — Key Metrics

Metrics are the numbers that tell you if the business is doing well or going off track. Split them into two kinds:

  • Business metrics — conversion rate, retention rate, % of paying vs free users, daily active users (DAU), monthly active users (MAU), number of downloads.
  • Personal metrics — your own long-term goals, e.g. "10,000 customers by end of year" or "$100M revenue in 3 years."
Snapchat's key metrics Conversion rate, daily active users, number of snaps sent per day worldwide, and average time spent in the app per day.

Block 9 — Unfair Advantage

Similar to a "competitive advantage" — but specifically something hard to copy or buy. It's usually not obvious in the early stage.

Types of unfair advantage:
  • A secret formula/patent (e.g. Coca-Cola's formula).
  • An expert on the founding team (e.g. a rocket engineer from SpaceX joining your space startup).
  • Customer lock-in (e.g. Apple's proprietary chargers force you to keep buying Apple accessories).
Snapchat's unfair advantage Word of mouth + network effects, and being extremely popular with teenagers — an audience even Facebook struggled to reach. This is so strong that Facebook offered $3 billion to acquire Snapchat; the 23-year-old CEO refused, and the company later became worth much more.

Quick recap

  • Cost Structure: Fixed costs (constant) vs Variable costs (scale with usage).
  • Key Metrics: Business metrics (numbers) + Personal metrics (your own goals).
  • Unfair Advantage: Something competitors can't easily copy or buy.

Important reminder: Everything on the Lean Canvas is an assumption — not proven fact. The next step (next section) is to actually test these assumptions one by one.

24 Practice: Download and Fill out the Lean Canvas

Hands-on exercise

Action: Download the Lean Canvas (available in both Word and PDF) and fill it out for your own startup idea.

Tips from the instructors:

  • It may feel a bit academic/formal at first, but it's a genuinely useful brainstorming tool.
  • Fill it out with a co-founder or another person if possible — more people means more ideas and creativity.

25 Use ChatGPT to Help Define Your Business Model

Using AI as a brainstorming assistant — but only after trying it yourself first

Golden rule: Do the Lean Canvas yourself first. If you jump straight to ChatGPT, it's too easy — you don't train your own thinking. Use ChatGPT afterward to fill gaps, double-check, and get extra ideas.

Example used here: AI assistant for elderly people

Prompt 1 — Desirability & Viability assumptions

Before even touching the canvas, you can ask ChatGPT to list your assumptions in two categories:

  • Desirability assumptions — will people actually want this? (e.g. "elderly people will want companionship," "they'll want emergency support," "they'll find it convenient")
  • Viability assumptions — is this even possible to build/run? (e.g. "the AI must be reliable and user-friendly," "it must be affordable," "it must be secure")
Sample prompt "I want to create an AI assistant for elderly people. List in two columns: desirability assumptions and viability assumptions." (You can also ask for a specific number, like "list 10" or "list 20.")

Using ChatGPT for each canvas block

You can ask ChatGPT to brainstorm ideas for literally any block on the canvas:

BlockExample prompt
Problem"Describe 10 problems that an assistant for elderly people could solve."
Unique Value Proposition"List 5 unique value propositions for [this solution]."
Revenue Streams"Imagine 10 revenue models for such a business." Then push further: "Come up with more innovative, unlikely revenue models."
Channels"Imagine 10 sales channels for such a company."
Customer Segments"Who could be the customers for such a solution?"
Cost Structure"Imagine the cost structure of such a company."
Unfair AdvantageAsk what the "unfair advantage" could be — if ChatGPT doesn't get the term, simply ask it to "list them."
Sample ChatGPT ideas that came up
  • Problems: medication reminders, appointment scheduling, grocery shopping help, home safety monitoring, reducing social isolation, memory support, entertainment.
  • Revenue models: subscription, commission, advertising, data licensing, donation model, blockchain/crypto rewards, gamified social-enterprise model.
  • Customer segments: elderly people living alone, family caregivers, healthcare providers, senior living communities, insurance companies, transportation companies, financial institutions.
  • Unfair advantages: personalized support, advanced AI capabilities, remote monitoring, partnerships with healthcare/insurance companies, strong data & analytics, security/privacy, brand recognition, customer loyalty.
Important nuance: ChatGPT can list many possible unfair advantages, but you can't chase all of them at once. Your job is to pick one or two to focus your effort on — trying to have every advantage at the same time is unrealistic for a startup.

Quick recap

ChatGPT is a great brainstorming tool for the Lean Canvas — it's fast at generating raw ideas for any block. But it should support your own thinking, not replace the exercise of doing the canvas yourself first.

26 Should you keep your startup idea secret?

"Ask Gollum about it" — a short but important mindset lesson

Main takeaway: Talk about your startup idea as much as possible. If you keep it secret, nothing moves forward — you need feedback, connections, and momentum to actually build something.

The one exception

Only stay quiet if both of these are true:

  • You are an expert engineer in a very complex, specific technical field, and
  • You've found a specific technical solution to a hard problem

In that narrow case, don't share the technical details with someone who has the exact same skills as you (a rival engineer or competing company). For every other situation, execution matters far more than secrecy.

Example — Airbnb Airbnb was rejected by investors dozens of times — almost no one believed in the idea. It still became a massive success. Once it grew, copycats appeared everywhere. The lesson: people only start copying you once you're already making serious money — until then, the risk of "idea theft" is very small or marginal.

Quick recap

Ideas are worth very little without execution. Share your idea, get feedback, and move fast — that matters far more than protecting it from being "stolen."
