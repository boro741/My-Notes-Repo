13Evaluate the Existing Competition and Substitutes

Lecture 13

Section 1 covered problem, solution, and customer. This section adds two more dimensions: competition and market size — the numbers investors actually care about in a pitch deck.

The uncomfortable reflex Many founders avoid googling their own idea because they're afraid of finding competitors and getting discouraged. Do it anyway — search under several different phrasings to catch both direct and indirect competitors.

Scenario 1 — You find no competition at all

Don't celebrate — get suspicious No competition usually means no market: nobody else is chasing paying customers because there aren't any. Telling investors "I have zero competition" is itself a red flag to them, not a strength.

Two possible explanations if this happens:

ExplanationWhat it means for you
There's genuinely no marketLet the idea go — nobody's coming to the party.
You're radically ahead of the curveYou'll need huge resources/time to create the market from scratch — high risk, high reward (see Tesla below).
Tesla — creating a market that didn't exist Sports cars had heavy competition; electric cars had light competition. Tesla combined both into "electric sports cars," a market with essentially no competitors — and nearly went bankrupt multiple times doing it. Even with Elon Musk-level resources, creating a market is the riskiest form of innovation.
Timing failures — right idea, wrong decade Microsoft invented the tablet first, but Apple popularized it. Virtual reality was pitched in the 1980s–90s, but hardware quality was poor and prices too high — the market wasn't ready yet.

Scenario 2 — Some (sparse) competition

TimingSignal
Early — other startups (not giants) are exploring the space Encouraging: the space is growing but not yet dominated. Example: launching a mobile messaging app in 2009, alongside early WhatsApp, Viber, WeChat, and Beluga (later acquired → Facebook Messenger) — smartphone adoption was just taking off. GOOD TIMING
Late — a "unicorn" (>$1B valuation) already dominates Discouraging: WhatsApp sold for $19B just 4 years after launch; Uber is worth $60B+. Launching the same idea a few years later means fighting an entrenched giant. BAD TIMING
The exception: significant structural advantage Didi Kuaidi (China) and Ola (India) competed with Uber successfully because they had comparable capital (backed by Alibaba/Tencent) and home-market advantages Uber couldn't easily replicate. Without a comparable edge, don't attempt to out-fund an incumbent.

Scenario 3 — Overcrowded market

Default answer: stay away In a saturated market, everyone fights over shrinking pieces of share using the same old sales/marketing playbook.
The exception: innovate instead of just competing Spotify entered an overcrowded music industry (including Apple's iTunes) but changed the revenue model itself — subscriptions and a free tier instead of paying per song. You don't out-market an overcrowded field, you out-innovate it (these are called "innovation hacks," covered in a separate Business Model Innovation course).

Recap table

You find…Outcome AOutcome B
No competitionNo market → drop itVery innovative → huge effort to create a market
Some competitionEarly stage / good timing → go for itUnicorn already dominant → drop it unless you have a real edge
Lots of competitionTraditional approach → drop itYou can innovate to stand out → worth pursuing
Exercise Google your idea with several different wordings. Map out which of the three scenarios (and which outcome within it) you're in.

14Use ChatGPT to Research your Competition

Lecture 14

Step 1 — surface direct competitors

I want to create an AI assistant for elderly people. List 10 up and coming startups in that area which recently received venture capital funding.
Data cutoff applies here too The list can be outdated or made up — every company name has to be individually googled and verified. In the lecture, one result ("Care Coach") turned out to have pivoted into something broader, while another ("Lifeboat Solutions") checked out as real and active.

Step 2 — widen the net

Don't stop at 10 — ask for 20+. Don't be discouraged by the list; there will always be other people working in your space.

Step 3 — ask about substitutes (indirect competition)

What are the current substitutes for such a product?

Because the conversation retains context, ChatGPT understands "such a product" without you re-describing it. For the elderly-assistant example: personal caregivers, community centers, medical alert systems.

What are the current non-technological substitutes for such a product?

This variant strips out tech-sounding competitors and surfaces what people use instead of any technology at all — useful for understanding the true baseline you're competing against.

Direct vs. indirect competition Direct = same solution, same technology. Indirect/substitute = different approach, same underlying need being met. You need to map both before judging how crowded your space really is.

15List: Best ChatGPT Prompts to Research your Competition

Lecture 15 — reference list

  • I am creating a [product/service description]. List 10 up and coming startups in that area which recently received venture capital funding.
  • List the main competitors in the [industry/niche] (and compare them to [my company/product]).
  • List my competitors' strengths and weaknesses and how I can use this information to gain a competitive advantage.
  • What kind of [products/services] do my competitors offer and how do they compare to [my product/service]?
  • Share a PESTEL analysis of [competitor's business].
  • What [pricing/marketing] strategies does [competitor] use?
  • What kind of content does [competitor] publish and how can it be improved?
  • What kind of social media presence does [competitor] have and how can I leverage social media to compete with them?
  • What kind of partnerships and collaborations does [competitor] have?

Swap the bracketed placeholders for your own business — keep every claim verified rather than taken at face value.

16Evaluate the Market Potential of Your Business Idea

Lecture 16

Three cases, in increasing order of difficulty:

🚕
Known market, known product
Easiest — reports and data already exist.
📱
Known market, innovative product
Harder — you may spill into adjacent markets too.
🚁
No known market at all
Near-impossible to size precisely — rely on proxies.

Case 1 — Known market: Total Addressable Market (TAM)

Global taxi & limousine market ~$100B / yr
Germany taxi & limousine market ~€2.9B / yr
BlackLane's limousine-only segment ~€1B / yr (35% of German market)

TAM = revenue you'd capture at a hypothetical 100% market share. Build it top-down: global figure → country figure → your specific segment's share of that country figure.

Missing proportion? Extrapolate from a comparable country If you can't find what % of the German taxi market is limousines, but you know the US limousine segment is ~25% of its taxi/limo market, use that as an approximate proxy — weaker, but usable.
TAM ≠ what you'll actually get 100% market share (pure monopoly) never happens. You still need a separate, subjective estimate of your realistic share — e.g. 5%, 10%, 15% — based on your business model and available capital.

Case 2 — Known market, but an innovative product

Why Uber is worth so much more than "just" the taxi market If valued only on the global taxi/limousine market (~$60B+), Uber's valuation wouldn't make sense on its own. Uber actually captures pieces of multiple markets at once:
Market Uber also capturesExample
Public transportationMetro/tram/bus riders switching to Uber in big cities
Food deliveryUber Eats, Uber Fresh
Parcel deliveryUber Rush
Car ownershipPeople who'd otherwise buy a car choose Uber instead

Concrete number from the lecture: in San Francisco, the entire taxi market is worth ~$140M/yr, yet Uber alone makes ~$500M/yr there — proof much of its revenue comes from people who never used taxis in the first place.

Case 3 — No known market yet

Use as many different proxies as possible: data from other countries, or data from substitute products/services.

Drone delivery — an illustrative proxy chain No existing "drone delivery market" to size directly, so borrow from comparable signals: Amazon Prime's under-1-hour delivery service in parts of the US (proxy for demand for ultra-fast delivery), DHL's real drone trial delivering medication to a remote German island (proxy for remote-area demand), and general infrastructure gaps in remote or poor regions (additional addressable segment).
Be honest about the uncertainty In this case your market-size estimate will always be rough. The most reliable next step isn't a better spreadsheet — it's testing the actual product with real customers (covered later in the course).
Recap Combine market reports, country data, competitor numbers, substitute-product data, and proxies. The goal was never a perfectly precise figure — it's a plausibility check on your idea's potential, scaled to your own ambition.

17Use ChatGPT to Evaluate your Potential Market

Lecture 17

Expect evasiveness on hard numbers ChatGPT often avoids committing to a precise figure (it "doesn't want responsibility" for a wrong number). Sometimes you get lucky — e.g. it cited a Statista figure of ~€1.3B for the German limousine industry in 2020 — but 2020 was a lockdown year, so treat even a concrete-looking answer as needing context.

When it won't answer directly, ask for the process instead

List the steps to find out the Total Addressable Market [for the limousine company in Germany].

General steps it gives: define the market → identify target customers → determine target market size → estimate the percentage of that market you can capture. Because the conversation retains context, you often don't even need to restate "for the limousine company in Germany."

Then work the steps one at a time, in the same thread

Expand on "identify target customers" and execute it.

→ Business professionals, tourists, event attendees, luxury transportation seekers, etc.

Estimate the total addressable market of business professionals in Germany who use limousine services.
Critical skill: spotting weak reasoning In the lecture, ChatGPT's first attempt simply equated "business professionals" with all employed people in Germany (43.9M), then arbitrarily assumed 10% are "business professionals" → 4.4M. That chain of assumptions is too crude to trust.

Refine the segment definition

Estimate the number of business executives (managers, C-suite, etc.) in Germany.

Better answer: ~3.3M companies in Germany (2020) → assuming ≥1 executive each → at least 3.3M; cross-checked against a separate estimate of ~2.7M managers. Still approximate, but a tighter, more defensible number than "10% of all employed people."

Layer in frequency and price to reach a usable estimate

How often do business executives/managers in Germany use limousine services? What is the average price for a ride like this?

Frequency data may simply be unavailable ("I don't have access to this data" — which is a better answer than a fabricated one). Pricing came back usable: ~€60–200/hour, or fixed rates of ~€150–500 depending on distance/duration.

Putting it together Segment size × frequency × price ≈ a rough TAM contribution from that one segment (business professionals). Repeat for tourists, event attendees, and luxury transport, then sum the segments and apply your own realistic-capture percentage.
Why this is still worth doing Where ChatGPT does have the underlying data, it can surface it far faster than manually reading dozens of scattered web articles — the value is speed of research, not certainty of the final number.

18Profitability & Scalability

Lecture 18

Two cost buckets, every business

BucketTraditional example (restaurant)
Running costs (ongoing)Rent, salaries, utilities, taxes, marketing, ingredients, permits/licenses
Upfront costs (one-time, before launch)Kitchen equipment, renovation/decor

Profitability = revenue − running costs. Break-even = how long it takes for accumulated profit to pay back the upfront investment.

Why a classic "business plan" breaks down for innovative businesses For a restaurant, both cost buckets and expected revenue are well-documented and predictable. For a novel startup, you usually don't know what customers will pay, or how much marketing spend it will take to convince people to try something they've never heard of. Marketing is often the biggest running cost and product development the biggest upfront cost for tech startups — commonly financed by venture capital across a much longer, far less certain timeline than a traditional business.

Scalability — the concept that decides whether VCs bet on you

Definition Scalability = the ability to grow revenue exponentially while not growing costs at the same rate.
Business typeWhy scalability is low/high
Single restaurant To make more money you need more space — a second restaurant means duplicating upfront cost and staff, plus new management overhead. Hiring help to manage it eats into margin. LOW
Franchise (e.g. McDonald's) Franchisees fund and operate each location; the franchisor collects commission with minimal added operating burden per unit. HIGH
Software / tech startup The same software can serve thousands or millions of users with little added human/financial cost per additional user. HIGH
Agency (marketing, real estate, etc.) You're reselling people's time/skills for a margin — the only way to grow is to hire more people, which scales cost roughly linearly with revenue. Heavy to manage, fragile in downturns. LOW
Scalability isn't all-or-nothing Even traditional businesses can improve scalability through automation, standardization, and outsourcing — this goes deeper than this course covers (see the separate Business Model Innovation course referenced as a bonus).

Two takeaways to keep

  1. The business must be profitable — running costs below revenue.
  2. Ideally the business has some scalability — not necessarily "billion-dollar tech unicorn" level, but ask yourself: once running, how do I grow revenue without growing costs in the same proportion?

Fixed vs. variable running costs are covered in the next section — both still count as "running costs," separate from upfront investment.

19Estimate Running and Upfront Costs using ChatGPT

Lecture 19

Start broad, as a sanity checklist

What are the running costs of a restaurant business? What are the upfront costs of a restaurant business?

Good for making sure you haven't forgotten a cost category — the answers align with the theory from the previous lecture.

Push for specific numbers

Estimate these costs for a typical 40-seat restaurant in New York.

Even with a broad prompt (no borough, no cuisine type specified), it returned concrete monthly running-cost figures and an upfront-cost range of $111,000–$228,000.

Useful, not verified Treat any such figure as an unverified starting point for your own research, not a fact to plan a business around.

The one type of question to avoid: opinions

How scalable is this business?
ChatGPT's positivity bias Language models tend to respond encouragingly almost regardless of the actual merits of an idea — "with the right strategy, location, and execution, this can definitely work." In the lecture, even after correctly noting that restaurants are harder to scale than other business types, it still ended on an upbeat "it is possible to scale and achieve long-term success" — undermining its own accurate observation.
Rule of thumb Never ask ChatGPT to validate or judge your business idea directly (e.g., "does this make sense?", "how scalable is this?"). Use it to gather facts, checklists, and structured research — but make the judgment calls yourself.

🧭 Section Cheat Sheet — Product-Market Fit

QuestionHow to answer it
Is there competition?None → suspect no market. Some, early-stage → good timing. Some, dominated by a unicorn → risky. Overcrowded → need real innovation to justify entry.
How big is the market?TAM (top-down: global → country → segment) × your realistic capture %. For innovative products, check if you're also pulling from adjacent markets (Uber effect). For brand-new markets, use proxies + real-world testing.
Will it be profitable?Revenue − running costs > 0, and you can afford + recoup the upfront costs (break-even).
Can it scale?Can revenue grow much faster than costs? (Franchise/software = yes. Agency/single-location = no, by default.)
The rule that carries over from Section 1 ChatGPT is excellent at fast research, checklists, and structured brainstorming — startup lists, substitute products, TAM calculation steps, cost breakdowns. It is unreliable for judgment calls — anything resembling "is my idea good?" gets a positivity-biased answer. Keep those two uses separate.
