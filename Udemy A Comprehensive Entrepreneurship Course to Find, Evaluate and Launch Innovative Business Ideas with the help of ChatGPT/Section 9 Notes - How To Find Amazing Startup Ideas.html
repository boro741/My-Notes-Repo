🎬 Lecture 77

Introduction – The Entrepreneurial Mindset

💡 Core Idea: Most great startup ideas start with spotting a problem. Problems and opportunities are two sides of the same coin — it all depends on how you look at it.

Entrepreneurship is like a roller coaster. It has ups and downs, fear and excitement. But the real question every aspiring founder asks is: Where do I start? How do I find a business idea with real potential?

This course is for you if you:

  • Struggle to come up with ideas at all
  • Found an idea but got discouraged because the competition is huge
  • Have lots of ideas but don't know which one to pick
🔍 The Common Pattern

How famous startups got their ideas

Some founders were in financial trouble. Others were passionate about changing an industry. Others had a strong vision of the future. But most started in the same place — a frustrating, everyday problem.

Common everyday problems that became billion-dollar businesses:

  • Can't find a parking spot → parking app startups
  • Stuck in traffic → navigation & ride-sharing apps
  • Waiting on hold with customer service → chat-based support tools
👓
Put on Your "Entrepreneurial Glasses" Every time you face a problem, ask: "How can I solve this? Can technology help? Can I build a business from it?" This simple habit will train your mind to see opportunities everywhere.

Ways 1, 2 & 3 – Expertise, Observation & Trends

💡 There are 7 proven ways to find great startup ideas. This lecture covers the first three.

1 Use Your Expertise — Be a "Nerd"

Being really good at something — even as a hobby — is a huge advantage. Side projects built to solve personal problems often become great businesses.

📌 Example

Gmail

Started as a side project to build a better email search tool. The creator was an expert who noticed something that could be improved — and built it.

📌 Example

Hotmail

Two Apple employees (Jack Smith & Sabeer Bhatia) wanted to discuss private business ideas but their work firewall blocked personal email. So they built a web-based email service that worked from anywhere, not linked to any ISP. That was Hotmail.

📌 Example

Oculus Rift

Palmer Luckey was a 15-year-old teenager obsessed with VR headsets. He collected many, tested all of them, and found every flaw: low resolution, heavy, too expensive. He built his own improved prototype. You don't need to be a professional — passion + deep knowledge is enough.

"Be nice to nerds. Chances are you'll end up working for one." — Bill Gates

2 Observe People Around You

You don't have to be an expert. Just pay attention to how people behave and what frustrates them in everyday life.

📌 Example

Slack

Founders watched how chaotic and distracting office communication had become — people buried in messages across many apps. Slack's entire pitch was one simple idea: Be less busy.

📌 Example

Tinder

Founder Sean Rad noticed how stressful it was to approach someone you like, especially when you're shy. His insight: what if you could only talk to someone if both of you agreed first? That mutual opt-in became Tinder (2012).

📌 Example

Airbnb

Two design graduates (not tech experts!) couldn't pay rent in San Francisco. During a design conference with no hotel rooms available, they rented air mattresses in their apartment for $80/night via a basic website. Three people showed up. That simple moment became a multi-billion dollar company.

🧠
Key Insight Many people rented rooms. Many people saw the same problem. Only those with an entrepreneurial mindset realised: "This isn't just my problem — it's everyone's. I can build a global business from this."

3 Monitor Future Trends

Don't start a business in a dying industry. Look at where the world is heading and position yourself there early.

Big global trends to watch:

  • Increasing urbanisation of cities
  • Growing population & ageing demographics
  • Climate change and the shift away from fossil fuels
  • AI, robotics, drones, 3D printing, Virtual Reality
📌 Example

Tesla

Elon Musk spotted the mega trend: the world must move away from fossil fuels. He built powerful electric sports cars to lead that change. Tesla didn't just sell a car — it rode a global wave.

📊
Useful Tool: Gartner's Hype Cycle Published every year — shows which technologies are emerging and how mature they are. Use it to find growing tech areas with real business potential (e.g. drones, robotics, VR, 3D printing).

Ways 4, 5, 6 & 7 – Inefficiencies, Middlemen, Copycats & Passion

4 Spot Inefficiencies in Old Industries

Old, established industries often have terrible customer service and outdated practices because there is not enough competition to push them to improve. When a new, better solution comes in — it wins fast.

📌 Example

Cable TV → Netflix

In the US, cable companies like Comcast were legally protected — one company per city. No competition meant no reason to improve. Customers had no choice. Then Netflix arrived and people "cut the cord." Cable companies lost millions of viewers almost overnight.

📌 Example

Taxis → Uber

Taxi licenses were limited, creating local monopolies. With no reason to improve, the experience stayed bad for decades. Uber came in and fixed every single pain point at once:

Old Taxi Problem Uber's Solution
Wait hours in a queue at the airport Driver notifies you when they arrive
Worried driver is taking a longer route Track your ride live on your phone
Pay cash, price is a surprise Confirm the price in advance, pay digitally
No way to hold a bad driver accountable Rate your driver after every ride
📐
Rule of Thumb: Improve and Simplify Find industries with monopolies, bad customer service, or frustrating processes. Then make people's lives easier. The formula: Identify friction → Remove it → Build a business around the relief.

5 Cut Out the Middlemen

Some industries become so stuffed with unnecessary layers that the customer ends up paying far more than needed. Technology can bypass those layers and connect buyers directly to what they need.

📌 Example

Travel Agencies → Expedia

Before: you visited a travel agent who booked everything for you and charged a fee. Now: Expedia lets you book flights, hotels, cars and more yourself online. It became the world's largest online travel company.

📌 Example

Banks → TransferWise (now Wise)

Banks charged very high fees on international money transfers. TransferWise (founded by Skype's founders) built a peer-to-peer system that offered much cheaper rates. Their viral campaign exposed banks' hidden fees. Today, Wise handles billions in transfers with far lower costs.

🔎
Where to look? Real estate is still packed with middlemen — agents, brokers, title companies, lawyers. Any industry where you pay multiple people just to complete a simple transaction is ripe for disruption.

6 Copy & Improve (The Copycat Method)

Copying a successful idea and executing it better — or in a new market — is completely normal in business. Truly original ideas are rare. Most successful companies built on existing concepts done smarter.

📌 Example

Rocket Internet

Rocket Internet built its entire business by copying successful startups and launching them in new markets faster than the originals could expand. They copied eBay, Airbnb, Groupon and more. When Groupon launched in the US, Rocket launched "CityDeal" in Germany. It grew so fast that Groupon had to buy it out to stay on top.

"Success is 1% inspiration and 99% perspiration." — Thomas Edison (quoted by Rocket Internet's founder)

You can also copy the format or mechanic of a product and apply it to a totally new space:

📌 Pattern Copying

The "Tinder for X" & "Uber for X" Pattern

Once Tinder succeeded → apps appeared for pets, shopping, recipes — all using the left/right swipe mechanic.

Once Uber succeeded → services appeared for helicopters, groceries, dry cleaning — all using the one-button order mechanic.

Once a concept is proven in one industry, test it in others.

7 Follow Your Passion

Building a startup is hard. There will be rejection, setbacks and self-doubt. Without genuine care for what you're building, you won't have the energy to keep going through the tough moments.

📌 Example

Pinterest

Ben Silbermann loved collecting things as a kid — stamps, insects, books. He believed that what you collect says a lot about who you are. So he and his childhood friend Evan Sharp brought the collecting experience online and created Pinterest to connect people with shared interests.

📌 Example

The Honest Company (Jessica Alba)

Alba spent much of her childhood in hospital. As a mother, she had an allergic reaction to baby products and worried about her baby's safety. After trying to change things through lobbying and failing, she started a company that sells only safe, non-toxic products for babies. Her personal passion became a billion-dollar business.

Final Thoughts

🔑 Key Takeaways from Lectures 78 & 79

  • Stop avoiding problems — start embracing them. Every frustration is a potential business.
  • Write down every idea you come up with. Build on them over time.
  • You don't need to be an industry expert to disrupt it (Airbnb founders knew nothing about real estate; Spotify came from people outside the music industry).
  • No one is 100% qualified to judge a new idea. Only testing will tell. Most investors rejected Airbnb early on.
  • Ideas rarely come out perfect. Expect to pivot (change direction) multiple times — that is completely normal.
  • Join your local startup community. Go to startup weekends. Meet potential co-founders and brainstorm together.
"Live in the future, then build what's missing." — Paul Graham

Use ChatGPT to Name Your Business

💡 Naming a business is harder than it looks — most good names are already taken. ChatGPT can quickly generate many unique name ideas, including ones with available .com domains.

A good name needs to be unique, memorable, and have an available domain. This is actually quite hard to do manually. ChatGPT makes the process much faster.

Find 10 unique names for a startup which creates an AI assistant for elderly people

You can ask for 20, 50, or as many as you need. It costs nothing and takes seconds.

Find 10 unique names for a startup which creates an AI assistant for elderly people and which also have available domain name extensions with .com
⚠️
Important: ChatGPT's data has a cutoff date. A small number of the suggested domain names might already be taken. Always verify domain availability on a domain registrar (like Namecheap or GoDaddy) before finalising your decision.

Even with that caveat, this is a much faster starting point than brainstorming names manually for hours.

Best ChatGPT Prompts to Find Business Ideas

💡 ChatGPT is a powerful brainstorming partner. The goal is NOT to get one perfect answer — it is to generate many ideas quickly, then filter them with your own judgement.

📌 Prompt 1 – Growing Markets & Trends

What are the current growing markets and trends to start a business in?

Example results: e-commerce, sustainability, healthcare, AI, personalisation, digital marketing, education technology, cybersecurity, remote work, mental health. A great starting point to identify broad directions.

📌 Prompt 2 – Future-Focused Thinking

What will be an important business in 2026? [or 2035, or 2050]

Try different years and compare. 2035 results included: clean energy, smart cities, autonomous vehicles, healthcare tech, AI, VR/AR, cybersecurity, space technology, biotechnology, circular economy. 2050 added space colonisation, advanced robotics, etc.

💡
Pro tip: The difference between results for 2026 vs. 2050 shows you which industries are just emerging vs. which are long-term bets. Use this to choose your time horizon.

📌 Prompt 3 – Industries Ready for Disruption

Which market is ready for disruption right now?

Results: transportation (EVs, scooters, bikes), education (online learning, bootcamps), real estate (tech-enabled buying/selling), construction (3D printing, sustainable materials).

📌 Prompt 4 – Finding Middlemen (Supports Way #5)

In which businesses are there too many middlemen?

Real estate example: agents, brokers, title companies add high fees and slow closing times. Use this prompt to identify industries where layers can be removed with technology.

📌 Prompt 5 – Customer Pain Points in Your Industry

What are the most common customer problems in the real estate industry?

Results: lack of transparency, high costs, complicated process, slow timelines, not enough property information, poor communication from agents, difficulty finding the right property.

Replace "real estate" with any industry you are interested in. You can also specify: "list the 10 most common" to control how many you get.

📌 Prompt 6 – Product-Level Problems

What are the most common customer problems and complaints with e-bikes?

Replace "e-bikes" with any product you want to improve or disrupt. A great way to find specific features or gaps to build around.

📌 Prompt 7 – Societal Problems

List 10 current unresolved problems in society.

Results: climate change, inequality, political polarisation, discrimination, education access, healthcare access, mental health, cybersecurity, ageing population, migration. Big problems = big opportunities for mission-driven startups.

📌 Prompt 8 – Low Barrier Entry Ideas

List 20 innovative businesses with low barriers to entry.

Tends to return freelance/service-type businesses. Good for finding quick-start options that don't need much upfront investment.

⚠️
Always use your own judgement. ChatGPT can suggest outdated or even bad ideas. For example, it suggested "graphic design" as a low-barrier business — but with AI image tools on the rise, that market is shrinking. Always think critically about what it gives you.

🔑 How to Use ChatGPT for Idea Generation

  • Use it to generate many ideas fast — not to find the one perfect answer.
  • Start broad, then refine with more specific follow-up prompts.
  • Use it to research deeper into ideas you already have in mind.
  • Always independently verify important facts, domain names and market data.

Get More Inspiration for Great Startup Ideas

💡 You now have a complete framework. Keep feeding your mind with new ideas and real-world examples to stay sharp and inspired.

The instructors recommend watching the "Business Disruptors" YouTube channel to see how famous companies differentiate themselves. New videos are published regularly — useful for continuous learning.

Business models to study as extra inspiration:

  • Nvidia — Could it become the most valuable company in the world?
  • TikTok — What makes it so popular? (Full business model breakdown)
  • CloudKitchens — The ghost kitchen / dark kitchen model
  • Unity — What makes this gaming engine so valuable?
  • SpaceX — Disrupting the space industry
  • Square — Payment technology explained
  • Airbnb — What makes it so successful?
  • Netflix — Business model strategy
🚀
Action Step For each company you study, ask: What problem did they solve? How did they remove friction? What trend did they ride? What made customers choose them over the old way? This analysis sharpens your ability to spot the next big opportunity.

🧠 The 7 Questions to Ask Yourself Every Day

  • What am I really good at? What do I know more about than most people?
  • What frustrated me or the people around me today?
  • What big trend is growing that I could build around?
  • Which industry has terrible customer service or no real competition?
  • Where are there too many unnecessary middlemen adding cost and complexity?
  • Is there a business model I admire that I could apply to a different industry?
  • What am I so passionate about that I'd work on it even without being paid?
