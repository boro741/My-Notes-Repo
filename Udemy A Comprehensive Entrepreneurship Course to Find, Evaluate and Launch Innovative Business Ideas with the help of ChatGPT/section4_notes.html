Lecture 27

What does it mean to actually "Start a Business"?

First-time founders often get their priorities wrong. They get one idea, feel excited about it, and jump straight into "official-looking" work: renting an office, printing business cards, designing a logo, registering a company, hiring people.

Key idea This is called vanity work — it feels productive and serious, but it does nothing to prove that customers actually want the product. All of this happens before getting a single real client.

This heavy approach can work for very traditional businesses (a market that's already proven, like opening a regular shop). But those businesses usually have small profit margins and lots of competition.

The lean approach (for innovative ideas)

  • Spend as little money as possible
  • Do only the minimum legal/admin work required
  • Only do the work that helps you get your first client or user

Some entrepreneurs have generated pre-sales just from a simple proof-of-concept video — before the product even existed. That's an extreme case, but it shows the core principle: test the demand first, build the structure later.

Why content creation matters

To test demand, you need either a marketing budget or an audience that already listens to you. Building an audience (YouTube, TikTok, Instagram) around topics related to your future business is the leanest way to validate interest — and later, sell to that same audience.

Example A fitness influencer sells fitness products online and runs a pizza restaurant, because many of her followers are locals. Her existing audience lets her test almost any new idea before fully building it.

Building an audience is hard work, but it's becoming one of the cheapest and most reliable paths to business success. Even on Kickstarter, the best crowdfunding campaigns often start with an existing audience.

Remember Stay flexible. For innovative ideas, be ready to pivot toward whatever turns out to actually be profitable — don't fall in love with your first idea.

Lecture 28

How to Test Your Idea in Practice — Introduction

Earlier in the course, you mapped your assumptions on the Lean Canvas: who your customers are, what they care about, and whether they'd actually pay. Now it's time to find real answers.

Key idea Getting people interested is good. Getting people to actually pay is the real proof.

The 4 ways to validate an idea (in order)

StepMethodEffort level
1Landing PageVery low
2Demo VideoLow–medium
3PrototypeMedium
4Minimum Viable Product (MVP)Highest

This order goes from a simple showcase to an actual working version of the product — this is the same order most startups follow. You don't need to code or be a designer to do any of these; they are basic, learnable skills every entrepreneur should master.

Before you start, ask yourself What exactly do I want to validate? Common goals: Do people like this? Will they pay? Is the interface easy to use? Are these the right marketing channels? Is my pricing right?

Lecture 29

Build a Landing Page

A landing page is just a single-page website (you only scroll — no separate pages). Its one job is to convert a visitor into an action.

Two common types of conversion

  • Newsletter signup (lead generation): collect email addresses to measure interest by how many people sign up.
  • Pre-buy: a bolder move — get visitors so excited they pay before the product even launches.

Keep the page extremely simple so the visitor is guided straight to the one action you want: subscribe or pay.

Example — Buffer Buffer built a simple landing page. When visitors clicked "Pricing," they saw: "You caught us before we're ready — leave your email and we'll let you know." This tested interest and generated leads before launch. Later, they showed different pricing plans and tracked which one people clicked most — this told them the right price to charge.

Landing page best practices

  • Strong header image — good quality, avoid generic/overused stock photos
  • Short, clear copy — explain the product and your unique value in a few words
  • Credibility boosters — awards, press mentions, testimonials, partnerships
  • Lots of white space — keep it clean, not cluttered
  • Clear call-to-action buttons — repeat them as you scroll, make them big
Small detail, big impact Orange/red buttons tend to convert better than green/blue ones — a red button can increase conversions by up to 30% compared to a green one.

You don't need coding skills — free tools exist that let you build a landing page in under 10 minutes (if materials are ready) or a couple of hours if starting from scratch.

Recap A landing page is fast, cheap, and tells you whether people like your idea — plus gives early pricing signals. Pair it with a social media page to track interest even further.

Use ChatGPT to Write the Copy of Your Landing Page

You can ask ChatGPT to write your landing page copy for you. The approach shown in the lecture:

  1. Briefly describe your startup (example used: "an AI assistant for elderly people")
  2. Ask it to write the headline, sub-headline, main paragraph, and any other ideas
Example output

Headline: "Introducing [Product Name] — Your Personal Assistant for Elderly Care"
Sub-headline: "Empowering the Elderly to Live Independently with Technology"

ChatGPT also suggested: highlight key features (medication reminders, emergency response), share success stories for social proof, offer a free trial/demo, and emphasize affordability.

You can keep refining by asking it to rewrite with different tone, specific words to use/avoid, or a different style.

Watch out When asked to remove "technology jargon," ChatGPT still used terms like "machine learning" and "natural language processing" — it didn't fully follow the instruction. It did add SEO keywords correctly though. Lesson: always review AI output critically — don't assume it followed every instruction perfectly.
Pro tip Feed ChatGPT the customer segments you identified earlier in the course (e.g. elderly care homes, healthcare professionals). It can tailor the copy's language specifically for each target audience.

Lecture 31

Create a Demo Video

A demo video shows your product in action. It's powerful for crowdfunding campaigns and can generate huge attention — sometimes millions of views and dollars.

Example — Dropbox Founder Drew Houston couldn't get a working version ready, so he made a video showing how Dropbox would work. It went viral — subscribers jumped from 5,000 to 70,000 overnight.
Example — Myo (arm sensor) A demo video of a wearable sensor that lets you control devices with hand movement led to 10,000 units pre-sold in 2 days — several million dollars in sales before a single unit was shipped.
Example — Exploding Kittens A card game that asked for $10,000 on Kickstarter raised that amount in 8 minutes, and over $8 million within a month.
Key idea No matter how unusual your idea sounds — if people pay for it, that's real validation.

You likely already have the equipment needed to make a basic video (phone camera, free editing software). It takes more time and planning than a landing page, but is worth it. Embed it on your landing page, post it to YouTube and social media.

Lecture 32

Use ChatGPT to Write the Script of Your Demo Video

Ask ChatGPT something simple like: "How do I create a demo video for this product?" — it will list general steps: write a script, record a voiceover, create a storyboard, etc.

Then ask it to actually execute step one and write the script. In the lecture's example, ChatGPT produced a full script with shot descriptions and narrator lines (e.g. opening on an elderly person at home, narrator talking about how technology can feel confusing as we age).

Watch out AI-generated scripts can feel cheesy or like generic TV ads. You can guide it with specifics: number of people in the scene, their gender, the setting, how long the video should be (in minutes or pages).
Recap ChatGPT won't create a perfect script on the first try, but it's a great tool to get unstuck and generate a solid starting draft you can refine.

Lecture 33

Create a Prototype

A prototype lets people see and interact with the "look and feel" of your product instead of just hearing you describe it. Designers use this to explore ideas and get detailed feedback.

Type 1 — Paper prototyping

The simplest option: just pencil, paper, and imagination.

  1. Pick the main features you want to test (e.g. the home page of a Trello-like app)
  2. Draw each screen on paper
  3. Show users the "home page" drawing, ask them to point at a button
  4. Show them the next drawing that would appear after that click
  5. Observe: do they understand the flow, or get confused?
  6. Improve your drawings based on their feedback

Type 2 — Online prototyping

More professional, better simulates real interactions. Two tools mentioned:

ToolFree plan
EnvisionOne free project
InVision (Product IO)15-day free trial

These tools are beginner-friendly — no technical skill needed. Some let you embed the prototype directly into your landing page or demo video.

Recommended path Start with paper prototyping — it's free and fastest for complex ideas. Then move to an online prototype for a higher-fidelity version you can share with real visitors and test the actual user experience.

Lecture 35

Build a Minimum Viable Product (MVP)

The MVP is the last and most powerful validation method. It's a working version of your product — stripped to its most basic features — that real users can actually use, not just look at.

Key distinction A demo video is a simulation — people can only watch it. An MVP is functional — people can actually use it, give feedback, and often pay for it. (Note: the famous Dropbox video was not an MVP — it was just a demo video, even though many people mistakenly call it one.)

Real MVP examples

Zappos Nobody believed people would buy shoes online without trying them on. The founders took photos of shoes at local stores, listed them on a basic website. When an order came in, then they bought the shoes from the store and shipped them. No inventory, no warehouse — just a test of real demand.
Food on the Table Founder Manuel Rosso personally approached shoppers in grocery stores and offered a $10 concierge service: he'd track sales, buy discounted food, deliver it, and suggest recipes — all before building any app or store partnerships. Many refused, some accepted — enough to prove the idea. The company was later sold to Food Network.
Groupon First sale: half-price pizza from a restaurant in the same building as their office.
Airbnb Sold 3 nights on air mattresses in the founders' own apartment for $80 each, using a very basic website. The first real feature was simply "check in at different places" — everything else came later.
"If you're not embarrassed by the first version of your product, you've launched too late." — Reid Hoffman, founder of LinkedIn
Why this matters Skipping this validation step risks wasting time and money building something nobody wants. In the worst case, an MVP helps you fail fast and pivot to something better, instead of failing slowly and expensively.

MVPs generally need some technical skill (unlike a landing page or video), except for very complex ideas where a demo video might be a better substitute for now.

Lecture 37

What Resources Do You Have at Your Disposal?

Before moving forward, be honest about your real capabilities: What can you actually do? What skills does this idea require? How much money do you have?

Your options depend on your situation

Your situationWhat you can do
Solo founder, no technical skillsStart with content creation — build your own audience first
Need a technical solutionPartner with a co-founder who can build it
Have fundingOutsource development (riskiest — pure financial loss if it fails)
Working inside a companyUsually more resources: internal team, known budget, known skills
Reality check Looking for "a technical co-founder to build my idea" is a running joke in startup circles — it usually means one person expects to do all the heavy lifting. If you're not technical, you must bring something equally valuable to the table (e.g. sales ability, an audience, deep market knowledge, funding).

Generally, the best entrepreneurs are either technically skilled in what they're building, or excellent at managing and convincing people to join them.

Don't aim for perfection Perfectionism slows startups down, especially early on. The real goal: get a paying client for a minimally working solution. If that works, you've proven the business model — then you improve it and raise your prices.

✅ Section 4 — Full Recap

There are 4 ways to validate a business idea, from simplest to most advanced:

  1. Landing Page — a one-page website to test interest and collect emails or pre-sales
  2. Demo Video — show the product in action to build excitement and pre-sell
  3. Prototype — a clickable/paper mockup so users can experience the interface
  4. Minimum Viable Product (MVP) — a real, working (if basic) version people can actually use and pay for

Don't be discouraged by early negative feedback — this whole process is a learning loop. Every test teaches you what users actually want, which helps you build a better product.

ChatGPT can support you at every single step: writing landing page copy, scripting your demo video, brainstorming prototype ideas, and outlining your MVP plan. It won't do the work perfectly on its own — always review its output critically — but it removes the "blank page" problem and speeds up your thinking.
