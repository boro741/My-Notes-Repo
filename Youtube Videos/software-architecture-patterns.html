Big idea behind all of them

Before we dive in, here's the one idea that ties every pattern in this piece together: a good architecture keeps the parts of your app that change a lot separate from the parts that barely change at all. Everything else — layers, onions, hexagons, plugins — is just a different way of drawing that line. Once you see that, all nine patterns start to feel like variations on a theme instead of nine unrelated things to memorize.

Chapter One

Layered architecture — organizing your app like a cake

This is the pattern almost everyone meets first — you might also hear it called n-tier architecture. The idea is refreshingly simple: organize your app into logical layers, where each layer has one clear job. Think of it like slicing a system into bite-sized chunks, each one doing a single thing well.

💡 quick definition

"Layer" here just means a group of code that's responsible for one kind of work — like a department in a company. The sales department doesn't do accounting, and the accounting department doesn't do sales. Each layer only talks to the layer right next to it.

Most apps you'll build use three main layers:

The Three Classic Layers
🖥️
Presentation LayerYour user interface or APIs — what the user actually sees and clicks.
⚙️
Business Logic LayerHandles the core rules and decisions your app makes.
🗄️
Data Access LayerManages talking to your database — reading and saving information.

Each layer only knows about the one directly below it. Change one, and the others stay untouched.

💡 what's "business logic"?

It's just the rulebook your app follows to make decisions — things like "apply a 10% discount if the cart is over ₹2,000" or "reject the order if the item is out of stock." It's the actual thinking your app does, separate from how it looks or where it stores data.

Let's make this concrete. Say you're building an e-commerce site. The presentation layer is responsible for displaying the product pages you scroll through. The business logic layer takes care of things like calculating discounts or processing an order. And the data access layer? That's your connection to the product and inventory database — the part that actually fetches "is this in stock?"

This pattern is fantastic for large, enterprise-style systems. It keeps things modular, and if you need to change something in one layer — say, redesign the checkout page — the other layers stay completely intact. It also makes life easier when different teams own different layers: the frontend team lives in the presentation layer, the backend team lives in business logic, and the database team owns data access, all without stepping on each other's toes.

That said, it's not perfect for everything. As your system grows more complex, a strict layer-by-layer setup can start to feel rigid — sometimes a request has to pass through every single layer just to do something simple, which adds unnecessary overhead.

Chapter Two

Onion architecture — protecting your core logic

Picture an actual onion: multiple layers wrapped around a core, right in the center. That's the whole metaphor. The idea is to keep your business logic isolated at the very center, protected by outer layers like infrastructure and APIs.

The golden rule of onion architecture: everything depends on the core, but the core depends on nothing outside of it. The center doesn't know or care that a database exists, or that there's a website calling it. It just does its job.

Onion Architecture
CORE
Core (center)Pure business logic — e.g. transaction processing, account management. Zero outside dependencies.
Middle layerRules about how the core gets used — application-specific logic.
Outer layerAPIs, databases, and the website or app that people actually interact with.

Imagine you're building a banking app. At the very center of the onion, you have transaction processing and account management — the pure business logic. The outer layers handle everything else: how that logic gets exposed through APIs, how data gets saved to a database, or how it's presented on a website.

The major advantage here is that your core logic becomes easy to test, extend, and refactor. You can swap out an entire database — say, move from one provider to another — without touching a single line of your core business logic. This pattern shines when your application needs to keep evolving and changing over time, but you want the heart of it to stay rock-solid and stable.

🧅 Why the "no outward dependency" rule matters

If your core logic secretly depends on, say, a specific database library, then upgrading or replacing that library forces you to touch your most important code — the part you least want to break. Onion architecture makes that impossible by design.

Chapter Three

Hexagonal architecture — swappable parts, via ports & adapters

Hexagonal architecture is a close cousin of onion architecture, but it takes the idea one step further. Your core business logic shouldn't directly depend on external systems — like a database or an outside API — at all.

Instead, the core talks to the outside world through ports (interfaces — basically a contract saying "here's what I need") and adapters (the actual implementations that plug into those ports and handle real communication).

💡 ports & adapters, in plain english

A port is like a wall socket — it defines the shape of the plug, nothing more. An adapter is the actual appliance you plug in — a lamp today, a phone charger tomorrow. As long as the plug shape (the port) stays the same, you can swap what's plugged in without rewiring the wall.

Say you're building a payment system. The core handles the actual payment logic — validating card numbers, calculating totals, and so on. But it interacts with external payment providers like Stripe or PayPal through a port. So if you ever need to swap Stripe for PayPal, you just plug in a different adapter — the core logic never even notices the change.

Payment System, Hexagonal Style
💳 Core: validate card, calculate total
↓   via ports (interfaces)   ↓
🅿️
Stripe adapterHandles real API calls
💰
PayPal adapterSwap in anytime
🗄️
Database adapterSaves the receipt
📧
Email adapterSends confirmation

Swap any adapter without touching the core in the middle.

Hexagonal architecture is ideal for applications that plug into multiple external systems, or need to evolve over time. The separation guarantees your core business logic stays pure and testable, while every external dependency gets swapped in and out cleanly, one adapter at a time.

Chapter Four

Modular architecture — self-contained building blocks

Here the idea is to break your application into self-contained modules. Each module owns one specific slice of your app's functionality, and modules talk to each other only through well-defined interfaces — never by reaching into each other's internals.

💡 what's an "interface" here?

An interface is simply an agreed set of rules for how two pieces of code talk to each other — like a restaurant menu. You don't need to know how the kitchen cooks the dish; you just need to know what you can order and what you'll get back.

Picture a Content Management System (CMS) — the kind of software that powers a blog or a company website. You might have separate modules for user management, content creation, and SEO optimization. Each module operates independently, but they can integrate whenever needed — for instance, the SEO module might reach into the content module to apply SEO rules to a blog post before it's published.

Inside a CMS
👤
User ManagementLogins, roles, permissions
✍️
Content CreationWriting & publishing posts
🔍
SEO OptimizationApplies rules to published posts

Each module can be built and shipped on its own — they only meet at agreed interfaces.

By keeping your code modular, you make it far easier to extend and maintain. Each module can be developed, tested, and deployed independently — which means different teams can work in parallel without blocking each other. And as your system grows, it stays manageable, because no single module ever becomes a tangled, overgrown mess on its own.

Chapter Five

Microkernel architecture — start small, add plugins

Also called plug-in architecture. This pattern starts with a minimal, bare-bones core system, and you add features by developing plugins or extensions on top of it. The core provides only the essentials; plugins handle the specialized stuff.

Think about a code editor like Eclipse or IntelliJ. The core system provides basic text editing and project management — nothing fancy. But if you want to develop in Java, you add a Java plugin. Want to connect to a database? Install a database plugin. Every feature gets added only when it's actually needed, without bloating the core.

A Code Editor's Microkernel
🧩 Core: text editing + project management
＋
☕
Java PluginAdds Java-specific tools
🗃️
Database PluginConnects to your DB
🐞
Debugger PluginStep through your code

Nobody is forced to install a plugin they don't need — the core stays light.

Microkernel architecture is great whenever you need extensibility — systems that grow over time, or whose features vary a lot between different users. IDEs and CMS platforms both benefit hugely from this: you start small, and only add functionality as it's actually needed, one plugin at a time.

Chapter Six

Event-driven architecture — reacting to what just happened

This pattern is built for systems that need to react in real time. Instead of one service directly calling another and waiting for a reply, services produce "events" whenever something important happens, and other services independently consume those events — asynchronously.

💡 "event" and "asynchronously", decoded

An event is just an announcement — "hey, this thing just happened." Asynchronously means the services reacting to it don't have to do it immediately, in lockstep, or in any guaranteed order — they each react in their own time, independently, like people reading a group announcement whenever they happen to check their phone.

Take an e-commerce system. When a customer places an order, an event fires: order_placed. Nobody had to tell the inventory service, the notification service, and the analytics service to run one after another — they each independently notice the event and react on their own schedule.

Example · One Order Event

A single "order placed" event quietly triggers three completely independent reactions, each handled by a different service, with none of them waiting on each other.

📦 StockInventory service updates count
📧 EmailNotification service confirms order
📊 SaleAnalytics service logs the sale

Each service works independently, simply by reacting to the same event.

Event-driven architecture is ideal for creating decoupled systems that scale easily — "decoupled" just means the services aren't tightly wired to each other, so any one of them can fail, get updated, or get replaced without breaking the rest. This is exactly how real-time giants like Uber and Netflix structure huge portions of their systems.

Chapter Seven

CQRS — splitting reading from writing

CQRS stands for Command Query Responsibility Segregation — a mouthful, but the idea is simple. It splits your application's write operations ("commands") from its read operations ("queries"), so each side can be optimized completely independently.

💡 command vs. query

A command changes something — "update this account," "place this order." A query just asks for information — "show me this balance." In most apps these get mixed together in the same code path. CQRS says: keep them apart on purpose.

In a banking app, the command side handles account updates and processes transactions. The query side handles showing you your transaction history or your account balance. Because these are split, you can tune each one separately — for example, making the read side lightning-fast for the millions of people just checking their balance, while keeping the write side extra careful and consistent for actual money movement.

CQRS in a Banking App

✏️ Command (Write)

Update account, process a transaction. Optimized to be safe and correct, even if it's a little slower.

👁️ Query (Read)

Show balance, show transaction history. Optimized to be fast, since it's requested constantly.

both sides ultimately stay in sync with the same underlying data

This separation lets you improve scalability by optimizing reads and writes on their own terms. CQRS is particularly useful when reads far outweigh writes — which is true for most apps, since people check things far more often than they change them — or when different parts of your system are fine with eventual consistency, meaning the read side might lag the write side by a moment, but it'll catch up.

Chapter Eight

Service-oriented architecture (SOA) — independent services, working together

In SOA, your application is made up of services that communicate over a network — usually using the SOAP or REST protocols. Each service represents one specific business function, like billing, inventory, or customer management.

💡 "protocol" and "SOAP / REST"

A protocol is just an agreed set of rules for how two computers talk to each other — like a shared language. SOAP and REST are two common "languages" services use to send requests and responses over the internet. REST is the far more common one today, and it's the same style your browser uses to load most websites.

Picture a large retail company. Its SOA setup might include separate services for inventory management, order processing, and billing. These services are loosely coupled — meaning they don't depend heavily on each other's internal details — and they interact over a shared service bus, a kind of central highway that routes messages between services.

A Retail Company's SOA
🚌 Service Bus (SOAP / REST)
↕
📦
InventoryTracks stock levels
🧾
Order ProcessingHandles new orders
💳
BillingCharges the customer

Each service can be scaled, updated, or replaced entirely on its own.

This allows each service to scale independently — if billing suddenly needs more computing power during a sale, you can scale just that one service. SOA is perfect for enterprise systems with many interdependent components, since each service is autonomous, which makes the overall system more flexible and easier to scale as a whole.

Chapter Nine

Clean architecture — logic that never has to change

Clean architecture was popularized by Robert C. Martin — better known in the developer world as "Uncle Bob." The goal here is to ensure your business logic is completely independent of external frameworks, the UI, and databases.

💡 what's a "framework"?

A framework is a pre-built toolkit that gives your app a head start — think React for building interfaces, or Django for building web servers. Frameworks are incredibly useful, but they also change and get replaced over time. Clean architecture makes sure your most important code doesn't get glued to whichever framework happens to be popular today.

Just like onion architecture, dependencies flow inward — everything points toward the core, and the core points to nothing. This keeps your most important logic completely isolated from the churn happening around it.

Clean Architecture
ENTITIES
Entities (core)The most fundamental business rules — e.g. "a task can be created, updated, or deleted."
Use casesThe specific application logic that uses those rules.
Interface adaptersTranslates between use cases and the outside world.
Frameworks & driversUI, databases, web frameworks — the most replaceable layer.

In a task management app, the core handles the fundamentals — creating, updating, or deleting a task. The external layers handle everything else: how the UI looks, or how data actually gets saved. So if you switch from a relational database to a NoSQL one, or even rebuild the whole thing as a mobile app instead of a website, your business logic doesn't have to change at all.

That's the real strength of clean architecture: your core functionality stays stable, no matter how much the external world evolves around it. It's perfect when you want a system that's easy to maintain and adapt over time — your core logic stays untouched, even as the UI or infrastructure changes underneath it.

The Recap

Nine patterns. One question to ask yourself.

Before picking a pattern, just ask: "which part of my app is likely to change, and which part do I want to protect from that change?" Your answer usually points straight at the right pattern.

These aren't just theory — they're practical tools you reach for once you know what problem each one solves. Most real systems actually mix a few of these together: an onion core, wrapped in a layered API, occasionally firing events. Once the individual ideas click, combining them starts to feel natural.
