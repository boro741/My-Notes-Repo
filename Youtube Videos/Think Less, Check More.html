Chapter One

The planes that came home

When people say "you're overthinking it," the speaker's answer is: "No, I'm thinking the normal amount. I'm just bad at it." Most of us are the same. But a few people see what everyone else misses. Here is the most famous one.

In World War II, the US military studied the bombers that made it back from missions. The bullet holes were mostly on the wings and the tail. The plan sounded simple: add armor to the wings and the tail.

Then a mathematician named Abraham Wald looked at the same planes and said: "No. Put the armor where there are no bullet holes." Why? Because planes hit in those spots never came back.

💡
Tip: what is a mathematician doing here? Wald wasn't a pilot or an engineer. He was good at spotting what numbers really mean. That is the skill in this story, and it is a skill you can copy.

Everyone looked at the exact same planes. So why was he the only one who saw it? Was he a genius, or was he doing something in his head that you can learn? And if you learn it, what will you start noticing in your own life?

Chapter Two

The trick: ask about what's missing

Wald wasn't staring at the bullet holes. He was thinking about the planes that weren't there.

Every plane on that airfield had made it home. So every hole in it was a hole a plane could survive. Wings and tail were full of holes because a plane can take a hit there and keep flying. The engines were almost clean, not because nobody hit them, but because planes hit in the engine never came back to be counted. The generals studied the planes in front of them. Wald asked about the planes that weren't.

🎯 The whole trick, in one question

"What am I not seeing, and why am I not seeing it?" That's it. You don't need a war to practice. You need three questions, and they work in a coffee shop.

💡
Tip: this has a name. It's called survivorship bias: we only study the things that survived, so we get a wrong picture. Think of "successful startup founders who dropped out of college." You never hear about the thousands who dropped out and failed.

Chapter Three

The coffee shop

Imagine you own a small coffee shop. One counter. A register at the front. One person at the back making every drink. Each morning the line reaches the door and customers complain about the wait.

You see a long line and one person at the register. The answer seems obvious: hire a second cashier. It works, sort of. People pay faster. Then they walk to the other end of the counter and wait there instead. The person at the machine was already going as fast as they could. Orders now arrive faster, but the coffee doesn't. You paid for a cashier, and customers just got a new place to stand.

👀 "Customers are waiting"Something you saw. A fact.
💭 "We need another cashier"Your guess about why. An explanation.

Those two felt like one thought. They are two. That is the mistake, and it happened before you hired anyone. Now the three questions.

Question 1: What do I actually know?

Before fixing anything, stand in the shop and watch. Where are people standing? Have they ordered? Paid? Are they holding a receipt, waiting for a cup? In this shop, most people waiting have already paid. They're waiting for the machine. Paying was never the slow part; making coffee is. We fix the most visible part of a problem and call it the whole problem. The register line was easy to see. The machine was behind the line, so nobody looked.

Question 2: What am I assuming?

Go to the person at the machine. Ask what slows them down in the rush. Watch them make 10 drinks. Say they tell you: the drinks are fine, but in the rush they read orders, steam milk and hand over cups all at once. One more pair of hands would let them just make coffee. Now you have a better guess. It's still a guess, and you already made this mistake once: a reasonable idea became a decision before anyone checked it.

Question 3: How can I check?

Say out loud what you expect: "With a second person making drinks, customers should get coffee sooner." That's a sentence you can test. Pick one busy morning. Put a second trained person at the machine. Change nothing else. Time what you actually care about: the whole wait, from joining the line to holding the coffee. Compare with a normal busy morning.

✅
Sooner? Keep the person on during rush
❌
Not sooner? Cheaper lesson than a salary
🔁
Wrong guess? Go back and watch again
💡
Tip: "checking" ≠ "solving everything". You don't need to solve the whole shop in your head. You need one thing you can check by Friday.

Chapter Four

Three questions on real life

You probably don't own a coffee shop. But these questions work on anything, especially the problems you keep thinking about in circles.

Example 1 · A test score of 58

Your guess: "I'm bad at this subject." What you know: which questions you lost. What you're assuming: that you lost them because you didn't understand the material. Look at the paper. Maybe you lost the last four because you ran out of time. Maybe you knew the method but misread the question. Each one needs a different fix. Check: pick one, change it for the next test, and see if the score moves.

Example 2 · The message with no reply

What you know: no reply for 2 days. Your guess: they're annoyed with you. What else could explain it? They're busy, they forgot, or they don't know what to say. Check: send one short follow-up asking for what you need. You don't have to decide how they feel before you do that.

Chapter Five

Same mistake, three sizes

At the airfield, the planes Wald could inspect had already survived. In the shop, the short register line hid the wait at the machine. An unanswered message tells you less about the other person than your first reaction does.

1
You see part of the evidenceHoles on landed planes, a long register line, 2 days of silence
2
You build an explanationArmor the wings, hire a cashier, "they're annoyed"
3
You act as if you had checkedMoney, time and feelings spent on a guess
Wald didn't think harder than the generals. He asked one question they skipped.

That's what "think less" means here: less time defending a guess, and one thing you can check.
