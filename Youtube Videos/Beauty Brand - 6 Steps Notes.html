0Intro — The ₹300 Cr Mistake

Tarun co-founded mCaffeine (body care) and Hyphen (face care), both now part of a group doing 1000+ crore in revenue. Building it took 10 years and ₹300 crore burnt — and he's brutally honest about why so much of that was avoidable.

Beauty & Personal Care (BPC) — why it's a hot category

  • New-age BPC brands (not around 5-10 years ago) already do ~₹14,000-15,000 cr in revenue.
  • Forecast to hit ~₹50,000 cr in the next 10 years — ~₹35,000 cr of pure incremental growth, much of it from brands not even launched yet.
  • Category has grown strongly (~historically ~25%/yr) and is still forecast to keep growing at a healthy double-digit clip.

The mistake: reversing the decision order

Before raising big money, Tarun's team was capital-efficient: ~₹15 cr in funding got them to a ₹100 cr business (6x capital efficiency), profitable, growing fast. Then in the 2021-22 funding boom, they raised ~₹250 cr in 18 months — and started making decisions in the wrong order: Capital first ("we raised money, now deploy it") → Competition ("match what they're doing") → Channel ("go online AND offline") → Consumer (an afterthought). Result: ~₹200 crore burnt in just 2 years, spread thin across too many products and channels.

Raising capital tempts you to decide top-down (Capital→Competition→Channel→Consumer). Bootstrapped founders default to the reverse — and it's the healthier order.
Key insight: Capital doesn't build a good brand. A good brand attracts capital — not the other way round. Data across DTC funding "waves" backs this up: the earliest, most heavily-funded wave (~$980M raised) has the fewest breakout brands; a later wave that raised roughly a third as much (~$320M) produced sharper, better-run brands. Less capital, forced discipline, better business.

Contribution Margin (CM1 / CM2) — the metric that keeps you honest

Every ₹100 you charge splits into: Gross Sales (MRP minus discounts/channel margins) → Net Revenue (minus tax) → minus COGS (raw material + manufacturing) = Gross Margin → minus fixed logistics/warehousing = CM1 → minus marketing spend = CM2. CM2 is what's actually left in your pocket per unit sold.

Same ₹50 net revenue, two outcomes. Spend ~30% on marketing → CM2 stays positive (₹15). Spend ~100% chasing growth → CM2 goes negative — you're paying people to use your product.
If CM2 is negative, no volume saves you. Tarun's line: at that point "you're literally paying people to take a bath." More units sold just means more losses — a business needs CM2 positive before it can scale profitably off fixed-cost break-even.

Finding your gap: the BPC Pyramid

Consumers don't buy every category on day one — they graduate over time. Face wash and moisturizer sit at the base (everyone uses them = biggest market, most competitive). Sunscreen and serums are the middle layer (exploded in the last 5-7 years as awareness grew). The top is where a new brand should actually enter: a genuine niche with almost no competition (e.g. body scrub for mCaffeine).

Enter through an uncontested niche at the top, earn trust, then let the customer graduate down into higher-frequency and complementary categories over 5-7 years.
  • Rule: never enter a household with a highly competitive category first — pick a niche door.
  • Once trust is built in the niche, the customer naturally graduates to a higher-frequency category (e.g. body wash), then a complementary one (e.g. lotion).
  • To spot the gap: look at what the most educated Indian consumer already uses from developed markets (Korea is the reference market Tarun name-checks) but that's still unaddressed locally.
  • Brand building is a 10-year game, not a quarterly sprint.
🥤 The "Innocent" smoothie test: Before quitting their jobs, the founders of Innocent Drinks set up a stall at a music festival with two dustbins — one labelled "yes, we should quit our jobs for this," one "no." People tasted the smoothie and threw the cup in whichever bin. The "yes" bin filled up — they quit their jobs the next day. Tarun's own version for a new pain-care idea: stand outside 2-3 gyms, hand out a cheap lab-made prototype (~₹600 to produce), and don't just measure "did they try it" — measure whether they used it up and wanted more. Trial without repeat means the product hasn't earned its "dopamine moment" yet.
Tarun's 6P framework — the "video game" rule: the weaker your score on an earlier step, the harder (and costlier) the later steps become.

1Proposition

The proposition is your promise — what you're selling and why it matters, in one line.

🧓 The grandmother test: Can you explain your product to your grandmother in 10 seconds, in plain language, with no jargon? If she doesn't get it — or worse, doesn't care — the proposition has failed before the product even ships.
  • Think of it as your "elevator pitch": if you met a customer in a lift, you'd only get a few seconds to land it.
  • If the proposition doesn't click, the customer never even gets to your product's quality.
  • Every later step (product, packaging, pricing) exists to deliver on this one promise — so get it razor-sharp first.
Tip: A brand is just "the promise of the value you're going to deliver." Keep delivering on it and the brand strengthens. Break it repeatedly and the brand gets diluted — or killed.

2Product

The product has to physically deliver whatever the proposition promised. The gap between what you promise as a brand and what the product actually is has to be as small as possible.

  • If you claim to be "the most innovative body care brand," you can't ship a generic soap bar — the mismatch will be obvious the moment someone opens the pack.
  • The shorter that distance between the brand story and the physical product, the sharper (and more trustworthy) the brand feels.
  • Example: mCaffeine's whole origin is built on caffeine's real, well-studied (Stanford, Imperial College, Exeter) skin benefit of pulling excess water from tissue — an old, proven ingredient nobody had built an India brand around.
Watch out: a great pitch backed by a mediocre product is the fastest way to burn trust — and once broken, that trust is very expensive to rebuild.

3Packaging

Packaging has two jobs: catch the eye and deliver the message. It's arguably your best brand investment — it's the one thing that sits in front of the customer for their entire shelf life or scroll time (8-30+ days), so it's worth obsessing over.

Three levers you can differentiate on:

  • Color — pick a palette nobody else in the category owns.
  • Design — the graphics, layout, typography.
  • Physicality — the actual shape/mold of the pack (e.g. a shampoo bottle shaped like a grenade stands out on a shelf full of identical white tubes).

You don't need to win on all three — but you need to win on at least one, or you end up as forgettable "me-too" packaging lost in a sea of lookalikes.

Tip: Physicality is the hardest lever (new molds cost money and time) but also the most defensible — it's the one competitors can't copy overnight.

4Pricing

Rule of thumb: don't price more than ~20% above your closest competitor. But at that price, the product must genuinely feel like it's worth even more — if you charge ₹120, it needs to feel like a ₹140 product.

Pricing is a bad moat. Someone can always undercut you — there's no permanent "unfair advantage" on cost. The real, durable edge is a superior product priced right, not the cheapest price in the category.

Work the price backwards into your cost structure, not forwards:

  1. Decide the sell price (e.g. ₹600, ~20% over a ₹500 competitor).
  2. Check what gross margin that leaves you (target: 65%+ gross margin in BPC).
  3. If your COGS eats more than ~35% of that price, something upstream is broken — formulation, sourcing, or vendor selection — not the pricing itself.

Two stress tests before you call something a "launch"

Stress testBar to clear
Gross margin≥ 65% (BPC benchmark) — below this, the business likely isn't worth building
CM2Must be positive at scale — a small CM2-negative trial (₹10-30 lakh) is fine as an R&D / market-development experiment, never as a real launch

5Platform

Your price and how much "education" the product needs decides which channel it should live on first.

The more a product needs explaining, the earlier in this order it should sit. Quick commerce always comes last — it rewards products that already work, not products still finding their story.
  • D2C — first choice when the product needs heavy education (new ingredient, new use-case). Only viable if AOV is ₹500+; below that D2C economics don't work.
  • Vertical commerce (Nykaa, Purplle, Myntra) — works once a category is a little more established (e.g. your 3rd serum launch, not your 1st).
  • Horizontal (Amazon, Flipkart) — best for well-understood, high-volume basics like soap or face wash.
  • Quick commerce — treat it as a discipline exercise, not a discovery channel. Limited shelf space means only your top, already-proven SKUs get in.

6Promotion

Promotion is paid marketing — and it's the step where all the sins of steps 1-5 come to collect their bill.

The core warning of the whole episode: the weaker your proposition, product, packaging, pricing, or platform fit, the more money you'll be forced to pour into promotion just to compensate. That's exactly how ₹200 crore got burnt in 2 years — chasing growth through ads while the fundamentals underneath weren't tight.
  • If a product is a genuine "me-too" with nothing sharp about it, the only way to move it is to sell it cheapest and throw the biggest promotional budget behind it — an expensive, low-margin game.
  • Whenever a product is burning unusually high marketing spend, audit the other five P's first — the real problem is almost always sitting upstream, not in the ad account.
  • At Tarun's company, the co-founder holds a deliberate veto over proposition, product, packaging, and pricing decisions — precisely so promotion spend doesn't have to bail out weak fundamentals.

Closing — The Overall Thesis

Product-market fit in BPC isn't "units sold" — it's category share. Own a genuine double-digit (10%+) share of the specific sub-category you compete in (e.g. toners, body lotion) before believing you've "made it." Large legacy beauty players typically defend 30-35% share in their core categories — that's the ceiling to aim toward, not 1-2% spread thin across ten categories.

Two signals to watch as you scale

  • Customer acquisition cost (CAC) should hold steady or improve even as the number of new customers you acquire grows — that's proof the proposition and pricing are genuinely working, not just being subsidized by spend.
  • Repeat rate should climb over consecutive months. On marketplaces where you can't see repeat data directly, watch the proxy: if your ad-spend-to-sales ratio on a platform keeps falling, organic/repeat demand is kicking in.
The one-line thesis: Capital doesn't build a brand — it just gets attracted to one that's already working. Consumer obsession, plus doing things in the right order (Consumer → Channel → Competition → Capital, and Proposition → Product → Packaging → Pricing → Platform → Promotion), is what actually builds a 1000 crore brand. "Think like a bootstrapped founder, execute like a growth-stage founder."

Overall takeaway

Capital doesn't build a brand — consumer obsession and the right order of decisions do. Get Consumer → Channel → Competition → Capital right, then execute Proposition → Product → Packaging → Pricing → Platform → Promotion in that exact sequence, and the money takes care of itself.
