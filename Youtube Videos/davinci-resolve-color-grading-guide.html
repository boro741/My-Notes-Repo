Before you touch a single slider

Get your room ready

Before any color grading happens, two boring settings make everything else work correctly. Skip these and your grade will look wrong no matter what you do afterwards.

  • Open Settings → Color Management. Set Timeline color space to DaVinci Wide Gamut Intermediate.
  • Set Output color space to Rec.709 A if you're on a Mac, or Rec.709 Gamma 2.2/2.4 on Windows. This makes your exported video look exactly like what you see while editing.

💡 Why does this even matter?

Think of "color space" as the rulebook your screen and your export use to decode color. If the timeline and the output disagree, your careful grade can look completely different once you export it. Setting this once, upfront, keeps what-you-see = what-you-get.

Once that's set, open the Color page (bottom toolbar). If it looks cluttered on your first visit, that's normal — turn off the Clips strip, the Timeline strip, and the Gallery panel from the icons at the top so only your image and the node area remain. Drag the panel dividers until you have a clean, spacious layout. A tidy workspace makes everything below far less overwhelming.

The shortcut worth taking

Grab the free cheat-sheet tool

Instead of building a color-grading setup from scratch, this tutorial uses a free, ready-made node tree — nicknamed a "Power Grade" — that already has every tool arranged in the right order.

💡 What's a "node"?

A node is one single adjustment step, shown as a little box you can plug things into. Instead of one giant panel of sliders, DaVinci Resolve lets you chain small, focused steps together — one node just for exposure, another just for color, another just for the final polish. That chain is your "node tree."

To use it: open the Gallery panel, drag the downloaded Power Grade files into a Power Grade folder, then right-click it and choose Apply Grade on any clip. It instantly builds this entire node chain for you:

Node 1 · CST

Undo the flat "log" look

If you shot in "Log," your footage arrives looking washed-out and gray on purpose. The first node — called the CST — undoes that so the image looks normal again, before any actual grading starts.

💡 What is "Log" footage, and what's a CST?

Log is a flat, low-contrast recording style cameras use to capture more shadow and highlight detail. It looks bad straight out of the camera on purpose — it's raw material, not the final image. The CST (Color Space Transform) node tells Resolve exactly which camera and log format you used (e.g. Sony S-Log3, Canon Log, Blackmagic Film) so it can convert that flat footage back into a normal-looking image.

Just type your camera's color space and gamma into this node — for example S-Gamut3.Cine / S-Log3 for Sony, or Canon Cinema Gamut / Canon Log 3 for Canon. Get this one setting right, and everything downstream gets much easier.

Nodes 2–4 · Exposure, contrast, saturation

Balance the image

Next come three "balancing" nodes. Their only job is to spread the image's tones from true black to true white, using as much of the available range as possible — nothing creative happens here yet.

Reading the Scope
Before

Flat log footage: everything bunched in the middle of the scope.

After balancing

Shadows near the bottom, highlights near the top — full range used.

The scope (Parade or Waveform) shows exactly where your tones sit. Stretching it out = more information to work with later.

💡 Lift, Gamma, Gain & Offset — what's the difference?

These four wheels are how you nudge color and brightness in DaVinci. Each one targets a different part of the image.

LiftShadows
GammaMidtones
GainHighlights
OffsetWhole image

A simple routine: push Offset up a touch to lift the overall image, pull Lift down slightly to ground the shadows, push Gamma up to brighten midtones (where skin usually lives), and raise Gain for the highlights. Watch the scope the whole time — if the line slams flat against the very top or bottom, you're clipping and losing detail permanently, so back off.

For Contrast, use your eyes: if skin looks muddy or blotchy, lower the contrast slightly, or drag the pivot point left so the contrast affects shadows more than highlights. For Saturation, add just a little — flat log footage needs some color back before the next step can work properly.

⚠️ Why bother balancing at all?

The next chapter relies on picking out specific colors (like skin) from the rest of the image. That's nearly impossible on a flat, low-saturation image — everything looks too similar. A well-balanced image separates naturally, which makes every step after this one far easier.

The Qualifier & Power Windows

Separate the colors

This is where you tell Resolve "only change this part of the image" — usually skin, sky, or clothing — so later adjustments don't affect everything at once.

💡 What's a Qualifier?

A qualifier lets you click on a color (say, someone's skin) and Resolve automatically selects every pixel that's similar to it. Press Shift+H any time to preview exactly what's selected — colored areas are selected, gray areas aren't.

A first click rarely gives a perfect selection. Refine it with a few sliders:

  • Hue width — narrows the selection to just the color range you clicked (e.g. only reds/oranges for skin).
  • Saturation & Luminance — excludes areas that are too dull or too bright/dark to be a real match.
  • Denoise & Blur radius — softens the edges of the selection so it doesn't look harsh or cut-out.

If the color selection alone isn't precise enough, add a Power Window — a shape (circle, square, or a freehand outline with the pen tool) that masks off an area of the frame. For a moving subject, Resolve's built-in tracker can follow it automatically frame by frame. If you have the paid Studio version, Magic Mask does this almost instantly with far less manual drawing.

💡 Bonus: the Vectorscope skin-tone line

Open the Vectorscope and turn on Show Skin Tone Indicator. It draws a line showing exactly where natural skin tones should sit. Nudge your Gamma wheel until your subject's skin lands on that line for a realistic (not orange, not gray) result.

Stacking selections with the Layer Mixer

Once you've isolated one thing (say, skin), you can add another node for a second object (like a jacket) and a third for everything else (the background). A special Layer Mixer node stacks these with a simple rule: whatever you isolated first always wins.

1
Main objectHighest priority — usually skin or your subject.
2
Second objectNext priority — e.g. clothing.
3
BackgroundLowest priority — everything left over.

This is exactly how the classic "orange skin, teal background" look gets made: color the background freely, and the skin and clothing simply stay protected underneath because they were isolated first.

⚠️ Don't overdo it

Push any single color isolation too hard and the image starts to look broken — harsh edges, blotchy skin, fake-looking color. Small, subtle separations always look more convincing than dramatic ones.

The Global node

Build the final look

With the image balanced and separated, this is where the actual creative decisions happen — the mood, the color cast, the personality of the shot.

💡 Serial vs. Parallel (Global) nodes

Nodes can chain two different ways. It matters more than it sounds.

Serial nodes

Each node builds on the last. Handy for quick fine-tuning, but a little information gets lost with every step.

Parallel (Global) nodes

Every node reads from the same original source. Nothing gets lost, which is why the "Global" look is built here.

Inside the Global node, three tools do most of the work:

  • Curves — drag the shadows, midtones, and highlights of the tone curve to add gentle contrast, exactly like the balancing step, but now with intent.
  • Primary wheels — this is where you add an actual color cast: cooler shadows, warmer highlights, or any combination you like.
  • A reference image (optional) — drop in a still you like the look of, place it side-by-side on the scope, and roughly match where its tones sit. Great when you're not sure where to start.

Prefer a specific, exact color? The Hue vs Hue curve or the six-sided Color Warper let you grab one precise color on a graph and shift it wherever you like, without touching anything else in the frame.

Adjustment nodes

Add small touches

A few optional finishing details live in the "Adjustment" nodes. None of these are essential, but they add polish.

💡 Why not just use the Saturation slider?

The regular saturation slider also quietly changes brightness and contrast as a side effect. The Color Slicer's saturation tool only touches saturation — nothing else — so colors intensify without the image looking artificially brighter.

If you're grading a person and have the paid Studio version, Face Refinement can automatically detect a face and offer gentle, targeted controls — skin smoothing, brightening the eyes, reducing shine, or softening eye bags. Small, subtle nudges here go a long way; overdo any single one and it starts to look artificial.

Skin smoothingKeep it light — full smoothing looks plastic-y.
Eye brightenA tiny lift makes eyes pop without looking fake.

You can also revisit Power Windows here to brighten a subject in the middle of the frame or darken the edges for a subtle vignette — small, targeted brightness shifts that quietly guide the viewer's eye.

Fine-tune & Final nodes

Finish with grain & sharpness

The very last two nodes exist purely to add texture and clarity — the difference between a "digital-looking" grade and one that feels finished.

Film grain

A tiny bit of grain adds texture and makes an image feel less sterile. You have two options:

  • If you're on the free version of Resolve, use a downloadable film-grain overlay clip, set its blend mode to Overlay, and layer it on top of your graded clip.
  • If you have the Studio version, drop the built-in Film Grain effect onto its own node and adjust size, softness, and strength directly.

💡 My export looks slow or choppy — why?

Film grain is heavy on your computer. If playback stutters while you're working, temporarily hide grades with Shift+D, switch your timeline to quarter-resolution playback, or drop to a 1080p timeline while editing — then switch everything back to full quality before your final export.

Sharpening & scaling

The final node usually adds a touch of sharpening. Lower the blur radius slightly to sharpen, or raise it to soften. A lesser-known control called Scaling decides which size of detail gets sharpened — push it up and fine details like eyelashes and skin texture become noticeably crisper, especially useful before uploading to social media where compression can flatten an image.

The Deliver page

Export your clip

Once you're happy, switch to the Deliver page. This is where your finished, graded clip leaves DaVinci Resolve and becomes an actual video file.

💡 Got subtitles?

Subtitles won't appear in your exported video unless you open the subtitle track settings and switch it to Burn into video before rendering. It's an easy step to forget.

⚠️ Slow or glitchy exports?

A laptop running hot will drop frames and can leave white blocks in your export. Export somewhere cool, or point a fan at your laptop — it genuinely speeds things up and prevents render errors.

Rec.709 footage

Shot on a phone? Same idea

Not everyone shoots in Log. If your clip came straight from a regular phone, the exact same six-step chain still applies — only the very first node changes.

In the CST node, set both the input and output to Rec.709 / Rec.709. This effectively passes the image through unchanged, since a normal phone video isn't flat like log footage — it's already contrasty and saturated out of the camera.

Good newsPhone footage already has strong contrast — balancing needs less work.
Go gentlyMost phone video is 8-bit, so pushing color separation too hard causes banding and blotchy skin fast.

Beyond that one change, the process is identical: balance, isolate skin and sky as color-qualified objects, build a Global look, add small touches, then finish with grain and sharpening. It's genuinely possible to make basic phone footage look cinematic — you don't need to own a proper camera to start practicing color grading.

One last thing

Six steps. Every clip. Every time.

You don't need to memorize color theory to grade well — you need to run the same short checklist on every clip until it becomes automatic. Here's the whole guide in one glance:

01Set upColor space + clean workspace
02Convert (CST)Undo log, or pass Rec.709 through
03BalanceStretch exposure, contrast, saturation
04SeparateQualify skin, clothes, background
05Build the lookCurves + wheels in the Global node
06FinishGrain, sharpening, then export

Grade a few clips, compare your before-and-afters, and keep an eye on images you admire — your taste sharpens far faster than any tutorial can teach it.
