What Top-Tier Software Architects Do Differently

Gregor Hohpe, a retired Silicon Valley architect who built systems at Big Tech, reveals the one thing that separates great architects from the rest. It's not complexity. It's not knowing all the buzzwords. It's something almost invisible.

Source · Beyond Coding Podcast Guest · Gregor Hohpe (AWS, Google veteran) Focus · Architecture behavior, mentorship, and decision-making

Good architects are usually the ones where magically everything goes well and nobody knows exactly why. The bad ones? They're much easier to spot. They spew buzzwords. They tell people "everything must be cloud-native" or "loosely coupled." They hoard decisions. They act like oracles who have all the answers.

But here's what separates the truly great architects from everyone else: they don't try to be the smartest person in the room. They make everybody else smarter.

This isn't a feel-good statement. It's a operating principle that changes everything about how you approach architecture, how you communicate, and how you build trust. In this deep dive, we'll explore what Gregor Hohpe learned from decades in Silicon Valley, and why the best architects often seem to effortlessly solve problems that look impossibly complex to everyone else.

Part OneThe Problem with Bad Architects

The easiest way to understand great architecture is to first see what goes wrong. Bad architects fall into predictable traps, and recognizing them is your first line of defense against becoming one.

The Three Types of Bad Architects

The Buzzword Oracle

Believes every problem has a silver-bullet answer. "Everything must be cloud-native." "Microservices is the only way." Posts architectural rules on walls for people to follow without context or reasoning.

The Dictator

Hoards decision-making power. Wants to approve every component, every technology choice, every line of reasoning. Creates a checkpoint system where nothing moves without their say-so.

The Oracle

Expects people to come asking questions and looking for magic answers. Doesn't engage in dialogue or collaboration. Spouts decisions without helping people understand the reasoning behind them.

Why They Fail

All three versions share one common trait: they don't trust the people around them. They don't amplify others' thinking. They create bottlenecks instead of bridges. And they turn architecture into a gatekeeping function rather than an enabling one.

💡 What You Need to Know

Gatekeeping vs. Enabling: A gatekeeper controls who can do what. An enabler helps people make better decisions. Great architects are enablers. They might even "disappear" into the background because everything just works.

"Too many architects tried to find answers when they don't have a question. Here's the thing that answers all possible questions. That would be nice. That doesn't work anymore." — Gregor Hohpe

This is the core insight. In a rapidly changing world, you can't pre-answer every question. You can't design a reference architecture that covers all cases. The best you can do is help people think clearly about their specific problems.

Part TwoThe Amplifier Mindset

Great architects operate from a completely different premise. They don't see themselves as the smartest person who needs to make all the decisions. They see themselves as an amplifier.

What an Amplifier Does

An amplifier doesn't generate signal. It takes existing signal and makes it stronger, clearer, more useful. Applied to architecture, it means:

  • Absorb context. Listen deeply to what people are telling you about their system, their constraints, their business needs.
  • Uncover blind spots. Ask questions that reveal assumptions people didn't know they were making.
  • Offer perspective. Help people see different angles and trade-offs they might not have considered.
  • Distill complexity. Take a messy problem and help people see its structure more clearly.
  • Make it safe. Create an environment where people can think out loud without fear of judgment.

Notice what's not on this list: telling people what to do. Approving or rejecting their ideas. Hoarding information or decision-making power.

The Key Question

When someone comes to you with a problem, your instinct might be to solve it for them. But the amplifier asks differently: "How can I help you think through this better? What are we missing? What trade-offs are we implicitly making?"

This is harder than just giving answers. But it's infinitely more valuable.

The Phantom Sketch Artist Metaphor

Gregor uses a powerful metaphor to explain this dynamic. In the old days, when a bank got robbed, police would call in a sketch artist. The witnesses knew what the robber looked like. The sketch artist had never seen the person. But together, they created an accurate drawing.

The sketch artist didn't know the subject. The witnesses couldn't draw. But the collaboration produced something neither could achieve alone.

As an architect, you're often the phantom sketch artist. You don't know their system as well as they do. They don't have your skill at expressing ideas visually and structurally. But if you collaborate authentically, you extract knowledge they already have and play it back to them in a format that suddenly makes everything obvious.

💡 The Collaboration Shifts

You're not saying "you're doing this wrong." You're saying "let me draw what I think I'm hearing, and you tell me if I got it right." They immediately see gaps, because now the problem is visual and concrete instead of vague and verbal.

The best feedback you can get is: "That's wrong." Because wrong means you can iterate toward right. Vague means you're both confused.

Part ThreeComplexity: Your Job is to Conquer It, Not Add to It

Software today is genuinely more complex than it was a decade ago. Distributed systems, microservices, cloud platforms, container orchestration—this isn't a choice. It's just how modern software works.

Inherent Complexity vs. Accidental Complexity

Here's the critical distinction: some complexity is inherent. If you're building distributed systems, you have to deal with retries, timeouts, idempotency, backpressure, retry storms. You can't design your way around it. That's physics, not opinion.

But a lot of complexity is accidental. It comes from poor abstractions, unclear naming, unnecessary coupling, or building for scale you don't yet need.

A great architect's job is to:

  • Identify inherent complexity and make it easier to deal with through good abstractions
  • Eliminate accidental complexity ruthlessly
  • Keep cognitive load manageable so people can actually change the system without breaking things
The Hidden Cost of Complexity

When systems get too complex, something invisible happens: people become afraid to touch them. They stop making changes. A small code change creates unexpected failures somewhere else. Nobody knows why. So nobody wants to risk it.

When you're afraid to change your code, you've got legacy. And that's the worst case in a fast-changing world.

Simple is Not a Target, It's a Constraint

There's a famous principle: "Make it as simple as possible, but no simpler." This is crucial. You can't simplify inherent complexity out of existence. What you can do is make it intuitive to deal with.

"Good architects don't pretend the complexity doesn't exist. They make it easier for people to deal with." — Gregor Hohpe

A distributed system is complex. But a well-designed distributed system API can hide that complexity behind something intuitive. The complexity is still there—it's just not in people's faces at every decision.

Part FourHard Skills, Soft Skills, and the Multiplier Effect

To be a great architect, you need both technical depth and communication skill. But it's not just about having both. It's about the intersection—where they amplify each other.

The Hard Skills You Need

  • Understand your tech stack deeply. Not just theoretically—you need hands-on experience
  • Know the trade-offs. Every technology choice has downsides. Understanding them is essential
  • Learn domain-driven design. How to model complex problems in code
  • Understand operational aspects. Monitoring, observability, failure modes
  • Keep learning. Technology changes fast. Decisions made 10 years ago might not hold today
⚠️ The Dangerous Trap

Having technical skills from 5-10 years ago and no longer keeping them current is one of the biggest mistakes architects make. You might remember when microservices were the answer to everything. But that was before cloud databases scaled effortlessly. Before serverless was practical. Before containers became standard.

Your old decisions were right for their time. But "their time" is over.

The Soft Skills That Actually Matter

Communication. Listening. Asking good questions. Helping people articulate what they already know. The ability to sketch things out visually. Knowing when to push back and when to let something go.

But here's the secret: you don't need to be good at all of these separately. You need to be exceptional at the intersection.

The Multiplier Effect

It's not "good tech person" + "good communicator" = "good technical communicator." It's something deeper. It's when your technical knowledge makes your communication more precise, and your communication skill helps you extract technical insights from others.

The Test

Do people come to you to make them smarter? Not to solve problems for them, but as a sounding board? A rubber ducky they can explain ideas to, who asks a few clarifying questions, and suddenly they see things differently?

The Visual Thinking Skill Nobody Teaches

One of Gregor's biggest insights is about visual communication. Most architects learn to talk and write. Very few learn to sketch and diagram effectively.

Why does this matter? Because words are fuzzy. You can say "these two systems have a relationship" and both people will imagine it differently. But draw two boxes with or without a line between them, and suddenly there's no ambiguity.

Visual thinking works because it forces precision. A picture doesn't have the fuzziness that words do.

✏️ On Learning to Draw

You don't need to be a gifted artist. You need muscle memory. Practice. The best way to learn is to pair with someone who already does it, watch them, get feedback. Don't learn from books—learn by doing it with someone who can coach you.

The Ping-Pong Between Left Brain and Right Brain

This is the real skill. Good diagrams are a weird combination:

  • Left brain (structured, logical): You use your engineering mind to get something down. Boxes, arrows, relationships, connections.
  • Right brain (creative, intuitive): You step back and ask "are we missing a dimension? Is there another way to express this? What aren't we seeing?"

You iterate between these two modes. Structure, then creativity, then structure again. The diagrams that stick are the ones where both hemispheres show up.

"You don't need to be a genius at either. But if you can flip back and forth, that makes a world of difference." — Gregor Hohpe

Part FiveKeeping Your Knowledge Current

Technology evolves faster than any individual can keep up with alone. The solution isn't to work harder. It's to build a network.

The Scout, Not the Cartographer

Enterprise architecture used to mean creating huge landscape diagrams. Cataloging every application. Documenting every integration. By the time you finished, everything had changed.

The modern role is more like a scout. You have an objective. You want to answer a specific question: "Should we adopt GenAI? How should we integrate this new framework?" You send scouts to look at the relevant parts. They come back with a timely, purpose-driven map.

The map isn't trying to be perfect or complete. It's trying to answer: for this decision, what matters?

The Danger of Snapshot Thinking

You can't maintain a perfect, up-to-date diagram of your entire system anymore. The world moves too fast. Instead of being the cartographer, be the scout. Have a purpose in mind. Answer specific questions. Then move on.

Building Your Network is Not Optional

You cannot possibly stay current on your own. You need:

  • People who are hands-on. Talk to people building things with the technologies you need to understand
  • Trusted sources. Not social media (everyone's selling something). Real peers you can grab coffee with
  • Mentors and mentees. Teaching forces you to sharpen your thinking. Learning from others keeps you humble
  • Regular engagement. This isn't a quarterly thing. It's ongoing conversation

The practical approach: when you need to catch up on something new, don't read blog posts or watch YouTube. Find someone trusted who's actually building with it. Have them spend a few hours with you. Show you their code. Explain their reasoning. You'll learn more in two days of high-bandwidth engagement than in two months of solo research.

Part SixThe Political Game: Building and Spending Capital

Architecture isn't just technical. It's political. And understanding the politics isn't cynical—it's how you actually get things done.

Political Capital is Real Currency

You have a limited amount of "political capital" in any organization. You build it by:

  • Delivering on promises
  • Being supportive and collaborative
  • Being transparent about what you're doing
  • Being fair and open-minded
  • Building trust over time

You spend it when you push back, challenge assumptions, or call out a problem that powerful people have invested in.

The Core Principle

You must earn before you spend. And you must spend wisely. Don't waste capital on small skirmishes everywhere. Save it for the big things where you genuinely believe something is going to be a train wreck.

The Jester Metaphor

In medieval courts, the jester was the one person allowed to tell the truth. They could criticize the king because they had no other agenda. They weren't trying to increase their power or budget. They just wanted to speak the truth.

This is what great architects do. You have high influence but little direct power. You don't control budgets or headcount. So you can be trusted. You're not trying to empire-build. You're trying to help the organization make better decisions.

This trust is your superpower. Guard it carefully. Spend your capital wisely.

💡 The Balance

You need to be comfortable knowing that not everything will be perfect. You can't change every architectural sin. Some cats will do what they want regardless of what you recommend. That's okay. Sleep at night by focusing on the things that truly matter.

Part SevenSuitable, Not Perfect: Making the Right Trade-offs

There is no universally good or bad architecture. Only suitable and unsuitable ones. And suitability depends entirely on the context.

Why Trade-offs Matter More Than Best Practices

Everyone wants to say their architecture is "good." But good at what? Fast? Scalable? Cheap to build? Easy to understand? Easy to deploy? You can't optimize for everything.

The right question isn't "is this a good architecture?" It's "does this architecture do what it needs to do, given the constraints we have?"

Good Architecture

Makes conscious trade-offs. The team understands what they're optimizing for and what they're de-optimizing. They can articulate why.

Bad Architecture

Makes unconscious trade-offs. Or makes trade-offs and pretends there aren't any downsides. Or follows a best practice without understanding why it's relevant to this specific problem.

The Big Ball of Mud is Not Always Bad

There's a software pattern called "big ball of mud." It's usually positioned as a negative. Spaghetti code. Poor structure. Classic anti-pattern.

But the authors who defined the pattern also noted something important: the big ball of mud is quick, cheap, and requires limited skill set to build. Those aren't bad qualities if you have a tight deadline and limited resources.

Maybe building a modular architecture when you need to launch in a month is the wrong trade-off. Maybe shipping something that works, even if it's messy, is the right call.

The key is understanding that you're making a trade-off. You're optimizing for speed at the expense of maintainability. That's a conscious choice. Just know that when the system grows, you'll need to refactor or rebuild.

"Architecture isn't good or bad. It's suitable or unsuitable for what you're trying to do." — Gregor Hohpe

Framing the Solution Space

One of Gregor's most powerful techniques is to expand the solution space instead of narrowing it. Instead of debating "monolith vs. microservices," you could break it down into two dimensions:

  • Design-time modularity (well-structured vs. spaghetti)
  • Runtime modularity (single deployment vs. many deployments)

Now you have four quadrants. A modular monolith is suddenly a valid option. You're no longer arguing about which is "better." You're discussing which quadrant is right for your constraints.

🎯 The Framing Technique

When conversations get stuck in "X vs. Y," step back and frame the underlying dimensions. What are we really trying to optimize? What are the constraints? Once everyone agrees on the map, they can discuss the territory without fighting over directions.

Part EightCommon Traps to Avoid

Even great architects fall into predictable traps. Knowing them means you can avoid them.

Trap #1: Stumbling on the Finish Line

You've done your job well. The problem, which seemed impossibly complex, suddenly becomes simple. Everyone looks at the diagram and says "of course, why didn't I see that?"

And then you doubt yourself. Could it really be that simple? You start second-guessing. Maybe I'm missing something. Maybe I need to add more detail, more rigor, more complexity.

This is the wrong instinct. If it suddenly makes sense, you've done an excellent job. You've abstracted away the irrelevant parts and exposed the true structure. That's what great architecture looks like.

Don't Stumble on the Finish Line

We've gotten so in love with complexity that we doubt ourselves when we find simplicity. If it seems obvious in hindsight, that's not a failure. That's the goal.

Trap #2: Underestimating Your Value

You unearth a hidden assumption. You sketch something out. You ask clarifying questions. And people say "that's obvious" or "that's too simple."

Don't let that diminish what you've done. If it was that obvious, they would have done it themselves. You extracted knowledge they had but couldn't articulate. You made the invisible visible. That's extremely valuable work.

Being a catalyst, making it easy for others to think clearly, sketching something out that suddenly makes sense—these are highly valuable skills. Don't let anyone tell you otherwise.

Trap #3: Using Tools as a Substitute (Not an Amplifier)

With generative AI, this trap is now more dangerous. You can ask an LLM for architecture advice. It generates something that looks reasonable. You paste it into your deck.

Here's the problem: you now have an answer, but you don't understand the reasoning. You can't defend it. You can't articulate the trade-offs. When someone asks "why this approach?", you've got nothing.

Tools should amplify your thinking, not replace it. Use them to get a starting point. But then you do the real work: understanding the problem, validating the solution against your specific constraints, articulating why this is the right call.

The Tool Test

Does the tool work for you, or do you work for the tool? If you're just copy-pasting output, you're working for the tool. If you're using it to expand your thinking and then shaping it with your own reasoning, you're in control.

Trap #4: Trying to Answer Questions You Weren't Asked

This is the "reference architecture" trap. You try to design an architecture that handles every possible question. That could ever come up. For any team. In any context.

It doesn't work. The world changes too fast. And you end up creating something too generic to be useful.

Instead: start with a question. "Should we adopt this new framework? How do we scale this subsystem? What's our strategy for AI?" Answer that question, specifically, with context.

Part NineFrom Engineer to Architect: The Path

Not everyone should become an architect. And that's okay. There are three highly valuable career paths in software.

Individual Contributor Engineer

Stay deep in your specialization. Become the best-in-class at what you do. No management overhead. Just pure technical excellence.

Technical Manager

Lead a team. Understand people, motivation, career growth. Still maintain technical credibility. Different skill set than architecture.

Architect

Stay an IC but with a focus on system-wide thinking. High influence, low direct power. Need network and political awareness.

The Choice

All three are valid. Pick based on what energizes you. If you love making individual people smarter and helping systems scale, architecture might be right. If you love coaching people and building teams, management. If you love pure technical depth, stay IC.

The Paradox of Success

If you do your job well as an architect, you eventually won't be needed. You've lifted everyone else to the point where they don't need you for basic stuff. Great, right?

The catch: you can't plan for this. Organizational change, turnover, new people arriving—these things mean you'll always have work. The goal is a direction to aim for, not something you'll necessarily achieve.

But if you do reach that point? Gregor's advice: "Just make sure nobody finds out. And enjoy it."

Part TenThe Architect's Relationship with Executives

Board members, CEOs, CFOs—these aren't going to question your technical decisions. That's your domain. But they have an incredible nose for fuzzy thinking.

What Executives Are Actually Checking For

They're not auditing your knowledge of Kubernetes. They're auditing your reasoning. They're asking:

  • Have you considered alternatives?
  • What metrics will tell us if this is working?
  • What's the upfront investment? Can we defer this?
  • How do you know success when you see it?
  • Are there hidden assumptions buried in here?

If you can walk through your reasoning clearly, you'll stand out. Most architects can't. They hand-wave. They cite best practices without context. They reverse-engineer from their preferred answer.

Executives can smell that. And suddenly they lose confidence.

The Architect Elevator (Going Upstairs)

When you go present to executives, you need both skills: a catchy visual or story that sticks, backed up with solid reasoning. If someone asks "why this approach?", you can articulate the logic. You're not hand-waving. You've actually thought it through.

This is where the ping-pong between left brain and right brain becomes essential. You need the visual or story (right brain) but it has to be defensible with solid engineering reasoning (left brain).

Part ElevenThe Hidden Curriculum: What Nobody Teaches You About Architecture

Most architecture training focuses on patterns, technologies, and design principles. But the real curriculum is invisible.

Know Your Domain

You can't be a phantom sketch artist if you don't understand the subject matter. If you're designing a banking system, you need to understand banking. If it's e-commerce, you need to understand retail and fulfillment.

This doesn't mean you need to be a domain expert. But you need enough fluency to ask good questions. To understand the pain points. To recognize when a technical solution is solving the wrong business problem.

Understand the Anatomy

Good sketch artists study human anatomy. Not so they can draw realistic people (though that helps), but so they understand the underlying structure. Where the joints are. How the body moves. The skeleton that makes everything possible.

Similarly, great architects understand the "anatomy" of their systems. Not just what it looks like, but why it's structured the way it is. What are the load-bearing walls? Where are the weak points?

Be Social, Not Isolated

The worst version of an architect is someone who sits in a quiet chamber and receives requirements and spits out designs. They're isolated from the people building the system. From the users of the system. From the constraints of the real world.

You cannot be a great architect in isolation. Period. You need to be around people. Talking to them. Learning from their mistakes and successes. Building a network. Staying current.

ConclusionThe Invisible Skill That Makes Great Architects

If you take away one thing from this: great architects don't try to be the smartest person in the room. They make everybody else smarter. And through that, systems naturally become better.

This is an amplifier mindset. It's uncomfortable at first. You're trained as an engineer to solve problems. To know things. To be right. And now you're being asked to do something different: to create the conditions for others to solve problems well.

But this is where the real power lies.

You don't control whether microservices get adopted or monoliths stay in place. You can't dictate technology choices. You can't approve every decision. But you can help people think clearly. You can uncover blind spots. You can ask the question that reframes an entire debate.

And when you do that consistently, magically, everything goes well. Nobody knows exactly why. That's not a mystery. That's excellence.

The Practical Starting Point

Start small. With your next architectural decision or team discussion, try this: instead of giving an answer, ask questions. Instead of approving or rejecting, sketch something and ask "is this what you meant?" Instead of lecturing, listen deeply.

See what changes. The difference might surprise you.
