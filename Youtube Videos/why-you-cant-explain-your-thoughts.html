Why You Know It, But Can't Say It

You have a great idea in your head. Then you open your mouth and it comes out messy. Here's why that happens, and five simple fixes. Spoiler: you don't need a bigger vocabulary.

Chapter One

The problem we all share

You're in a meeting. Someone asks your opinion. You know exactly what you think. But the words come out in the wrong order, you jump between topics, you forget your main point, and you walk away thinking, "That's not what I meant."

Most people's fix is to read more books and learn fancier words. But even after that, explaining your own thoughts still feels hard. Why?

Maybe vocabulary was never the problem. Maybe the real difficulty is how your brain organizes a thought before it becomes speech. Once you see that, getting better at speaking has less to do with sounding clever, and more to do with understanding your own mind.

Chapter Two

Big words don't make you smart

In 2006, psychologist Daniel Oppenheimer tested a common belief: do fancy words make you look smarter? The answer was no, usually the opposite.

Readers judged writing full of needlessly complex words less favorably than writing that said the same thing simply. His paper was even given a joke title that was itself needlessly complicated, to prove the point.

💡
Processing fluency is how easily your brain can take in information. Easy to read means it feels familiar and clear. Hard to read means the listener has to work, and often trusts you less.
Sounds fancy"We should leverage and utilize the new framework to optimize our operational efficiency going forward."
Says more"We should use the new system because it saves time."

Both give the same advice. The simple one gives a clear recommendation and a reason. Technical terms are fine when an idea truly needs them. The trouble starts when your words serve your image instead of the listener's understanding.

Your goal is not to make people notice your vocabulary. It's to help them understand your thinking.

Chapter Three

From web to tree to string

If words aren't the issue, why is it hard to explain even simple things? Because speaking is a three-step job, and you can only say one word at a time.

Psycholinguist Willem Levelt described speech production roughly like this: first you work out what to say, then you prepare the words and grammar, then you speak. Knowing the idea and knowing how to say it are two different challenges.

💡
Macro planning means deciding the big picture before you speak: what matters, how the pieces connect, and what order to say them in.

Cognitive scientist Steven Pinker (in his book The Sense of Style) points out the challenge. A handy way to picture it, as a teaching metaphor and not a literal brain scan:

How a thought becomes speech
1. WebYour thoughts: everything connected to everything
2. TreeStructure: main point, support, examples
3. StringSpeech: one word after another

Nobody can hear your web. They only hear the string, in the order you choose.

🧭 A quick example

Your manager asks, "Why is the project delayed?" You know: the client changed the requirements and the team needed extra time. But you start with Monday, then Tuesday's email, then a chat with a colleague. Thirty seconds later there's still no answer. You understood the project fine. You just hadn't organized the information around the question.

Chapter Four

The five traps (and fixes)

Most explaining problems fall into five common traps. See which ones feel familiar.

1

Reaching for the bigger word

You want to say "We need to fix the problem." But you start wondering: "Should I say implement a corrective strategy?" Now part of your attention is on how you sound, not on what you mean.

💡
Impression management is how we try to shape what others think of us, like dressing up for an interview. It's normal. It only hurts when it distracts you from the actual message.
FixAsk: "What am I actually trying to say?" Then say that. "Reduce the cost" beats "optimize our financial expenditure" if you just mean spend less money.
2

Starting at the beginning, not the point

A friend asks why you left your old job. The real answer is "little room to grow." But you start with when you joined, your duties, your manager changing... and finally reach it. We tend to retell things in the order we remember them, not the order that's easiest to understand.

Backstory firstLong story about your responsibilities, then a new manager, then... finally the reason.
Answer first"Almost. The main work is done, we're just testing one feature."
FixUse the answer-first approach: say the one sentence that answers the question, then add the reasons and background if needed.
3

Explaining too many connected ideas at once

You state your concern, remember an example, which reminds you of last year, and suddenly you're explaining a different problem. Every detail feels relevant to you. The listener is lost.

💡
Cognitive load is how hard your brain has to work on a task. Working memory is your short-term mental notepad, and it's small. Too many unconnected ideas overflow it. (Scientists still debate its exact size.)
FixPick the two points that matter most and announce them: "I have two concerns. One, it costs more than expected. Two, we haven't tested it." Add extra detail later if asked. Don't make your thinking shallow, organize its depth.
4

Panicking when you pause

You get stuck on a word. Silence. You feel their eyes. So you fill it with "um, like, you know" and rush into the next sentence before you've planned it.

I think the plan…is worth testing

That gap is your brain planning. It's normal.

Researchers link pauses to planning: finding words, building a sentence, working out a hard idea. Psychologists Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree found that "um" and "uh" can actually serve a purpose in conversation. (Exactly what each one does is still studied.) The problem isn't the pause. It's anxiety about the pause.

FixIn a tough interview question, say: "Good question. Let me think about the clearest way to explain it." Then take a moment. You don't have to erase every filler word. Just focus on your meaning.
5

Explaining a thought you haven't fully worked out

This one is the big reveal. You feel you understand something. But when you explain it step by step, you get stuck. You can recognize a good explanation, but can't produce one.

💡
Illusion of explanatory depth means we feel we understand how things work, until we're asked to explain the details. Then we find gaps. (Not every struggle means this. Sometimes it's nerves or word-finding. But sometimes it is.)

Test yourself: you know a fridge keeps food cold. But can you explain how it moves heat from inside to the room? Trying to explain shows what you still need to learn.

FixDon't re-read and assume familiarity equals understanding. Close the book and explain it in your own words. When you get stuck, don't hunt for a fancier word. Ask: "Do I understand this well enough to explain it?"

Chapter Five

One daily practice

Once a day, pick something you think you understand: an article, a decision, a work process, a new idea. Explain it out loud as if talking to someone who knows nothing about it.

1
Pick one thingSomething you believe you understand.
2
Explain it aloud, start to finishNo script. No trying to sound polished.
3
Notice where you get stuckDid you skip a step? Bury the main point? Find a gap in your knowledge?
4
Fix that part and try againYou're training both understanding and communication.

Chapter Six

Four habits to start today

1. State your main point first

Before a meeting or hard conversation, ask: "If they remember only one thing I say, what should it be?" Put it in one sentence. Then choose two or three details that truly support it.

2. Give your explanation a visible structure

Use phrases like "There are two reasons I think this will work," or "The main problem is this, and here's why." They don't just sound organized, they help you organize the message and give listeners a path to follow.

3. Practice with no one to impress

Explain a familiar topic aloud. Record yourself if it helps. Don't judge your accent or confidence. Ask three questions:

Clear?Did I make my main point clear?
Relevant?Did each sentence help that point?
Lean?Did I add details I didn't need?

Pick one thing to improve and try again. The goal isn't a perfect performance. It's a better process.

4. Stop treating every pause as a problem

You don't have to answer instantly. You don't have to fill every silence. You don't need a sophisticated word when a simple one is more accurate. Give yourself time to decide what you want to say.

🔗 How the five traps connect

Fancy words come from managing impressions. Backstory comes from not choosing your answer. Rambling comes from not organizing your ideas. Rushing comes from discomfort with pauses. Struggling to explain comes from a half-formed thought. Different problems, different fixes. But all point to one thing: clear speaking needs planning, attention, understanding, and organizing information for another person.

Chapter Seven

Which one are you?

Your mind can stay curious, complex, and full of connections. You don't have to simplify everything you think. You only have to decide which part of that thinking belongs in the conversation right now.

So which trap do you recognize most? Bigger words? Too much backstory? Explaining everything at once? Panic on pauses? Or a thought that isn't fully formed yet? Name it, then fix that one.

Clear speaking isn't about finding the right words. It's about knowing what you mean.
