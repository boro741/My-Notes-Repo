Chapter One

The luck machine

Every outcome you've ever had — good or bad — is downstream of luck. You didn't choose the language you grew up speaking, the school that shaped you, or the accident you never had. All success is luck. The only thing that separates people isn't whether luck is involved — it's whether they've learned to engineer it.

Think of it as a stat in a game. Yesterday your "luck stat" was a 5. A reel went viral, a thousand strangers now know your name, and your luck stat quietly ticks up to a 7. Do that enough times — increase your surface area, let more people know you exist — and your luck stat compounds into the hundreds. That's the entire mechanism behind ten years of unlikely opportunity.

Same person. Same instincts. The only variable that moved is surface area — how many people know you exist.

The Lightning Catcher

Skyscrapers wear a spire to catch lightning before it hits anything else. Content is the same idea — you build the spire, and stand ready for the strike. You can't schedule the bolt. You can only make sure that when it comes, it hits you.

Here's the part people miss: content isn't the transaction. It's the antenna. It doesn't sell a t-shirt today — it makes it far more likely that something good finds you eventually: a fundraiser gets funded, a job appears, an embassy emails you out of nowhere. You don't get to pick which lightning strikes. You just have to be standing there with a rod.

📩 The email that actually happened

On May 19th, the U.S. Embassy reached out cold — no pitch, no funnel, just years of visible surface area. That's not a marketing case study. That's a lightning strike. You don't build content for that specific email. You build it so some email like that becomes inevitable.

Chapter Two

Content vs. distribution

These two words get used interchangeably, and that's the first mistake. Content is a unit of value — utility (I taught you something) or entertainment (I made your scroll worth it). Distribution is what content becomes once enough of that value compounds into a relationship.

Most people who see a piece of content reject it outright. A few watch it once and move on. But a small slice sticks around long enough to watch a second piece, then a tenth, then a hundredth — and at some point they quietly decide: I should follow this person. That decision is distribution. It's not the view count. It's the trust that accumulates in the space between views.

Content → Distribution
every viewer
repeat viewers
followers

Every video is a filter, not a funnel you control. Most people fall through. The ones who don't are your distribution — an audience that now makes small life decisions with you as a quiet input.

That's also why it's not just about views. Someone who's watched a single video has almost no trust in you. Someone who's watched hundreds — who's seen the behind-the-scenes, the outtakes, the version of you that isn't polished — has something closer to a real relationship. The world runs on mimesis: we unconsciously imitate the people we watch closely, which is exactly why distribution quietly shapes behavior, taste, and decisions, not just awareness.

Chapter Three

The trust ladder & the Nike question

Ask a room how much they trust Nike, on a scale of one to ten. You'll get sevens, eights, nines — never a ten. Nobody can say exactly which ad, which sneaker, which moment converted them. They just kept seeing Nike, again and again, until trust quietly crossed a threshold.

Here's the uncomfortable question: why do we assume hiring, partnerships, or deals work any differently? An employer who's "just kept seeing you around" on LinkedIn or Twitter is running the exact same trust calculation as a shopper deciding between two pairs of sneakers. You don't need to be looking for a job today. You need to be known enough that in three years, when you are, the decision already feels made.

"The minute Nike moved from brand-building content to 'let's make three rupees back on every rupee spent,' they lost $25 billion in market cap in a single day."

The instant a brand — or a person — switches into transactional mode, the compounding stops. Trust isn't measurable in real time. But it's very real, and it's very rememberable, and it punishes impatience harshly.

Chapter Four

The 4-7-11 rule

Google's own research put a number on the Nike question. To move a stranger into genuine, unprompted trust, they need to encounter you across roughly four different platforms, at around seven distinct touch points, adding up to eleven hours of total content.

Read that number again: eleven hours. Not eleven minutes — eleven hours, accumulated in small, unremembered slices, spread across feeds, recommendations, a friend's mention, a billboard glimpsed at a red light. Nobody consciously logs it. But it's the real price of "no way, I've never heard of you" turning into "of course, I know exactly who that is."

Chapter Five

How many touches, by deal size

The 4-7-11 rule is an average. The real number scales hard with what you're asking someone to say yes to — a ₹300 t-shirt is a different animal than an ₹80-lakh annual contract.

Touch points needed before a genuine "yes"
Basic B2C5–8 touches
Mid-ticket B2C11 touches · 4 channels
High-consideration B2C12–27 touches · 20+ hrs
B2B vendor shortlist17 touches
Complex B2B (₹80L+/yr)60–100+ touches
avg ≈ 29 touches, all industries

Selling insurance, real estate, a degree, or a course? Expect 12 to 27 touches and 20-plus hours of content before a stranger converts. Closing enterprise deals? Some of AOS's larger contracts took 60 to over 100 touch points before a signature — and plenty of those touches were a prospect quietly watching a video months before ever replying to an email. Across every industry, the average sits around 29 touches. If you're building a career or a business and expecting a shortcut, this is the actual scoreboard.

Chapter Seven

Founder-led content wins

The best brands doing this right now aren't run by a marketing manager with a content calendar. They're founder-led — because the founder is the one person in the building actually thinking in decades, not quarters.

Case Study · Zerodha

Two years into founder-driven content, Zerodha's "Markets by Zerodha" and "WTF" (with Nikhil Kamath) have racked up roughly 10 crore views — with effectively zero performance-marketing spend. The audience for "Markets by Zerodha" checks in every single morning. That's not a campaign. That's a habit — the highest form of distribution there is.

Chapter Eight

Why most marketers never do this

If distribution is this powerful, why doesn't every CMO run straight at it? Because the average marketing manager lasts two to three years in a role — and this is, by nature, a multi-year game with almost no clean attribution.

When a deal closes because someone quietly watched a founder's video eight months ago, no CEO turns around and thanks the marketing department for that specific reel. It just shows up as "someone reached out." Meanwhile, performance ads offer a dashboard, a clean number, and a story a manager can tell in a quarterly review before they move on to the next job. Distribution asks you to take a long bet on a career that might not even be yours in two years. Almost nobody takes that bet voluntarily — which is exactly why the people who do have so little competition.

Chapter Nine

Play the long game.

Whether it's a brand, a business, or your own career — the number of touch points you need with the people who matter isn't one or two. It's somewhere in the twenties or thirties. There is no shortcut past that math. There is only starting today instead of in three years, when you'll wish you had.
