Chapter One

Welcome to Planet Finance

Here's an uncomfortable truth: the things that hurt planet Earth often help planet finance. A war, a famine, a pandemic — for most of us, these are tragedies. For traders, they can be the best year of their career.

One veteran trader admits it outright: 9/11 was his most profitable year ever. He hates that it's true, but it is. This isn't a story about evil people rubbing their hands together. It's about how an entire parallel world works — a world made of numbers, connected by cables and radio waves, where fear and greed are quite literally converted into a price. This world runs alongside our own, mostly invisible, but it touches everything: your pension, the price of your fuel, the shirt on your back. Let's call it, as this documentary does, Planet Finance.

💡 Quick definition: what is "the market," really?

A market is just a place — physical or digital — where buyers and sellers meet to agree on a price. That's it. The drama, the jargon, and the skyscrapers are all built on top of that one simple idea: someone wants to buy, someone wants to sell, and the price is whatever they agree on.

Over the course of this piece, we're going to walk through the whole strange ecosystem: the old shouting trading floors, the oil market that once went below zero, the algorithms that trade in milliseconds, the bonds that pay out when hurricanes strike, and the ordinary people — a Japanese housewife, a Reddit crowd, a Canadian day trader — who got pulled into this world and came out either richer or ruined. By the end, you'll understand why one trader says the whole point of his job is to answer "what," never "why."

Chapter Two

The trading floor: chalk, chaos, and instinct

Before computers, a trading floor looked like a cross between a boxing ring and a stock exchange in a movie. Traders would tell you: walking through those enormous soundproof doors felt like walking into the Colosseum. Silence outside, then a wall of noise and smell hits you the second the doors open.

Every deal was scratched onto a giant blackboard called "the book." Two "board boys" — one left-handed, one right-handed — wrote as fast as humanly possible, recording who bought, who sold, and at what price. There was no dress code for behavior: traders did crossword puzzles when it was quiet, ate pizza in the pit, smoked on the floor, and occasionally threw punches.

The BookA blackboard ledger — buyers on one side, sellers on the other.
Open OutcryTraders shout and hand-signal bids across a crowded pit.
Reading the RoomYou could feel demand just from a trader's body language.

The strange magic of the floor was that just by standing there, you developed a "feel" for the market — you'd learn that a certain trader was always a big buyer, or that someone's nervous energy meant a huge order was coming. It was pure, sweaty, human instinct. One former floor member compares the vulnerability of it to something almost violent: every day you walked in knowing someone could take everything from you before you could react.

This all mattered because being a member of an exchange — literally owning a seat — was the only way to trade directly. It was a club. And like any club with a scarce membership, those seats eventually became worth a fortune. One trader bought his seat for $56,000 in the 1970s. He sold it in 2006 for $11.5 million, right before exchanges went fully electronic and those seats became worthless overnight. Four hundred floor members hit that same lottery, all at once — a story, he says, that's never really been told.

Chapter Three

Birth of a market: how oil futures began

Here's something most of us never think about: for most of the 20th century, nobody actually "discovered" the price of oil through supply and demand. A handful of oil-producing countries and oil companies would essentially meet in a room and announce it. "The price of crude is now eight." "Now it's ten." That was the whole system.

Michael Marx, a young floor trader who later became chairman of the New York exchange, thought that was absurd for a product the entire world depended on. He wanted to price oil the way corn or cattle were priced — in an open market, through free bidding. People told him it would never work; the big oil companies would crush it.

Before 1974A small group decided the global oil price behind closed doors.
1979Political upheaval in Iran disrupts oil supply — creating real risk.
TodayMillions of traders worldwide set the oil price through speculation.

💡 Why does a market need risk to exist?

A futures market only makes sense if there's genuine uncertainty to bet on. As Marx puts it: "If there's no risk, there's no reason for a futures market." When political unrest disrupted oil supply in 1979, nobody knew what tomorrow's price would be — and that uncertainty is exactly what gave his new oil futures market a reason to exist.

So Marx started a market for oil futures — essentially, bets on where the price of crude would go. From that point on, the global price of oil stopped being decided in a back room and started being decided by speculation, on a trading floor, in real time. It's a strange thought: something as physical and essential as oil is priced today largely by people who will never see a single barrel of it.

Chapter Four

Never waste a disaster

Here is the idea this whole world runs on, stated plainly by an oil trader: "Volatility is what we need. Volatility is our friend. When things are calm, we can't make too much money."

💡 What actually is "volatility"?

Volatility just means how much a price swings around — up and down — over a short period. Calm markets have low volatility (prices barely move). A crisis causes high volatility (prices swing wildly). Big swings mean big chances to profit — or to lose everything — which is why traders are constantly watching for it.

This is why a commodity trader's worst nightmare, according to the documentary's opening, is waking up to a headline that says "Peace declared." Calm is bad for business. Chaos — the Russian currency crisis, the London and Madrid train bombings, and above all, 9/11 — creates the price swings that let a "savvy trader" make real money, because prices "go haywire" instead of sitting still.

Planet Finance has a real tool for measuring this: the VIX, often called the "fear index." The higher it climbs, the more turbulence traders expect — and the more opportunity they see.

One trader who lived through 9/11 on the floor describes banks of phones ringing off the hook, people screaming "what's going on?" — even while they could see it happening in front of them. His conclusion is oddly practical: asking why something is happening is, in his words, "the weakest question in the hierarchy of questions" an investor can ask. While you're wondering why, the market is still moving. You have to react to what is happening, not chase an explanation for it. He calls himself, simply, "a what person."

"So when there's dislocation in the markets — which can come internally or externally — these are the times to make money. It's like never waste a disaster, almost. And I don't want that to be taken the wrong way."— A forex strategist, reflecting on trading through crisis

⚠️ An uncomfortable honesty

Several traders in this world are visibly uneasy about this logic. One says it plainly: "I don't want to say capitalize on a humanitarian crisis." The tension is real — the market doesn't feel emotion, but the people working inside it clearly do.

Chapter Five

From floor to fiber optic

Around the turn of the millennium, the shouting pits went silent. Exchanges "floated" themselves — turning from member-owned clubs into public companies — and trading moved onto computers. The physical trading floor, for the most part, no longer exists. Trades now happen inside data centers: rows of humming, air-conditioned computers in unglamorous industrial parks, chosen specifically because they're far from earthquakes, floods, and terrorist targets.

💡 What's a "matching engine"?

It's simply a computer that takes buy orders and sell orders and pairs them up. That's the modern, electronic version of the old blackboard "book." No matching engine, no trade — it's the literal heart of every electronic exchange.

And now speed itself has become the product. A physicist who builds ultra-fast data networks explains it simply: nobody cares about the absolute speed of a trade — only the relative speed. You don't need to arrive fast. You need to arrive first. Even a single millisecond of advantage, repeated many times a day, adds up to a fortune. That's why companies now spend hundreds of millions of euros building microwave towers across mountaintops — because a radio signal travels through open air faster than light bends through fiber-optic cable.

Here's the part that should surprise you: what actually travels down these multi-million-dollar cables is almost insultingly simple. As the network's designer puts it, it's really just "did the market go up, or did the market go down?" That's the entire message. The complexity isn't in the information — it's in getting that tiny, simple fact to the right computer a few microseconds before anyone else does.

Chapter Six

The hardest game on the planet

A neuropsychologist who coaches professional traders — many of them managing enormous sums of other people's money — describes trading as, quite literally, "the hardest mental game on the planet." Unlike a hand of poker, no hand ever really ends. Nothing is certain. Everything is ambiguous. It can change at any moment.

She has clients earning huge sums who still call her in genuine distress because they "only" made money 22 of the last 27 days. One trader wanted his wife to join a session, just so she could understand why he came home from an 18-hour day so stressed that it was affecting their kids. The job, she explains, is not to predict the future perfectly — nobody can — but to assess what you think you know, while staying aware of everything you don't. Even the best traders eventually get humbled; the market, in her words, exists partly "to make you humble."

💡 Why do emotions matter so much here?

Because every trade forces you to act under real uncertainty, with real money, right now. Fear, greed, and overconfidence aren't distractions from the job — recognizing and managing them is the job. A trader who can't tolerate risk, or who chases losses out of anger, tends not to last long.

This emotional cycle isn't unique to professionals — later in this piece, we'll watch an ordinary first-time trader in Japan go through the exact same rollercoaster in real time. Here's the pattern the documentary traces, from a person's very first trade to their very last decision of the day:

The psychologist's job, in the end, is to help her clients notice which stage they're in before it drives a bad decision — because on Planet Finance, the door out of a losing position can slam shut far faster than the door in ever opened.

Chapter Seven

What a futures contract actually is

Before we get to the day oil prices went negative, we need one piece of plumbing: what is a futures contract, really?

💡 Futures contract, explained with a cattle farmer

Imagine you raise cattle. Between now and when your bull is ready to sell, its price could rise or fall for reasons totally outside your control. So you sign a futures contract: an agreement to sell your bull at an agreed price, on an agreed future date, no matter what happens to the market in between. You've just traded away the uncertainty. The buyer, meanwhile, is betting they know where the price is headed — and can resell that contract to someone else before the actual delivery date arrives.

Futures were invented for exactly this reason: to let farmers, oil producers, and other real businesses lock in a price and manage risk. But here's the twist that matters most for everything that follows: almost nobody trading oil futures actually wants the oil. One veteran trader puts it bluntly — 98% or more of people buying or selling an oil contract have zero desire to ever actually take delivery of a single barrel. They don't want it, and they're not going to get it. They just want to be out of the position before the contract expires and delivery becomes mandatory.

Who Actually Wants the Oil?
everyone trading the contract
close out before expiry
forced to take delivery

98% roll their contracts over or sell before expiry. Only a tiny sliver ever ends up owning the actual oil — and it always gets delivered to one strange little town.

That town is Cushing, Oklahoma — the contractual delivery point for U.S. crude oil, chosen decades ago simply because it already had pipelines and storage tanks. It's an unremarkable place: rusty old tanks controlled by a handful of companies, connected by pipelines, with almost nothing else there. And yet virtually every price you see quoted for oil worldwide traces back to what's traded for delivery at Cushing. One journalist calls it flatly "the wrong place to base the entirety of pricing for global oil" — but it's the place we've got.

Chapter Eight

The day oil went negative

Now for the strangest single event in this whole story. In early 2020, the world went into COVID lockdown. Planes stopped flying, cars stopped driving — but oil wells can't just be switched off like a tap. Oil kept coming out of the ground with nowhere to go. Tankers full of unsellable crude began floating at sea, sometimes for a year or more, simply because there was no space left to store it on land.

💡 What's "contango," and why does storage matter so much?

When storage runs out, holding physical oil becomes a cost rather than an asset — you're paying every single day just to keep it somewhere. As one trader explains, if storing a barrel costs you $20 a day and nobody's buying, you might happily pay someone $18 just to take it off your hands, because that still saves you $2. This dynamic is what eventually pushed the price of oil futures below zero.

Enter Sed Sha, a young Canadian day trader who'd mostly traded Bitcoin. On his uncle's tip, he bought oil futures contracts as the price crashed — first at $5, then $2, eventually buying 212 contracts (212,000 barrels) at just one cent a barrel. He figured the price couldn't possibly go below zero. Nobody had ever seen that happen in the history of any exchange-traded contract, anywhere, ever.

A Black Swan Event

A black swan is a disruptive event nobody saw coming — impossible to prepare for, but hugely impactful once it strikes. Negative oil prices were exactly that: a "first time in history" moment that broke assumptions everyone had taken for granted.

On April 20th, 2020, with minutes left before the market closed, the price of West Texas Intermediate crude fell off a cliff — down through zero, eventually bottoming out at negative $37.63 a barrel. One trader recalls the White House itself calling to ask what on earth was happening and what they should tell the President.

Sed's broker's software couldn't even display a negative price — nobody had ever coded for that possibility. He found out at midnight that his joint family trading account was suddenly $13 million (about $7 million USD) underwater. His father, in his sixties and working since age 13, faced the possibility of losing everything he'd ever built, along with his son and brother, in a single afternoon. "It's like a disaster, a catastrophe," his father says. "My future generations will not be able to pay that amount even if they work day and night."

Chapter Nine

Someone always wins

Futures trading is what's called a zero-sum game: every dollar someone loses is a dollar someone else gains. So if Sed Sha and thousands like him lost fortunes that day, who was on the other side of those trades?

💡 Quick definition: going short

Most investing is "going long" — buying something, hoping it rises in value. "Going short" is the mirror image: you sell first, betting the price will fall, so you can buy it back cheaper later and pocket the difference. On the day oil went negative, the people who were short oil made extraordinary money as the price collapsed.

The big winners were energy giants like Shell — companies that own the entire chain from oil well to storage tank, giving them the financial muscle to simply sit out a bad pricing period while their own trading desks quietly profited on the downside. One trading investigator also points to a group of retail traders in Essex, England, who apparently made the biggest trade of their lives that day, aggressively selling into the falling price. And tick-by-tick data from the exchange shows something suspicious: someone testing the market with a single contract at zero, then 10, then 20, deliberately pushing the price into negative territory. Two years later, a firm called Vega Capital London was taken to court by traders who claimed the crash was intentionally engineered — a case that, as of this telling, remains unresolved.

"Somebody has to lose for everybody that wins. Every dollar that's made is a dollar that's lost."— A veteran oil trader

As for Sed Sha's family — they eventually were compensated by their broker, whose faulty software had failed to show negative prices in time, though they still absorbed a real loss of around $100,000. Sed also developed high blood pressure from the ongoing stress, a reminder that behind every "someone wins, someone loses" headline is a very real human body under real strain.

Chapter Ten

Betting on disaster: catastrophe bonds

If futures let you bet on the price of oil, what if you could bet on something even bigger — the odds of an actual disaster happening? That market exists too, and it was effectively pioneered by John Seo, a man who got into finance for the most unglamorous reason imaginable: he needed health insurance.

💡 What is a catastrophe ("cat") bond?

It works like insurance, flipped around. An investor lends money to, say, a city's transit authority. If no disaster happens before the bond matures, the investor gets their money back plus attractive interest. But if a defined disaster does strike — say, floodwaters reaching a specific height — the investor loses their entire principal, and that money is used to pay for the damage instead.

The case that put cat bonds on the map: after Hurricane Sandy flooded New York's subway tunnels in 2012, causing around $800 million in damage, the city's Transportation Authority discovered that traditional insurance premiums had more than doubled and still didn't cover the full risk. So they issued a cat bond instead.

Case Study · NYC Transit's Cat Bond

The bond only pays out if water in a specific tunnel hits a precise trigger height — for instance, 7.75 feet in one zone — and the storm carries an official name. So far, no storm since Sandy has come close. Investors haven't lost a cent, and the city has a guaranteed pool of money waiting the moment the water does rise that high. Engineers even use the exact trigger height to design physical flood barriers and know precisely how tall to build the sandbags.

7.75 ftexample trigger level
0payouts triggered so far

Why would any investor want an asset this risky? Because it's what's called non-correlated — a hurricane in Florida has nothing to do with whether the stock market crashes tomorrow. For a pension fund or hedge fund trying to diversify, an asset that moves independently of everything else is genuinely valuable, even if it's tied to something as grim as a wildfire or earthquake. John Seo's firm calculates the odds of disaster using not the "bell curve" that traditional finance loves — which is built for predicting the most likely outcomes — but models built specifically for rare, extreme "tail risk" events that ordinary finance is bad at handling.

Chapter Eleven

When the fire came for Bonny Dune

To see what these algorithms mean on the ground, the documentary visits Bonny Dune, a tight-knit rural community on the American West Coast. In August 2020, roughly 11,000 lightning strikes hit the region in a single night, sparking what became the Santa Cruz Lightning Complex fires. Almost everyone evacuated. One elder, John, stayed behind — determined to defend his home and help hold the line with bulldozers, cutting a firebreak between the flames and the community.

Meanwhile, hundreds of miles away, John Seo's team was doing something very different: calculating, in real time, what this specific fire would likely cost — running the numbers on burn ratios, property counts, and historical fire perimeters to figure out whether any of their catastrophe bonds might be triggered.

A 1.18% annual risk sounds tiny — but the model places this location in the riskiest 5% of all of California, based on distance to wildland, drought severity, and historical burn data.

The fire department, stretched thin across nine simultaneous fires, never made it to Bonny Dune. For many residents, this was the first time they truly understood something painful: as one puts it, they realized they were "not significant" and were "deemed easily disposable" in a numbers game weighing community wealth against the cost of protecting it. Catastrophe bonds, it turns out, only work for you if you own insurable property in the first place — for everyone else, protection means building your own bunker, quite literally, and hoping the wind blows the fire in another direction.

🔥 One man's real "insurance policy"

John built a small backyard bunker stocked with compressed air tanks, enough to survive inside for hours if the fire swept directly over his property. It's not a metaphor — for people outside the reach of Wall Street's models, resilience has to be physical, not financial.

Chapter Twelve

Mrs. Watanabe and the rise of retail trading

In Japan, there's a nickname used across trading floors from London to New York: "Mrs. Watanabe." It refers to the roughly 1.5 million Japanese retail investors — many of them housewives — who began speculating on foreign exchange (forex) in the 1990s, after Japan's economic bubble burst and savings accounts started paying essentially 0% interest.

💡 What is the forex market?

Forex (foreign exchange) is simply the buying and selling of one country's currency for another — betting that, say, the Japanese yen will rise in value against the US dollar. It's the largest financial market on Earth: more money changes hands here in a single day than the entire Japanese economy produces in a year.

The documentary follows one such trader — a jewelry designer trying to make ends meet — through the emotional cycle we mapped back in Chapter Six: optimism, excitement, euphoria, then real fear. On March 11, 2011, everything changed in an instant. A massive earthquake and tsunami struck Japan's east coast, causing a national catastrophe. On Planet Finance, this was a textbook black swan — and it triggered a bizarre currency spike. Japanese companies, needing to pay for domestic disaster costs, began selling foreign assets and buying yen back home, and the yen's value shot up so fast that some brokers' trading software crashed entirely, automatically liquidating retail positions at huge losses.

"This was a truly uncertain situation. I mean, the whole time it could have gone down. It was really quite scary."— A forex strategist, on trading through the 2011 Japan earthquake

The rescue came, ironically, from the world's biggest financial institutions. The G7 central banks coordinated an intervention to stabilize the yen — and the moment that headline hit trading floors in New York, the exchange rate reversed within ten seconds. Big hedge funds and banks had a direct phone line to the Federal Reserve, ready for exactly that moment. Mrs. Watanabe never got that call. This, in miniature, is the whole story of Planet Finance: the biggest players get the fastest information, and everyone else finds out from a headline.

Chapter Thirteen

Jack Ma and the biggest IPO ever

In 2014, Chinese entrepreneur Jack Ma decided to take his e-commerce giant Alibaba public — and, notably, chose to list it not in China, but on the New York Stock Exchange.

💡 What is an IPO?

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is the first time a private company sells shares of itself to the public. Before the IPO, only insiders and early investors own the company. After it, anyone can buy a small slice of ownership on the stock market — and if demand is high, the price (and the founder's paper wealth) can rocket upward on day one.

The demand for Alibaba shares was so overwhelming that traders describe people "jumping over each other" to get in. Investors saw it as their ticket into the fast-growing Chinese economy — often called "the Chinese miracle" — through a company frequently compared to Amazon. When trading opened, the share price jumped immediately, and it became, at the time, the largest IPO in history.

This IPO was set to be even bigger than Alibaba's — the largest ever.

Chapter Fourteen

When Beijing said no

This time, Jack Ma planned a dual listing: in Hong Kong, to capture international capital, and simultaneously in Shanghai, to tap into China's new, hungry middle class. Demand was staggering on both sides.

Ordinary people piled in too — including a Hong Kong taxi driver hoping to win an allocation of 50,000 shares, worth roughly half a million dollars, largely funded on borrowed margin. It was, for many, a shot at real financial transformation.

Then Jack Ma gave a speech to China's financial elite, publicly criticizing Beijing's regulators for holding back innovation and stifling a healthy financial market — remarkably blunt words for anyone to say about the Chinese government in public. Just two days before the scheduled listing, with no warning, Beijing pulled the plug entirely. It remains the largest IPO ever cancelled at such a late stage in history.

"If something seems too good to be true, it usually is too good to be true — especially on Planet Finance."

Jack Ma was summoned to Beijing and largely disappeared from public view for months. Ant Group was later forced to restructure, hand over user data to a state-owned agency, and comply with strict banking-style regulations. The taxi driver eventually got his interest payments reimbursed, but his dream of a life-changing windfall vanished. The lesson, as the documentary frames it: China ultimately decided it — not Planet Finance — sets the rules of the game within its own borders.

Chapter Fifteen

The vultures: short sellers who hunt liars

Not everyone on Planet Finance is betting that things will go up. Short sellers make their living finding companies that are lying, and betting their share price will collapse. One such investigator describes himself frankly: "I'm not interested in tearing down a company with good prospects. I'm interested in finding companies that are lying about their good prospects."

💡 Short selling, worked through with real numbers

Say a share trades at €100. A short seller borrows that share from someone who owns it (like a pension fund) and immediately sells it on the open market. If the price then drops to €10 — because the fraud gets exposed — the short seller buys it back for €10, returns the borrowed share, and pockets the €90 difference. If the price rises instead, though, the losses are, in theory, unlimited.

In the late 1990s, Belgium's proudest tech company was Lernout & Hauspie — a speech-recognition firm so promising that even Microsoft invested. One in six adults in Flanders owned its shares, drawn in as much by national pride as by profit. An Australian short seller, working from the other side of the world, calculated that the company's finances didn't add up — roughly 100 million dollars was simply missing. He bet around $30,000 that the company would fail. He was right: the fraud was exposed, the share price collapsed to zero, and its founders were eventually convicted and ordered to pay over $650 million in damages they'll likely never fully repay.

A similar story, but far more dangerous, played out with Germany's fintech star Wirecard. Two separate teams of short sellers — one in the UK, one in Sydney — independently discovered the company was processing suspicious "high-risk" payments and appeared to be a front for illicit money flows, possibly linked to gambling operations and, eventually, the Russian state. When they published their findings, Wirecard didn't just deny it — the company allegedly hired former intelligence operatives to surveil the journalists and short sellers involved, following them, filming them, and frightening their families badly enough that police put emergency response on their home phone lines.

Being right didn't mean being paid — for years, the stock kept rising as German regulators, major shareholders, and even the Chancellor publicly defended Wirecard, and billions in German pension money kept flowing in. The short sellers were nearly squeezed out of their positions before the truth finally caught up: Wirecard collapsed entirely, and the case remains one of Europe's largest corporate frauds.

💡 What's a "short squeeze"?

If a stock a short seller is betting against keeps rising instead of falling, they eventually have to buy the shares back at a painful loss just to close the position — driving the price up even further as more short sellers rush to exit at once. It's the mechanism that turned the next chapter's story into a genuine phenomenon.

Chapter Sixteen

GameStop: when the crowd fought back

During the pandemic, millions of newly housebound, newly bored people opened trading apps for the first time. Many gathered on an online forum called Wall Street Bets, where a shared grievance was building: a dislike of big hedge funds that had bet heavily against — "shorted" — a struggling video-game retailer called GameStop.

The plan the crowd landed on was almost mischievously simple: if enough retail traders bought GameStop shares and refused to sell — "held the line" — the price would climb. And because hedge funds were short the stock, a rising price meant they'd eventually be forced into a short squeeze, buying back shares at ever-higher prices just to escape the pain, pushing the price up even further.

Case Study · GameStop, January 2021

Wall Street Bets had swelled to roughly 13 million members. As retail buying pressure mounted, several major hedge funds with large short positions suffered billions in losses, and at least one required emergency financial rescue. Commentators called it a David-versus-Goliath moment — ordinary traders, organizing through social media, briefly overpowering some of the most sophisticated players in the game.

For many participants, this wasn't really about GameStop's DVDs or its outdated business model — it was about a generation that grew up in the shadow of the 2008 financial crisis, watching the gap between rich and poor widen without ever seeing the people responsible held accountable. Finally, they had a way to hit back at "the big guys" using the market's own mechanisms.

But the ending is more complicated than a clean victory. Many retail traders, including some featured in the documentary, eventually sold their shares and took profits — showing less unified "solidarity" than the movement's rhetoric suggested. As one veteran short seller wryly observes about crowds like this: "If you're playing poker with a whole lot of rich people, and you look around and you don't know who the patsy is, then you're probably the patsy." Some in the crowd made real money. Many others, arriving late to the hype, likely didn't.

"It was a demonstration of the mania of the crowd at the moment, and how easy it was, using social media, to whip the crowd into a frenzy."— A veteran short seller, reflecting on GameStop

Chapter Seventeen

What Planet Finance taught me.

Every trader in this world eventually says some version of the same thing: the strongest question you can ask isn't "why" — it's "what." Why comes later, if it comes at all. What is happening right now is what you have to react to.

Planet Finance turned out to be less about cold numbers than about raw human emotion — fear and desire, expressed as a line on a graph. And its most valuable commodity isn't oil, or gold, or even information. It's risk itself, a product that can be packaged, priced, and traded, whether it comes from a hurricane, a pandemic, or a war. As one trader admits, almost sheepishly: it's a bit like never wasting a disaster — an idea nobody quite wants to say out loud, and yet nobody in this world denies.

— That's the tour. Stay curious, and watch your downside.
