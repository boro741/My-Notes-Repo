1AWS Budget & Billing Setup

Picture this: you launch a test server on Friday, forget about it over the weekend, and open your AWS bill on Monday to find charges you didn't expect. This happens to real teams, not just beginners. AWS uses a pay-as-you-go model — you're only charged for what you actually use. But that cuts both ways: if you leave something running, you keep paying for it, whether you're looking at it or not.

Getting to your bill takes two clicks: click your account name in the top-right corner of the AWS Console, then click Billing and Cost Management.

💡 New word IAM stands for Identity and Access Management. It's the AWS system that decides who can log in and what they're allowed to touch. By default, that includes deciding who is even allowed to see the bill.
Gotcha: a regular IAM user cannot view billing information by default — even one with full admin permissions everywhere else. The Root user has to turn this on first: log in as Root, open Account Settings, find "IAM user and role access to billing information," check Activate, and save.
The Root account grants billing permission once; after that, authorized IAM users can view billing details.
🏭 In real companies Real teams treat the Root login the same way here as everywhere else in AWS: it exists to flip account-level switches like this one, and nothing more. Nobody uses it for daily technical work — that all happens through IAM users or roles instead.

Reading Your Billing Dashboard

Three numbers matter most here. Month-to-date cost is what you've actually spent so far this month. Forecasted cost is AWS's best guess at your final bill, based on how you've been using things lately. Last month's cost is exactly what it sounds like — last month's final total, already settled.

Monitoring Charges & the Free Tier

Under Bills → Charges by Service, AWS breaks your bill down clearly — EC2 servers cost this much, EBS storage drives cost that much, NAT (Network Address Translation) Gateways, which route private traffic out to the internet, cost this much. That breakdown is the first place to look if a bill feels higher than expected.

💡 New word Free Tier — a set amount of AWS usage every month that costs nothing, meant for learning and small projects. As an example, you get 750 free hours a month on a t2.micro server — enough to run one small instance non-stop for the entire month. The Free Tier dashboard tracks your usage against these limits, and turns the bar red once you're close to going over.
🏭 In real companies "Charges by Service" tells you EC2 cost this much and storage cost that much — useful, but not enough once a company runs dozens of projects in one account. So most teams add cost allocation tags to every server, things like team=payments or env=production. AWS then lets finance break the bill down by tag, so nobody has to guess which team's servers are actually driving the cost.

Setting Up Budget Alerts

Two simple alerts catch almost every accidental bill. The first fires the instant any charge at all appears — useful for catching a mistake on day one. The second tracks your total monthly spend and warns you at 85% and 100% of a limit you set, like $10.

Two budget safety nets — one flags accidental initial charges, while the second tracks total monthly spend.

After every hands-on lab, run through the same short checklist:

  1. Check the Free Tier page to confirm you're still within your usage limits.
  2. Open Bills → Charges by Service to verify there are no unexpected charges.
  3. Confirm your budget email alerts are still active.
  4. Terminate — meaning permanently delete — any lab server you no longer need.

2What is EC2?

Suppose you need a computer to run your app, but you don't want to buy hardware, plug in cables, or sit in a data center yourself. Real companies solve this by renting computers instead of owning them. That's exactly what EC2 gives you.

💡 New word EC2 stands for Elastic Compute Cloud. It's AWS's main service for renting virtual computers, called instances, that live inside Amazon's own data centers.
💡 New word IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) means AWS hands you the raw computer — its CPU (Central Processing Unit), its RAM (Random Access Memory), storage, and network — and you decide what runs on top of it: your operating system, your software, your configuration. EC2 is AWS's core IaaS offering. You're in charge of what's inside the box.

Real teams treat an EC2 instance the same way whether it runs for ten minutes or ten months: you choose the size, you're billed while it's running, and you shut it down the moment you're done. A team debugging a one-off data problem might spin up a large instance for twenty minutes and pay pennies. A company's main database might run the same instance size nonstop for years.

The EC2 Ecosystem

The EC2 ecosystem: the virtual server (Instance) works together with EBS (hard drive), ELB (traffic balancer), ASG (auto-scaler), and AMI (operating system template).
💡 New word EBS (Elastic Block Store) is a virtual hard drive that attaches to an instance. Unlike memory, an EBS drive keeps its data even if the instance stops or restarts.
💡 New word AMI (Amazon Machine Image) is a saved template — an operating system plus whatever software was already installed on it — that AWS uses to create a new instance. You can pick Amazon Linux, Ubuntu, Windows, or a custom image your own team built.

The diagram above shows that EC2 usually isn't alone in a real setup. EBS gives it a persistent hard drive. An Elastic Load Balancer (ELB) spreads incoming visitors across several instances. An Auto Scaling Group (ASG) adds more instances automatically when traffic climbs, then removes them again once things quiet down. Both get their own deep dive later in the course — for now, just know EC2 sits at the center of a bigger picture, not off on its own.

🏭 In real companies In 2023, Amazon's own Prime Video engineering team published a widely-read post about redesigning one of their internal monitoring tools. It had originally been built from many small serverless pieces. Once that tool had to handle a lot of steady, predictable traffic, moving it to a simpler application running on EC2 and ECS instead cut its infrastructure cost by around 90%. The lesson wasn't "serverless is bad" — it was that renting steady EC2 capacity can beat pay-per-request pricing once a workload is large and predictable. That's the exact same tradeoff you'll see again in the pricing section below.

3What Can You Customize on an Instance?

When you launch an instance, AWS doesn't just hand you a generic box. It asks you to choose almost everything about it, the same way you'd configure a real server before buying one — from its operating system down to which network traffic it's allowed to receive.

SettingDescription
Operating System (OS)Linux (Amazon Linux, Ubuntu), Windows, or macOS.
CPU / Compute PowerNumber of virtual processor cores (from 1 core up to 96+ cores).
RAM / MemorySystem memory capacity (from 0.5 GB up to several Terabytes).
Storage DrivesNetwork storage (EBS) or high-speed physical disk (Instance Store).
Network SpeedNetwork interface card speed and bandwidth.
Public IP AddressAllows your instance to send and receive traffic over the internet.
Security GroupA virtual firewall controlling allowed inbound and outbound network traffic.
User Data ScriptA setup script that runs automatically when the instance boots for the first time.
💡 New word Instance type — AWS's name for a specific bundle of CPU, memory, and network performance, like t2.micro or m5.large. Instead of picking CPU and RAM separately, you usually just pick a type that already matches the combination you need.
💡 New word Security Group — a virtual firewall attached to your instance. It's a list of rules stating which traffic is allowed in (inbound) and which is allowed out (outbound). Everything not on the list is blocked automatically.
💡 New word VPC (Virtual Private Cloud) is your own private, isolated slice of AWS's network — every EC2 instance lives inside one. A NACL (Network Access Control List) is a second firewall that sits at the edge of an entire VPC, checking traffic before it even reaches an individual instance's Security Group.
🏭 In real companies A typical production setup layers both firewalls. The VPC is split into public and private sections. Web servers that need to be reachable sit in the public part, behind a Security Group open only on ports 80 and 443. Database servers sit in a private part with no direct path to the internet at all, and their Security Group usually only allows traffic coming from the web servers' own Security Group — not from any IP (Internet Protocol) address at all. NACLs add a coarser, VPC-wide layer on top of that, mainly used to explicitly block a known-bad IP range across every instance at once.

4Bootstrapping with EC2 User Data

Imagine launching fifty identical web servers by hand — installing the same software and running the same setup commands on every single one. Nobody does that in practice. Real teams automate it with something called a User Data script.

💡 New word Bootstrapping means running setup commands automatically the first time a computer starts. On EC2, you do this with a User Data script — a small script you paste in while launching the instance. AWS runs it exactly once, the very first time the instance boots, and never again on a normal restart.
User Data scripts execute once during initial server startup with full administrator privileges.

Common Uses of User Data Scripts

  • Installing operating system updates and patches.
  • Installing web server software (like Apache or Nginx) or a database application.
  • Downloading application code and config files automatically.
  • Starting software services automatically the moment the server boots.
Gotcha: a User Data script runs with root privileges automatically. You never need to type sudo before your commands — the script already has full administrator rights.
🏭 In real companies A short User Data script is fine for a demo or a small project. Once a company has dozens of servers that all need to be set up identically and reliably, most move to something sturdier — baking a custom AMI that already has the software installed, or using a configuration tool like Ansible or Chef to manage servers after they boot. User Data scripts tend to survive for one job in that world: pulling down the latest application code or config at startup, while the heavier lifting moves elsewhere.

5EC2 Pricing Models

The exact same EC2 instance can cost very different amounts, depending only on how you agree to pay for it. AWS offers six different ways to pay, and picking the wrong one is one of the easiest ways to accidentally overspend — or to skip a guarantee you actually needed.

Comparing EC2 pricing discounts against On-Demand rates. Larger discounts require longer commitments or accepting interruptions.

1. On-Demand

On-Demand is the default: pay by the second or hour, with zero commitment. You can start an instance now and stop it in ten minutes with no penalty at all. This is the right choice for development, testing, learning, or anything where you're not yet sure how long you'll need it.

2. Reserved Instances (RI)

Reserved Instances flip that trade: commit to running an instance for a full 1-year or 3-year term, and AWS cuts the price by up to 72%. That only makes sense once you already know you'll need the capacity the whole time — a core production database running nonstop is the classic example.

3. Savings Plans

Savings Plans work a little differently. Instead of committing to one specific instance, you commit to spending a certain dollar amount per hour — say $10/hour — for 1 or 3 years. AWS gives you up to a 70% discount, and in return, you can move that commitment across different instance families, operating systems, or Regions as your needs shift.

4. Spot Instances

💡 New word Spot Instance — spare AWS computing capacity that nobody else is currently using, offered to you at up to a 90% discount. The catch: AWS can take it back with only a two-minute warning, whenever that capacity is needed elsewhere.
Gotcha: never put a database or a customer-facing web server on a Spot Instance. Losing it with two minutes' notice is fine for a batch job. It's not fine for something people are actively using right now.
🏭 In real companies Netflix has publicly talked for years about running its video encoding jobs — turning raw video files into the different formats and quality levels you actually stream — on Spot Instances. Encoding is exactly the kind of workload Spot is built for: it's broken into many independent chunks, and if AWS reclaims one instance mid-job, that one chunk just gets retried on another instance. The savings compound fast at the scale Netflix operates.

5. Dedicated Hosts

Dedicated Hosts give you a physical server that no other AWS customer shares. They're mainly used for strict compliance requirements or for software with licensing rules tied to a physical processor core.

6. Capacity Reservations

Capacity Reservations don't discount anything — you still pay standard On-Demand rates. What they buy you is a guarantee: AWS promises that capacity will be sitting there, ready, in a specific Availability Zone when you need it.

🏭 In real companies Most real AWS bills mix several of these models on purpose. A common, AWS-recommended pattern looks like this: On-Demand for a new project until its traffic pattern is understood, Reserved Instances or Savings Plans for the steady baseline load once it's predictable, and Spot for anything that can tolerate interruption, like batch jobs or CI (Continuous Integration) pipelines. Picking just one model for an entire company's fleet almost always leaves money on the table somewhere.

Choosing the Right Pricing Plan

Workload TypeRecommended PlanRationale

6Hands-On: Launch Your First EC2 Instance

Here's the whole process end to end: launch an Amazon Linux server, lock it down with a Security Group, and use a User Data script so it's already running a web page the moment it boots.

  1. Open the EC2 Console and click Launch Instance.
  2. Give it a name, like My First Web Server.
  3. Pick an AMI: Amazon Linux 2 is Free Tier eligible.
  4. Pick an instance type: t2.micro (1 vCPU — virtual CPU — and 1 GB, gigabyte, of RAM) is also Free Tier eligible.
  5. Create a new Key Pair, e.g. my-key.pem, and download it. You'll need this file to log in later, and AWS will not let you download it a second time.
  6. Set up the Security Group with the inbound rules in the table below.
  7. Open Advanced Details and paste in the User Data script below.
  8. Click Launch Instance.
💡 New word Key Pair — two matching cryptographic keys. AWS keeps the public one on your instance, and you keep the private one (the .pem file) on your own computer. Together, they let you log in securely without ever typing a password over the network.

The table below opens three ports. SSH (Secure Shell, covered fully in the last section of this page) is for terminal access. HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) is for regular web traffic. HTTPS (HTTP Secure) is for encrypted web traffic. All three run over TCP (Transmission Control Protocol), the standard reliable method AWS uses for this kind of traffic.

Traffic TypeProtocolPortSourcePurpose
SSHTCP22Your IP (or 0.0.0.0/0 for practice)Terminal command access.
HTTPTCP800.0.0.0/0 (Anywhere)Public web traffic.
HTTPSTCP4430.0.0.0/0 (Anywhere)Encrypted secure web traffic.
Gotcha: opening port 22 to 0.0.0.0/0 (meaning "anywhere on the internet") is fine for a two-minute lab, but it's a bad habit for anything real. In production, SSH access is usually locked down to a specific office or VPN IP range, never the entire internet.

User Data Bootstrap Script

#!/bin/bash
yum update -y
yum install -y httpd
systemctl start httpd
systemctl enable httpd
echo "<h1>My First AWS Web Server!</h1>" > /var/www/html/index.html

Each line does one simple job. yum update -y updates the installed packages. yum install -y httpd installs the Apache web server software. systemctl start httpd starts it immediately. systemctl enable httpd makes sure Apache starts again automatically if the server ever reboots.

7Instance Lifecycle: Stop vs Terminate

Two buttons look similar in the console — Stop and Terminate — but mixing them up matters. One pauses your bill. The other deletes your server for good.

Stopping a server is reversible (like shutting down your laptop). Terminating a server is permanent deletion.
Server StateDescriptionBilling Status
RunningServer is online and active.✅ Billed for compute hours.
StoppedServer is powered off. Computer state is preserved on disk.❌ No compute charge (EBS storage drive is still billed).
TerminatedServer and attached root disk are permanently deleted.❌ All billing stops completely.
🏭 A common real mistake Stopping an instance feels like it should stop all charges, but it doesn't quite. Compute charges stop, but the EBS storage drive attached to it keeps costing money every month, until you either delete it or terminate the instance. Teams that stop dozens of unused test servers "to save money," then forget about them for a year, often find a pile of orphaned EBS volumes quietly adding up on the bill.
Lab habit: always terminate practice servers when you're done with them, not just stop them. Termination is the only state that guarantees zero ongoing charges.

8Connecting via SSH

Once your server is running, you need an actual way to get inside it and run commands. For a Linux EC2 instance, that's SSH.

💡 New word SSH (Secure Shell) is a secure way to log into a remote computer's command line over the network. It proves who you are using your Key Pair's private key, instead of a password. 
# Set key file permissions (macOS / Linux terminal):
chmod 400 /path/to/my-key.pem

# SSH command syntax:
ssh -i /path/to/my-key.pem ec2-user@YOUR_PUBLIC_IP

The chmod 400 command locks down the permissions on your private key file so only your own user account can read it — SSH actually refuses to connect if the file's permissions are too open. The username depends on which AMI you launched: Amazon Linux uses ec2-user, while Ubuntu uses ubuntu.

🏭 Why this matters in practice Plenty of companies now avoid opening port 22 at all. AWS Systems Manager's Session Manager lets you get a shell on an instance through IAM permissions alone, with no open inbound port and no key file to protect. Plain SSH with a Key Pair is still exactly what the exam expects you to know, and it's still common in practice — just know it's not the only way real teams do this anymore.
Gotcha: AWS only lets you download your private key once, at creation time. Lose that .pem file, and there's no "resend" option — you'd have to create a brand new Key Pair and set it up again.

Key Facts Worth Remembering

  • t2.micro is Free Tier eligible: 750 hours/month, 1 vCPU, 1 GB RAM.
  • User Data runs once, at first boot, with root privileges — never add sudo.
  • Security Groups are stateful firewalls; ports 22 (SSH), 80 (HTTP), and 443 (HTTPS) are the common ones to open.
  • Root EBS volumes are set to delete on termination by default.
  • Stopping pauses compute billing; EBS storage keeps billing. Terminating stops everything and deletes the server.
  • Spot Instances can be reclaimed with only a two-minute warning — never use them for databases or live web servers.
  • On-Demand rewards flexibility. Reserved Instances and Savings Plans reward commitment. Spot rewards tolerance for interruption.

The One-Paragraph Version

EC2 lets you rent a virtual computer instead of buying hardware. You pick its AMI, instance type, storage, and Security Group, and AWS can run a one-time User Data script the moment it boots. How you pay ranges from flexible On-Demand, to discounted Reserved Instances and Savings Plans for steady workloads, to deeply discounted but interruptible Spot Instances for workloads that can handle it. Stopping an instance pauses compute billing; terminating it deletes the instance and its billing for good. And before any of that, turn on billing alerts — AWS only charges for what you use, but "what you use" includes anything you simply forgot to turn off.
