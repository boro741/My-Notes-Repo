Distributed Systems · Event Streaming

Kafka isn't a mailbox. It's a tape recorder nobody erases.

SQS and RabbitMQ hand you a message, and once you've read it, it's gone — like a letter you throw away after opening. Kafka records everything to a tape that keeps running, and lets you (and everyone else) rewind to any point and listen again. That single difference explains almost everything else about it.

01 / First Principles

The core idea: a log, not a queue

Plain English

A queue is like a to-do list on paper — once a task is done, you cross it off and it's gone. A log is like a diary — you only ever add new pages at the end, and old pages never get torn out. Kafka is the diary. SQS and RabbitMQ are the to-do list.

A queue (SQS, RabbitMQ) is built around one promise: deliver each message to a consumer, then remove it. A log (Kafka) is built around a different promise: append every message to the end of an ordered, permanent file, and never touch it again during read. Consumers don't remove anything — they just remember which line number they last read. That line number is called an offset.

Left side loops to show messages disappearing after each read. Right side never loses a block.
Why

Because the log is never mutated by reads, any number of independent consumers can read the same topic at completely different speeds, and any of them can rewind to an earlier offset and reprocess history. A queue fundamentally cannot offer that, because the data is gone after one read.

02 / Motivation

Why Kafka exists when SQS/SNS already did

Plain English

Imagine one radio station (Kafka) versus mailing individual letters to each listener (SQS/SNS). Mailing letters works fine for a few listeners. But once you have thousands of listeners, all wanting to tune in whenever they want and rewind to yesterday's broadcast, you need a station that just keeps broadcasting on a loop — not a mailroom stuffing envelopes.

Kafka was built at LinkedIn around 2011 to solve a problem simple messaging queues weren't designed for: collecting huge, continuous streams of activity data (page views, clicks, metrics) from hundreds of services, at very high throughput, and letting many different teams independently consume and reprocess that same stream for different purposes — analytics, search indexing, monitoring, recommendations — without stepping on each other.

SQS and SNS solve a narrower, equally important problem: decoupling two services reliably, with simple delivery guarantees. They were never designed to be replayed, to guarantee strict per-key ordering, or to sustain millions of events per second at low latency the way Kafka's partitioned log does.

03 / Internals

Anatomy of Kafka

Plain English

A broker is just a computer holding part of the data. A topic is the name of a stream (like "order-events"). A partition is one lane of that stream — topics get split into lanes so the work can be spread across many computers instead of piling onto one.

A Kafka cluster is made of brokers — servers that store data and serve reads/writes. Producers write to a topic, which is really just a name for a set of partitions — and each partition is one append-only log, spread across brokers for scale.

One topic, split into partitions, spread across brokers. This is what lets Kafka scale write throughput horizontally.
TermPlain English
BrokerOne computer in the cluster, holding a slice of the data.
TopicThe name of a stream of events, like a folder label — e.g. "order-events".
PartitionOne lane within that stream, kept in order, stored on one broker (plus copies).
ProducerWhoever is writing new events into the stream.
ConsumerWhoever is reading events, at their own pace, from wherever their bookmark is.
OffsetA consumer's bookmark — "I've read up to here."

05 / Mechanism

Consumer groups & offsets — replay explained

Plain English

Think of the partition as a shelf of numbered boxes that never get thrown away. Two different teams can each have their own sticky note on the shelf saying "I'm up to box 5" or "I'm up to box 2" — and neither team's note affects the other's. One team can even move their sticky note backward to re-read old boxes.

A consumer group is just a named set of consumers that split the partitions of a topic between them, so the group as a whole reads every partition exactly once. Different groups reading the same topic are completely independent of each other — each group has its own offset per partition.

This is the capability a queue cannot offer: two consumers of the same stream, at different points in time, both valid.

06 / Mechanism

What happens when a consumer joins or leaves

Plain English

If three people are splitting a stack of letters to sort, and a fourth person shows up to help, the stack gets re-split so everyone has a fair share. Kafka does the same thing automatically — it's called a rebalance.

The label under partition 1 alternates teal ↔ purple to show ownership shifting during a rebalance.

This is also why offsets are stored durably (in an internal Kafka topic) rather than in a consumer's memory — after a rebalance, the new owner of a partition needs to know exactly where the previous owner left off.

The pattern you were picturing — "SNS notifies, SQS holds the queue" — is real and common in AWS (SNS fans out to several SQS queues, one per subscriber). But that's still queue-based underneath: each subscriber's SQS queue deletes messages once read. Kafka replaces both roles with one mechanism — the partitioned log — because replay and independent consumption are built into reading itself, not bolted on with extra queues.

08 / Comparison

Kafka vs. RabbitMQ

This is the pairing worth being precise about, because RabbitMQ is often assumed to be "Kafka's cousin" — but it's actually much closer to SQS/SNS in behavior.

TraitKafkaRabbitMQ
Core abstractionAppend-only log, consumer pullsTraditional message broker, pushes to consumers
After consumptionMessage stays until retention expiresMessage is removed once acknowledged
Routing logicSimple — by topic and partition keyRich — exchanges support direct, topic, fanout, header-based routing
ThroughputVery high — designed for millions of events/secLower — optimized for flexible routing, not raw throughput
ReplayYesNo
Best fitStreaming pipelines, event sourcing, log aggregationComplex task routing, work queues, RPC-style messaging
Why

RabbitMQ and SQS both delete on consumption — that single trait groups them together as "queues," no matter how different their routing features look on the surface. Kafka's retention-and-replay model is what actually separates it from both.

09 / Pitfalls

Common mistakes

Mistake

Treating Kafka as a simple task queue. If you just need "do this job once, then discard it," Kafka is heavier machinery than you need — SQS is usually the better, simpler fit.

Mistake

Assuming ordering across an entire topic. Kafka only guarantees order within a single partition. If you need strict ordering for a given entity (e.g. one order's events), you must key messages so they always land on the same partition.

Mistake

Too many or too few partitions. Partitions are your unit of parallelism — too few limits throughput and consumer scaling; too many adds overhead and can hurt latency.

Mistake

Forgetting consumer lag. If a consumer group falls behind and retention expires before it catches up, it silently loses data it never got to read. Lag needs to be monitored, not assumed away.

10 / Recall

Summary — what to remember

  • Kafka is a distributed log, not a queue: reading a message never removes it — consumers just move their own offset (bookmark).
  • That single design choice is what enables replay and independent consumer groups reading the same topic at different speeds — something SQS, SNS, and RabbitMQ can't do, because all three delete on consumption.
  • A topic is split into partitions, spread across brokers in a cluster, for parallel writes and reads. Order is only guaranteed within one partition.
  • When consumers join or leave a group, Kafka rebalances partitions between them automatically, using durably-stored offsets so no progress is lost.
  • Kafka exists because SQS/SNS were built for simple, reliable point-to-point or fan-out delivery — not for massive-scale, replayable, ordered event streams with many independent long-term readers.
  • RabbitMQ is architecturally closer to SQS/SNS (delete-on-consume, push-based) than to Kafka — despite the natural instinct to lump Kafka and RabbitMQ together as "message brokers."
  • Watch partition count, key-based ordering, and consumer lag — these are where most real-world Kafka mistakes happen.
