Chapter One

What I noticed about myself

I'm an SDET. My real job is quality — test automation, frameworks, making sure things don't break. But I keep drifting into Developer stuff, then Data Engineering, then DevOps. One video leads to another. One blog leads to a full course.

And here's the honest part: most of it I never use at work. So even after spending a month on a course, it fades away. I end up "knowing about" a lot of things but not being deep in anything.

I also had a suspicion: YouTube is deciding what I learn. It works exactly like product marketing. I didn't need the product, but the ad made me want it. Same with a video titled "Every Engineer MUST know Kafka in 2026" — I didn't need it, but now I feel I do.

The good news

My gut feeling was correct. The research below (Harvard, Kahneman, memory science) says exactly this. So this note is not about being hard on myself — it's about replacing a bad habit with a system that actually works.

Chapter Two

Why watching feels like learning (but isn't)

There's a name for what happens to me: the "illusion of competence". In plain words — the video is so smooth and clear that my brain thinks, "I get it, I know this now." But I don't.

Why? Because the person in the video already did all the hard work. They figured out the confusing parts, fixed the errors, and arranged everything neatly. I'm just watching the final, polished result. It's like watching Virat Kohli bat in highlights — it looks easy, but that's because you're only seeing the end product of years of practice.

The brain's shortcut (Kahneman)

Daniel Kahneman (a famous psychologist) found that when we face a hard question, our brain quietly swaps it with an easier one and answers that instead. So:

The swap that fools me

Hard question: "Can I set this up, debug it, and make it work by myself?"
Easy question my brain answers instead: "Can I follow what this guy is saying?"

The answer to the easy one is "yes", so I feel confident. But nobody asked the hard question.

Recognising vs. Recalling

Two different things happen in the brain:

  • Recognising — "Oh yes, I've seen this before." This is what videos train. It's like recognising a face in a crowd. Easy.
  • Recalling — Pulling the whole thing out of your own head with no hints, and doing it. This is like drawing that face from memory. Hard — and this is the one that matters at work.

Videos only train the first one. So I feel smart but freeze when I open a blank editor.

Chapter Three

The Harvard experiment that proves it

Researchers at Harvard (Deslauriers and team) taught the same topic to two groups. One group got a polished lecture from an expert. The other group had to actually solve problems themselves — messy and uncomfortable.

Then they asked both groups: "How much do you feel you learned?" And separately, they tested them.

How much they FELT they learned (out of 5)
Watched the expert lecture3.9 / 5
Solved problems themselves2.9 / 5
How much they ACTUALLY learned (test score)
Watched the expert lectureLower
Solved problems themselvesHigher (+6% to +12%)
Source: Deslauriers et al., Harvard University. The results were the exact opposite of the feelings.

The video-watchers felt they learned more but scored lower. The problem-solvers felt they learned less but scored higher.

"The struggle I feel when doing it myself is not a sign I'm failing. It's the actual feeling of learning happening." — The one line I need to remember

Why does the brain prefer videos? Because the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy while doing nothing. It's lazy by design — it always picks the easy road. Watching is easy. Debugging is hard. So the brain nudges me to click "next video".

Chapter Four

Why I forget that 1-month course

Just-in-Time learning is like buying vegetables when you're cooking tonight — fresh and used. Just-in-Case is like filling the fridge with things "I might cook someday". Half of it rots.

Chapter Five

The 4 levels of learning

Researchers Chi and Wylie made a simple ladder called ICAP. The higher I climb, the more actually sticks. Where am I most of the time? The bottom rung.

1
Passive — Watching / readingVideo courses, blogs. Roughly 5–10% sticks. This is my default.
2
Active — Typing alongCopying the tutorial code line by line, highlighting docs. A bit better, but still not mine.
3
Constructive — Making something newWriting my own summary, drawing a flow, designing a test matrix. This is where real understanding starts.
4
Interactive — Discussing and defendingExplaining to someone (or an AI) who pushes back and asks "but why?". Roughly 75–90% sticks. This is the goal.

This is exactly why my idea of "having a conversation with AI like it's an expert" is a good one. It moves me from rung 1 straight to rung 4. But — only if I do it correctly (Chapter 7).

Two more proofs that "doing" beats "watching"

61%
remembered after 1 week — by people who tested themselves
40%
remembered after 1 week — by people who just re-read

The Generation Effect: if I produce something myself (write the script, explain the idea), it sticks far better than reading it. The Testing Effect (Roediger & Karpicke): quizzing yourself isn't for checking learning — quizzing is the learning. Just like school board exam prep: the students who solved past papers did better than those who re-read the textbook five times.

Chapter Six

My T-shape: what to go deep on, what to just "know"

Yes, as an SDET I need to know Dev, Data and DevOps things. But there's a difference between going deep and knowing enough to test it.

Picture the letter T. The tall vertical line is my deep skill — quality engineering, automation frameworks, making systems testable. The wide horizontal bar is the surrounding stuff I only need to understand, not build from scratch.

Simple rule: for any neighbouring field, I ask "how can this break?" — not "how do I build this?"

Before I open any video, ask:

"Which column does this fall in?" If it's the left column — worth deep, hands-on time. If it's the right column — a 30-second AI summary is enough. Close the tab.

Chapter Seven

Using AI as my tutor — the right way

In 1984, Benjamin Bloom showed that a student with a personal one-on-one tutor performs way above a student in a normal classroom (about "2 sigma" — roughly, an average student becomes a top-2% student). AI gives me a personal tutor for free. That's huge.

So my instinct — "ask AI instead of watching videos" — is right. It moves me to rung 4 of the ladder. But there are two traps.

Trap 1: Letting AI do all the thinking

If I ask AI to write the code, explain the error, and fix it — the work gets done, but the skill stays with the tool, not me. Like using Google Maps for years and still not knowing the roads in my own city.

Trap 2: The "No-Wall" trap

In old-school learning, I eventually hit a wall — a blank editor, a weird error, no idea what to do. That wall is painful but it's where real learning happens. With AI, I may never hit the wall. Everything works, and I feel great, but I learned nothing.

Using AI summaries to filter — yes, do this

Most videos have 5 minutes of intro, 10 minutes of setup, and 3 minutes of actual content. Getting an AI summary first lets me check in 30 seconds: "Is this in my left column or right column?" If right column, the summary alone is enough — skip the video.

Prompts that make me think (not offload)

Don't ask "write me the code". Ask things like these:

1. Build the mental model (no code) I'm an SDET trying to understand [Technology, e.g. Kafka consumer groups]. What problem did this solve that older systems couldn't? Explain with an analogy to [something I know, e.g. a message queue / a ticket counter]. Don't give me code.
2. Find the corners (where it breaks) What are the 3 most common ways [Technology] breaks, fails silently, or gives wrong data? What do senior engineers watch out for that juniors miss?
3. Quiz me (Socratic mode) Act as a strict Principal Quality Architect. Don't give me answers. Quiz me on how [System] handles [scenario, e.g. duplicate messages under load]. One question at a time. Evaluate my answer and challenge my assumptions.
4. Check my own explanation Here's my 3-sentence explanation of how [Thing] works: [my own words]. Point out the gaps, mistakes and things I've assumed without knowing.

One strict rule

Never copy-paste AI text into my notes. Writing it in my own words is the learning. Copying is back to rung 1.

Chapter Eight

My actual plan: 80 / 15 / 5 and the 5-step loop

Curiosity is not the problem. Unlimited curiosity is. So I give it a budget — like a monthly budget where 80% goes to needs, 15% to savings, and 5% is "fun money".

How I split my learning attention
80%
15%
5%
Core workGo deeper in SDETCuriosity
  • 80% — My actual work. Sprint tasks, the test framework, team commitments. This keeps my job and my core skill strong.
  • 15% — Getting deeper in my own field. Not repetitive automation — improving things: refactoring the framework into clean modules, building better mocks, making tests run faster in parallel. This turns daily work into practice.
  • 5% — Curiosity window. About 25–30 minutes a day (one Pomodoro). Data pipelines, DevOps, whatever's interesting. Timer ends, I stop. No more 3-hour YouTube rabbit holes.

The 5-step loop for anything new

When something new catches my eye during the curiosity window, I run this loop instead of just watching:

  1. Filter with AI summarySummarise the video/blog first. Left column or right column of my T-shape? If right column — read the summary, done, close the tab.
  2. Watch/read for 15–20 minutes maxSet a timer. When it rings, close every tab and video. No notes yet.
  3. Blind recall — write from memoryBlank editor. Explain in simple words: why does this exist, what problem does it solve, how does it break? Write it like I'm explaining to a junior — no buzzwords.This is the Generation Effect doing its work.
  4. Let AI poke holesPaste my explanation and ask it to find gaps and wrong assumptions. Then go back to the docs only for those specific gaps — not the whole thing again.Never copy AI's text into my notes.
  5. Build it and break it (left column only)Type the test harness / Docker config / assertion myself, no copy-paste. Then deliberately break it — bad schema, dropped connection, race condition — and fix it by hand.Hitting this wall is what makes it real skill.

Quick self-check before any video

  • Is there a real problem at work pushing me to learn this? (Just-in-Time?)
  • Is it in my left column (deep) or right column (just know)?
  • Am I inside my 5% window, or am I stealing from the 80%?
  • Will I write something from memory after this, or just move to the next video?

Closing

Curiosity is fine. Binge-watching is not.

Being curious about Dev, Data and DevOps is a good thing — modern systems need an SDET who understands the whole picture. The mistake was how I learnt: passively, speculatively, endlessly.

From now: learn when work demands it. Go deep only in my own lane. Use AI as a tutor who quizzes me, not a servant who does my work. And keep curiosity to a 30-minute window.
