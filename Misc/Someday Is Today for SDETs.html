Book notes for SDETs

Someday is Today

Seven ideas from Matthew Dicks' book, in plain English. Each one comes with an example from an SDET's week, because that's your week. After seven years in testing you probably have a someday list too: the framework rewrite, the talk, the blog, the course. This is about starting it.

10 min read7 ideas1 checklist

Where the book comes from

Dicks survived three near-death moments, including a bad car crash and being held at gunpoint at 22. He says he didn't feel fear in the moment. He felt regret, because he hadn't done anything he wanted with his life.

Afterwards he went from broke, living in his car and working at McDonald's, to best-selling author, world champion storyteller and award-winning journalist. The message is in the title. Stop waiting for someday and use the time you have right now.

These notes are based on a video summary of the book, so they cover the main ideas and not every detail.

Idea 1 of 7

The Parking Lot Practice

You don't need the right place, the right mood or a free afternoon. You need a laptop and ten minutes.

Bug it fixes Waiting for the perfect setup before starting

Dicks arrived early for a dentist appointment. Most people would scroll their phone. He parked, opened his laptop and wrote part of his novel right there in the car park. His line for it is that productivity isn't pretty.

The pretty version is the colour-coded notes, the tidy desk and the café with the perfect latte. The video's host admits he once thought he needed exactly that to write his own book. Dicks points out that soldiers in the First World War trenches wrote in small journals while shells landed nearby. If they could write there, a parking lot is fine.

The same goes for the gym, eating well and starting a business. If you wait for the timing to be right, you wait a very long time.

Example · your week

You want to build a clean Playwright framework with proper layers. You keep telling yourself you need a free weekend. Instead, you have ten minutes before standup. Open the repo and write one ugly test for the bug that hurt you last sprint.

# Ugly on purpose. It runs, and that's the win.
def test_checkout_rejects_expired_card(page):
    page.goto(STAGING_URL + "/checkout")
    page.fill("#card", EXPIRED_TEST_CARD)   # TODO: move to fixtures
    page.click("text=Pay")
    assert "expired" in page.inner_text(".error").lower()

It's hardcoded and messy. It also runs, and it catches a real bug. You can refactor on Thursday. A passing ugly test beats a beautiful framework that doesn't exist yet.

Try this week

Next time you have a gap and a laptop, work on your someday project for ten minutes, wherever you are. Don't tidy the setup first.

Idea 2 of 7

The Minutes Mindset

Plan your day in minutes, not hours. Small gaps add up faster than you think.

Bug it fixes Losing small gaps to scrolling

Most of us plan in hour-sized blocks. That hides a lot of waste. A gap of seven minutes doesn't feel like a block, so we spend it on our phones.

Dicks tells a story about meeting an aspiring writer for breakfast. She turned up seven minutes late and apologised. He wasn't annoyed. He had used those seven minutes to write a few sentences, and he showed her the screen. When he asks writers how much they've written, he says the answer is almost always none.

Since his near-death experiences he tries to get value from every minute. Ten minutes won't write a chapter. It can polish the last three paragraphs or produce five good sentences.

The video's host did something similar while working full time as a doctor. Between patients and at lunch he added bullet points to video scripts and brainstormed titles, while colleagues scrolled.

Neither of them says to skip rest. The host's own book spends three chapters on it. The question is only what you do by default in the small pockets of time.

Example · your week

Your pipeline takes eight minutes. You know what usually happens. Slack, then email, then the build finishes and you've lost the thread. Keep a menu by length instead.

  • 3 minRename one unclear test. Write a test title for a bug ticket. Add a note to your learning log.
  • 5 minImprove one weak assertion message. Read the last three failures of your flakiest test. Outline a blog post.
  • 10 minWrite one new test. Review a small PR properly. Fix one flaky wait.
Try this week

Write a list of five 5-minute tasks for your someday project. Next time something is loading, pick one.

Idea 3 of 7

The Lily Pad Method

Big goals feel like mountains because we think we must start at step one. You don't.

Bug it fixes Feeling stuck on step one

Dicks says the only real failure for a creator is to stop making things. The trouble is that big projects feel heavy: a novel, an exam, a business, a new framework. Looking at the whole thing can be daunting.

We usually treat goals as a straight line, A then B then C. The lily pad method treats the tasks as pads on a pond. Break the goal into tasks, then hop to whichever pad suits your mood today. You'll land on all of them eventually.

Dicks coached a woman who wanted to write a memoir and didn't know where to begin. He asked if she had moments she knew she wanted in it. She did, so he told her to write those. Chapter 15 is fine if that's what you can write today.

Mo Gawdat, who has written many books, works on five or six at once. Each morning he asks which one he feels most pulled toward and writes that one for a few hours. He describes those hours as full of joy.

Example · your week

Say the goal is a test framework for a new service. The pads might be the first smoke test, a CI run, reporting, a test data factory and a flaky-test triage doc. On a high-energy morning, design the retry logic. On a tired afternoon, tidy the test data. If one pad makes you groan, hop to another. You're still moving.

A guardrail from meHopping is for beating overwhelm, not for avoiding the hard pad forever. Keep the list visible and tick pads off. The ones you keep skipping are the ones to look at.

Try this week

Take one big someday goal and split it into five pads. Pick today's pad by mood, not by order.

Idea 4 of 7

The Horizon Habit

Aim at a direction on the horizon, not a number you can't control.

Bug it fixes Goals that depend on things you can't control

💡

SMART goals are a popular goal format: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. For example, "Reach 80% API test coverage by June."

A SMART goal can still point at something you don't control. The video's example is a new YouTuber who aims for a million subscribers. It's specific, measurable and time-bound, and it depends on an algorithm and on other people. Chasing it can wear you down.

Dicks prefers horizon goals. A horizon goal is a loose direction, far away. "I'm going to be a storyteller" is one. It leaves room for teaching storytelling, consulting, or writing for advertising, which he has done. A goal is a destination, not a method. A horizon takes the pressure off, and your path can change without the goal failing.

The music version: if you want to be a musician, you could join a band, but you could also write songs, DJ, present radio, produce, teach, or share what you learn online. All of those are on the music horizon.

Example · your week

A SMART goal: "Get promoted to Principal SDET by March." That depends on budget, headcount and your manager's calendar. A horizon goal: "I'm the person who makes quality easy for the team." The paths are many. Test architecture, developer tooling, performance and reliability, security testing, engineering lead, teaching and conference talks. If promotions freeze, you're still moving along the horizon.

My add-onPair the horizon with something small you control each week, like one flaky test fixed or one post drafted. The horizon sets the direction and the weekly action keeps you moving.

Try this week

Write one horizon goal in a single sentence. Under it, list three different jobs or projects that would count as being on that horizon.

Idea 5 of 7

The Piano Precedent

Make a plan, then expect a piano to land on it.

Bug it fixes Plans that break and take your motivation with them

A line from the book says five-year plans invite the universe to drop a piano on you. If you plan your life in fine detail, something will break the plan.

That doesn't mean skip planning. The video makes the other point too. Many people who ask for life advice can't say what they want. There's a famous exchange in Alice in Wonderland about this. If you don't know where you want to end up, it doesn't matter which road you take.

The video also describes a graph, which it attributes, with some doubt, to Darren Brown's book on Stoicism. Put your plans on one axis and fate on the other. Your life ends up somewhere between the two. You'll rarely follow the plan exactly. Still, a plan lets you steer instead of being pushed around by the wind.

💡

Stoicism is an old philosophy that says to put your effort into what you control and accept the rest.

Example · your week

Picture a five-year automation roadmap. Migrate the Selenium suite in year two, build custom reporting in year three. Then comes a reorg, a product pivot, or a new tool that changes the whole approach. The roadmap becomes a museum piece. If you've been in testing for seven years, you've likely seen one get rewritten.

A rule of thumb from mePlan the next 90 days in detail, the next year loosely, and treat the five-year view as a horizon (idea 4). When a piano lands, you redo a quarter, not a whole life.

Try this week

Write a 90-day plan for one goal. Next to it, write the one thing you'd change if a piano landed on it next month.

Idea 6 of 7

The 100-Year-Old Plan

At a crossroads, ask what you'd tell yourself at 100.

Bug it fixes Choosing the urgent thing over the important thing

When you can't decide, picture yourself at 100 and ask what advice that person would give. Dicks' example: he's at his desk with deadlines, and his son asks him to play tag. Working is the disciplined option. His 100-year-old self says to go and play, because one day the kid won't want to.

The video mentions a similar habit from Alex Hormozi, who journals as a conversation with his 85-year-old self. That older self knows his context and goals, so the advice is the kind he trusts. The host says he has started doing a version of it too.

Why it works: short-term thinking makes us choose what's urgent over what's important. The host admits he still picks work over friends too often, because work is fun and has a deadline.

Example · your week

It's Friday at 6pm. One flaky test is red on the release candidate. A close friend is leaving the country and tonight is the farewell dinner. Urgent says stay and babysit the pipeline. Ask the 100-year-old. They'd probably say go to dinner, because the pipeline will still be there on Monday.

Not every case goes that way. If production is down, you stay. The point is to make the urgent thing prove it deserves your evening.

💡

A prompt to copy: "Dear me at 100, I'm choosing between ___ and ___. What would you say?"

Try this week

Pick one decision this week. Write the prompt above and answer it in the voice of your 100-year-old self.

Idea 7 of 7

The Purposeful Approach

Be efficient so you have time for what you actually care about.

Bug it fixes Productivity that feels empty

Productivity talk often turns into work, hustle and money. The video asks why you want to get things done efficiently. Its answer is that productivity is about spending more time on what you want to do. Get the must-do things done faster, so you can reach the want-to-do things.

Dicks likes what he calls soft goals. They don't look productive by the usual definitions, but they're intentional and meaningful to you. The video shares a client who wanted to watch the fifty greatest movies of all time on the couch with her husband. She felt it wasn't productive. The coach told her it was very productive, because it was her dream. The job was to trim wasted time elsewhere so she could do it.

It works the other way too. Being really efficient at work, study and chores frees time and energy for the things that matter.

Example · your week

You already do this at work. You automate regression so people can spend their time on exploratory testing. Treat your life the same way. Speed up the repetitive parts, like admin, chores and busywork, so there's room for your soft goals.

Some prompts to get you thinking: learn an instrument, cook one hard recipe a month, finish a game you keep restarting, take long walks without a podcast, or write something nobody asked for. Which one would you add?

Try this week

Write down one soft goal. Block one slot in your calendar for it this week and protect it like a release meeting.
