01 / First Principles

The core idea: postcard vs. sealed envelope

Plain English

A postcard (HTTP) can be read by anyone who handles it on its way to the mailbox — the mail truck driver, a nosy neighbor, anyone. A sealed envelope (HTTPS) travels the exact same route, through the exact same hands, but nobody along the way can read what's written inside — only the person who opens it at the final address can.

HTTP sends data as plain text over the network. Anyone positioned anywhere between your device and the server — your home router, your ISP, a public WiFi hotspot, a compromised network device — can read every byte, because nothing is scrambled. HTTPS is just HTTP with a layer added underneath called TLS (Transport Layer Security), which encrypts the data before it leaves your device, so it looks like meaningless noise to anyone intercepting it along the way.

Watch both packets travel the same wire — only the HTTP one is legible to whoever's watching in the middle.
02 / Your Question, Directly

Solving your DevTools confusion directly

Plain English

You opening DevTools is like you opening your own mail at your own kitchen table. Of course you can read it — it was addressed to you, and you're the one who unsealed the envelope. HTTPS never promised to hide your mail from you. It only promised to hide it from the mail carrier, the sorting facility, and anyone else who touched it in between.

Your browser is one of the two legitimate endpoints of the connection — the other is the server. TLS encrypts the data only for the trip in between. The moment your browser receives the encrypted response, it decrypts it (using the session key it negotiated during the handshake — see the next section) before it ever renders the page or populates the Network tab. DevTools shows you the decrypted, already-opened version, because it's inspecting your browser's own internal state — not the wire.

Correction

So seeing plaintext in DevTools isn't a contradiction of "HTTPS encrypts data" — it's exactly what should happen. If you instead ran a packet capture tool (like Wireshark) on the network between your machine and the server, that's where you'd see only unreadable, encrypted bytes.

03 / Common Misconception

It's not about "who can access" the site

Plain English

HTTPS doesn't check ID at the door. Anyone — Chrome, Firefox, a curl command, a bot, a script written by a stranger — can open a connection to an HTTPS website exactly as easily as an HTTP one. The lock isn't on the door; it's wrapped around the letter that travels afterward.

What HTTPS actually guarantees, once a connection is made, is three things:

GuaranteePlain English
ConfidentialityNobody in the middle of the network can read the data.
IntegrityNobody in the middle can quietly alter the data without it being detected.
AuthenticationYou have reasonable proof the server really is who its certificate claims it is — not an impostor.
04 / Mechanism

How the envelope gets sealed — the TLS handshake

Plain English

Before your browser and the server can start whispering in a shared secret code, they have a short public conversation to agree on what that secret code will be — without ever saying the code out loud. That agreement process is the handshake.

Steps light up in sequence — this whole exchange happens once, then a fast shared "session key" handles everything after.
Why two kinds of encryption?

The public-key math used in step 4 (asymmetric encryption) is secure but slow. So it's only used briefly, to safely agree on a much faster symmetric secret key — which both sides then use for the rest of the conversation. This hybrid approach is why HTTPS doesn't meaningfully slow pages down.

05 / Trust

Certificates — proving the server is who it claims

Plain English

Encryption alone stops eavesdropping, but it doesn't stop impersonation — a scammer could still set up a fake "yourbank.com" and encrypt the connection to it perfectly well. A certificate is like a notarized ID card: a trusted third party (a Certificate Authority) has checked that this server really does control the domain it claims to.

The shackle "closes" once the certificate is validated — that's your browser's padlock icon.

The trust chain

Browsers don't trust every CA blindly — they ship with a small, curated list of Root CAs they trust completely. A server's certificate is usually signed by an Intermediate CA, which is in turn signed by that trusted Root — forming a chain your browser walks and verifies.

Each link glows in turn as the browser walks the chain, ending in a checkmark once trust is established.
06 / Boundaries

What HTTPS does not protect against

Not covered

Malware or spyware on your own device. If something on your machine can read your screen or your browser's memory, encryption in transit doesn't help — the data is already decrypted there, same as in DevTools.

Not covered

A malicious or compromised server. HTTPS proves you're talking to the server the certificate names — it says nothing about whether that server is trustworthy, stores your data safely, or has good intentions.

Not covered

Phishing look-alike domains. yourbnak.com can get a perfectly valid certificate too — the padlock only confirms you're talking to whoever owns that exact domain, not that the domain is the "real" business you meant to visit.

07 / Applied to AWS

Where ACM and ACM Private CA fit in

Plain English

ACM is a vending machine for certificates that every browser in the world already trusts — used for anything the public internet talks to. ACM Private CA lets you become your own tiny trusted authority, for cases where only your own systems — not the whole internet — need to trust the certificate.

Your original understanding was close, with one refinement: ACM Private CA isn't specifically about "inside a VPC" — it's about issuing certificates you control trust for, wherever your own systems are (inside one VPC, across several VPCs, on-premises servers, IoT devices) — as opposed to certificates the public internet's browsers need to trust.

TraitACM (Public)ACM Private CA
Who trusts the certificateAny public browser or client, out of the boxOnly systems you've configured to trust your private CA
Typical usePublic-facing endpoints — an ALB, CloudFront, API GatewayInternal service-to-service traffic, mutual TLS, internal APIs, IoT device identity
Issued byA publicly trusted CA (Amazon Trust Services)Your own private CA hierarchy, created and managed in your account
Cost modelFree for certificates used with integrated AWS servicesPay for the private CA plus each certificate issued
ScopeMust prove you own the public domainYou define your own naming and trust rules entirely
Why it exists

Getting a publicly trusted CA to issue certificates for purely internal hostnames (like payments.internal) isn't possible or sensible — public CAs only vouch for domains you can prove public ownership of. ACM Private CA solves the "I still want encrypted, certificate-based trust, but only among my own services" problem.

08 / Pitfalls

Common mistakes

Mistake

Assuming HTTPS means "the site is safe." It only means the connection is encrypted and the domain identity is verified — not that the site's owner or content is trustworthy.

Mistake

Forgetting certificate expiry and renewal. Certificates have a validity window; ACM automates renewal for integrated services, but it's a common outage cause when certificates are managed manually elsewhere.

Mistake

Using a public CA certificate for purely internal hostnames (or vice versa) — mismatching which trust model actually fits the audience that needs to trust the certificate.

09 / Recall

Summary — what to remember

  • HTTPS = HTTP + TLS. It encrypts data in transit, between your browser and the server — it does not gate who can connect.
  • DevTools shows you decrypted data because your browser is a legitimate endpoint that has already opened the "envelope" — that's not a contradiction of encryption, it's the expected behavior.
  • The TLS handshake uses slow-but-secure asymmetric encryption briefly, just to safely agree on a fast symmetric session key used for the rest of the conversation.
  • Certificates prove server identity through a chain of trust rooted in a small set of CAs your browser trusts by default.
  • HTTPS doesn't protect against malware on your device, a dishonest server, or convincing look-alike domains — it only secures the transit and confirms domain identity.
  • ACM issues publicly trusted certificates for anything the internet talks to; ACM Private CA lets you run your own trust authority for systems only you need to trust — not strictly "inside a VPC," but wherever your own infrastructure lives.
