What is inside

Many people think software architecture is hard and confusing. They say there are no clear answers. That is partly true. But you can learn it step by step, and this guide will help you.

This guide explains the whole book in simple English. It keeps all the main ideas, rules and examples. It leaves out the exercises. Each chapter has a "Words to know" box, so new terms are easy.

Who this book is for

This book is for you if you want to understand what software architecture is. It is also for you if you prefer a lively talk over a dry lecture. If you said yes to both, keep reading.

It is probably not for you if you are brand new to the tech industry. Architecture makes more sense after you have built a few things yourself. It is also not for experienced architects who only want a reference book. And it is not for anyone who refuses to try a new way of learning. The authors joke that such a person would rather lick a 9-volt battery.

Your brain is choosy

Your brain loves new things. It looks for anything unusual and ignores the usual. Imagine you are on a forest walk and a tiger jumps out of the bushes. Your heart pounds, and your brain decides, "This is very important. Never forget it."

Now imagine you are studying in a warm, quiet room. Your brain sees no tiger, so it quietly drops the boring material. That includes the chapter on architectural decisions.

The fix is to make the material feel important. There are two ways. The slow way is repetition. You repeat the same idea until your brain says, "Fine, this must matter." The faster way is to give your brain more kinds of work. Pictures, stories, questions, and activities all use different parts of your head at once.

Think of your brain as a guard at the gate of a wedding hall. The guard only lets in guests who look interesting. Bring something interesting, such as a surprising picture, a joke, or a question that makes you say "wait, what?" Then the guard lets it in.

How the authors help you

The book uses a few learning ideas that have research behind them:

  • Pictures with labels inside. Words placed inside a picture are easier to remember than words in a caption at the bottom of the page.
  • A friendly, talking voice. Your brain pays more attention when it feels that someone is talking to it. This works even if that someone is a book.
  • Stories and people. Your brain notices people. So the book uses characters, companies, and dialogue.
  • Planned repetition. The same idea appears more than once, on purpose. It comes in different forms, so it stays in your memory in more than one place.
  • Feelings. Surprise, curiosity, fun, and the "I am great!" feeling when a puzzle works all help memory. You do not need a sad story about a dog.

Your part of the deal

The authors did their part. Here is what they suggest you do:

  • Slow down. The more you understand, the less you need to memorize.
  • Stop and think. When the book asks a question, answer it in your head before you look at the answer.
  • Do the exercises with a pencil. Writing things down counts as physical activity for learning.
  • Read the "There Are No Dumb Questions" boxes. They are part of the main content. They are not optional extras.
  • Read something light before bed. Your brain keeps working after you close the book. New, heavy material can get in the way of that.
  • Say it out loud. Try to explain an idea to someone else. You will find the gaps in your understanding quickly.
  • Drink water. Even a little dehydration makes thinking less clear. It can happen before you feel thirsty.
  • Listen to your brain. If you start skimming or forgetting what you just read, take a break. Pushing in more will not help.
  • Feel something. Make up captions for the pictures. Groaning at a bad joke is still better than feeling nothing.
  • Use it every day. Architecture is a skill, and you build a skill with practice.

How the book is organised

This is a learning book. It is not a reference book. Start at the beginning, because later chapters assume you have read the earlier ones. The book follows a simple pattern. It splits a big idea into small pieces. You understand each piece on its own. Then you join the pieces together. Hard ideas come only after the simple ones are clear.

The book also mixes learning styles. Some parts go step by step. Some show the big picture first. Some start with an example. This gives both sides of your brain some work, so you can stay focused for longer. Every chapter has exercises with solutions. Try the problem first. Look at the solution only if you get stuck.

The book also uses the 80/20 approach. It covers the parts of software architecture that you will use most. It does not try to be a full encyclopedia. The examples are simple, common business problems. This is on purpose, because abstract theory is hard to remember. Some "Brain Power" exercises have no single right answer. That is also on purpose. The crossword puzzles are the only optional part of the book.

The exercises are not extras. They are part of the main content. Reading about swimming does not teach you to swim. In the same way, reading about architecture does not teach you to be an architect.

Two do-it-yourself chapters

Chapters 9 and 12 are different. They are fully exercise-based. You act as the software architect from start to end. You choose the architectural characteristics. You build a logical architecture. You make the decisions, including which architectural style to use.

Chapter 9 is about TripEZ. It is said like "trapeze". TripEZ is an online trip-management dashboard that makes travel easier, especially for people who travel a lot for work. Travelers see and manage all their reservations, grouped by trip. They can do this in a browser or on a phone.

Chapter 12 is about Make the Grade. It is a standardized testing system. Students at Dataville Public School in certain grade levels all take the same test. The test shows how well the students, teachers, and school are doing. Both chapters are a good way to check how much of the book you remember.

Watching people lift weights at the gym does not make you stronger. You have to lift the bar yourself. This book is the gym. The exercises are your practice.

  • Take a break when you feel tired, and drink some water.

What Software Architecture Is

Architecture is the structure that holds a software system together. It is the part that is painful to change later. This chapter gives you a simple map with four dimensions, and shows how architecture differs from design.

  • Software architecture: the structure of a system, which is hard and costly to change later.
  • Architectural characteristics: the qualities a system must support, such as scalability or availability.
  • Architectural decision: a big choice about the system that has a long-term or major effect.
  • Logical component: a building block of the system that does one clear job.
  • Architectural style: the overall shape of the system.
  • Trade-off: a good point and a bad point that you weigh before you choose.
  • Design: the detail and appearance of a system, which is easy to change.

Architecture is the blueprint

Think of a house on your street. Its architecture is its structure. That means its shape, its number of rooms and floors, and its size. The building plan shows all the lines and boxes needed to build it.

Walls, floors, and the roof are hard and costly to change after the house is built. These are the important parts.

Software has a similar blueprint. It does not have walls and stairs. It has user interfaces, services, databases, and the ways they talk to each other. A service is a part of the system that does one kind of work. A software architecture diagram is the building plan. Like a house plan, it sets guidelines and limits. It also shows the vision for the finished product.

Architecture is also like a garden. The overall layout is the style. Each group of similar plants is a component. Each single plant is one piece of code. Weather changes a garden, and new technology changes a system. If you ignore a garden, weeds grow. In software, the weeds are problems that build up over time.

Four dimensions of architecture

A room is described by three numbers. They are length, width, and height. Software architecture is described by four dimensions:

  • Architectural characteristics. What the system must support. Examples are scalability (handling more users), testability, and availability.
  • Architectural decisions. Big choices with long-term or major effects. Examples are the kind of database, the number of services, and how services talk to each other.
  • Logical components. The building blocks of the system functions and how they work together. Examples in an online store are inventory management and payment processing.
  • Architectural style. The overall physical shape of the system. A house also has a style, such as Victorian or ranch.

A common mistake is to describe an architecture with only one dimension. Saying "our architecture is microservices" tells you only the style. Big questions stay open. Which qualities are critical? What are the main components? What major decisions did you make about how to build it?

The four pieces fit together

Think of each dimension as one piece of a puzzle. Each piece has its own shape. All pieces must fit together to make the full picture. The style must match the characteristics you chose and the decisions you made. The logical components must also match the characteristics, the style, and the decisions.

So when you change one piece, check the others. Say you decide you need very high scalability. That choice should appear in the style you pick. It should also appear in the components you define and in the decisions that follow.

You cannot skip a dimension. To describe an architecture properly, you need all four.

Characteristics come first

Characteristics are the abilities that are critical to the success of the system. Without them, you cannot make architectural decisions. You also cannot compare trade-offs. That is why they come first.

Imagine you must choose between two homes. One is roomy, but it is next to a busy highway. The other is small, but it is on a quiet street. Which matters more to you, size or quiet? You cannot choose until you know what you care about.

Software works the same way. Say you must choose a database. It could be relational, simple key/value, or a complex graph database. The best answer depends on which characteristics matter most.

  • A graph database is a good choice when you need fast searches across connected data. Speed matters most here.
  • A relational database is often better when the data must always stay correct. Correct data matters most here.

You may also hear these qualities called nonfunctional requirements. Other names are quality attributes, or "the ilities", because many of them end in "-ility". The book prefers "architectural characteristics". This term describes the character of the architecture and what it must support.

Choosing a database

These two cases are our own examples of the book's point. A team builds a shop that recommends products. It follows links between people, orders, and items. Search speed across those links matters most. A graph database fits this need.

Now think of a bank that tracks account balances. A lost or wrong number is a disaster. A relational database fits better, because it keeps related data consistent. The decision is the same kind, but the characteristics differ. So the answer differs.

Decisions guide the team

Architectural decisions are choices about the structure of the system. They have long-term or major effects. Once made, they act as limits. They tell the development team how to plan and build.

Think about a home. Should it have one floor or two? Should the roof be flat or sloped? These choices are hard to undo.

Here is a software example. The user interface must never talk straight to the database. It must go through services. This one rule shapes how the UI team works. It also shapes how every other part of the system reaches the data.

Expect many such decisions. A system can easily have several dozen documented architectural decisions. Bigger and more complex systems have more. Good decisions focus on the important themes. They do not cover every small detail.

Logical components are the rooms

A house is made of rooms. A system is made of logical components. Each component does one function. Examples are processing the payment for an order, managing item inventory, or tracking orders. A component should have a clear role and a clear responsibility. You should be able to say in one sentence what it does.

In code, a logical component usually lives in a directory or namespace. A directory is a folder of files. A namespace is a name that groups related code. Together they give your team a place to keep the source files for each business function.

Where payment code lives

Say the directory is app/order/payment. Its matching namespace is app.order.payment. This pair tells everyone that this logical component is Payment Processing. The source code that lets customers pay for an order lives there. It uses that namespace.

app/
  order/
    payment/     Payment Processing component

Here is a quick way to keep two terms clear. The domain is the problem you are solving. It is the "what". The functionality of the system is how you solve it. It is the "how".

Style is the overall shape

Houses come in styles such as Victorian, ranch, or Tudor. The style tells you a lot about the structure. Ranch homes usually have one floor. Colonial and Tudor homes often have chimneys. Contemporary homes often have flat roofs.

Software styles work the same way. Each style gives the system an overall shape. Each style also comes with its own set of characteristics. The book covers five common styles later, with one chapter for each. Here is a short preview:

  • Microservices scale very well. They also give a high level of agility. Agility means you can respond quickly to change.
  • Layered architecture is less complex and less costly.
  • Event-driven architecture has high scalability. It is also very fast and responsive.

It is important to get the style right early. Imagine you start building a one-story ranch house. Halfway through, you decide you want a three-story Victorian house instead. That is a major task. It breaks the budget and the move-in date.

Software is no different. A layered monolith is one big program with all its parts in one block. Moving a big one to microservices is a serious project. It is not a quick weekend refactor. Refactoring means changing code structure without changing what it does.

The book promises to show you later how to pick a style based on the characteristics that matter to you. You cannot choose a style until you know what is important. The answer may be one style or a combination of styles. So match the style to the characteristics you need.

Think of a Diwali house repair. Painting the walls again is cheap. Changing the house plan after the foundation is poured costs far more. An architectural style works the same way. If you pick it on day one, based on what the system needs, you avoid a very costly rebuild.

Architecture versus design

Architecture is more about structure. Design is more about appearance. In a room, the wall color, the furniture layout, and the carpet are design. The size of the room and the place of its doors and windows are architecture. They are part of the structure.

A typical business application follows the same split. The architecture is how web pages talk to backend services and databases. They do this to read and save data. The design is how each page looks. It covers colors, field placement, and which design patterns the developers use.

One order system, two views

Imagine a company that replaces its outdated order system with a custom one. Customers place orders, view or cancel them, and pay. They can pay with a credit card, a gift card, or both.

From a design view, you might draw a UML class diagram. UML is a standard way to draw software classes and how they relate. The diagram shows how the payment classes work together. It does not show how files are organised or where they are deployed.

From an architecture view, you might choose a separate service for each payment type. You would also add an orchestrator service that coordinates the payment process. Now you are talking about services, how they communicate, and how they are deployed.

A scale from design to architecture

Some decisions are clearly architectural. Choosing an architectural style is one. Other decisions are clearly design. Moving a field on a screen is one. Changing a field's type inside a class is another. Most decisions fall somewhere in between.

It matters where a decision sits, because that tells you who should own it. Some belong to the development team. An example is how to design the classes for one feature. Some belong to the architect. An example is picking the right style for the whole system. Others should be made together. An example is whether to split a service into two, or to join two services into one.

Three questions for any decision

To find where a decision sits, ask three questions. A decision is more architectural when it is more strategic. It is also more architectural when it is costly to change. And it is more architectural when its trade-offs are large.

Question 1: Strategic or tactical?

Strategic decisions are long term. They shape future choices. Tactical decisions are short term. They mostly stand on their own, but they may follow a wider strategy.

Deciding how big your new home will be is strategic. It limits how many rooms you can have. Picking a lighting fixture is tactical. It does not change the size of your dining table.

To judge this, ask three things:

  • How much thought and planning does it need? A few minutes to an hour is tactical. Days or weeks is probably strategic.
  • How many people are involved? A choice you make with one colleague is tactical. A choice that needs many meetings with many stakeholders is strategic.
  • Is it a long-term vision or a short-term action? If you will live with it for years, it leans toward architecture.

Question 2: How hard is it to change?

Martin Fowler is a well-known software architect and author. He once wrote that "software architecture is the stuff that's hard to change." The harder a decision is to undo, the closer it is to architecture.

Moving from a traditional n-tiered layered architecture to microservices takes much time and effort. So it sits at the far architecture end. Rearranging fields on a user interface screen is quick. So it sits at the far design end. Building a new room onto a house is architecture. Adding a floor mat is design.

Question 3: How big are the trade-offs?

A trade-off is a good point and a bad point that you weigh when you decide. Choosing a city to live in has big trade-offs. Examples are jobs, cost, and travel time to work. Choosing the color of your living room rug has small trade-offs.

Decisions with big trade-offs need more time and analysis. They tend to be architectural. Decisions with small trade-offs can be made quickly. They tend to be design.

Almost every decision has a trade-off, even a small one. If you think you found one with no trade-off, look again.

Using the three questions

Here is a case the book uses. You decide to use asynchronous messaging between the Order Placement service and the Inventory Management service. Asynchronous means one service sends a message and does not wait for the reply. Your goal is to make ordering feel faster. The customer should not wait while the business updates its inventory. Now check the decision against the three questions.

  • Strategic or tactical? Tactical. Not many people need to be involved. It does not need long-term planning. This pushes it toward design.
  • Effort to build or change? Low. Sending a message to another service is fairly standard work. This also pushes it toward design.
  • Trade-offs? Big. A queue makes ordering feel faster. But inventory may not update on time. This can cause back-order situations. This pulls it toward architecture.

Now combine the three answers. The decision lands near the middle, a little on the architecture side. So an architect should probably be consulted or involved.

Notice that you needed all three questions to see this. Any one alone would have given a misleading answer. The purpose of this exercise is to tell development teams when to bring in an architect. It also tells them when they can decide on their own.

Quick recap

  • Software architecture is the structure of a system. It is the part that is hard and costly to change.
  • Architecture has four dimensions: characteristics, decisions, logical components, and style.
  • The four dimensions fit like a puzzle. When you change one, check the others.
  • Design is about appearance and detail. Architecture is about structure. Most decisions sit between the two.
  • Ask if a choice is strategic, how hard it is to change, and how big its trade-offs are.
  • Characteristics come first. Without them you cannot make decisions or weigh trade-offs.
  • Choose the architectural style early. Changing it later means a major rebuild.

Meet Lafter

Sillycon Symposia is a startup in the style of the Bay Area in California. It runs technology conferences with a twist. Every event has comedy, because people who laugh stay interested. The company plans to build Lafter, a social network for technologists who share its conferences and jokes.

The business team wrote a requirements document. A requirement is a statement of what the system should do. Here is the short version.

  • Users register for usernames and approve a privacy policy.
  • They can post a "Joke", which is a long post, or a "Pun", which is a short post.
  • Followers react with a "HaHa" for strong approval or a "Giggle" for a milder one.
  • Speakers from Sillycon events get a special icon. They can host forums about their content.
  • Messages can have up to 281 characters. Users can also link to outside content.
  • The expected audience is hundreds of speakers and thousands of users.

The extra context matters just as much. The company works in many countries and has a very small support staff. It also expects "bursty" traffic. This means the system will be very busy during live conferences and quiet in between.

The cubicle lesson

Alex has just been named the architect for Lafter. Sam thinks the design is so simple that they can start building right away. Mara disagrees. You can skip analysis only for the very simplest applications, and Lafter is not one of them.

First, you must study the architectural characteristics and the logical components. Logical components are the main parts of the system, such as the parts that handle users or posts. Only then should you choose an architectural style. A style is a general way to organize a system. You can build almost any application in almost any style, but some styles fit better. Choosing the style first is the wrong order. First learn what the system must do well.

Mara also says architects should join the business analysts early and often. A small difference to a business person can be a big problem, or very hard to support, in the architecture.

Sam then suggests the agile approach. This means you start small and keep improving until the whole system exists. Mara points out that this does not work the same way for characteristics. It is very hard to make a system highly scalable if it was never designed to scale.

Think of it like building a house. You can add a basement after the foundation, walls, and roof are done. But it is a painful project. Architectural characteristics are like the foundation. You cannot easily add them later.

What is an architectural characteristic?

The domain is the problem you are solving, such as selling shoes or sharing jokes. Designing for the domain will take much of your effort. But an architect must also study the architectural characteristics. These describe the operational and structural qualities the system needs.

The book defines an architectural characteristic in three parts.

  1. It is a nondomain design consideration. This means it is not a business feature.
  2. It influences the structure of the system.
  3. It is critical or important to the success of the system.

Put simply, architectural characteristics are the capabilities a system must have for the project to succeed.

Structural design has two halves. One half is the logical components, which represent the domain. The other half is the architectural characteristics. Together they give you the structural considerations for an architecture.

Many teams call these "nonfunctional requirements". The book does not like this name. These qualities are functional. They just do not concern the domain, and the name makes them sound less important.

The book also dislikes two other names. "System quality attributes" sounds like something you check at the end of a project. "Cross-cutting requirements" is the least bad. But the word "requirement" ties it to domain behavior.

Requirements say what the application should do. Characteristics say how well it must do it, and why certain choices were made. For example, performance is often critical but rarely appears in a requirements document. You have to search for it.

When a quality becomes architectural

Security matters for almost every project. Every system needs basic precautions while it is designed and coded. But security becomes an architectural characteristic when the architect must make a special structural effort to support it.

The Lafter promotions feature

Lafter needs a feature that advertises upcoming promotions. It also applies rules for when each promotion is valid. You could build this as a monolith. A monolith is one deployable unit with one matching database. Or you could build it as a set of independent services.

In the monolith, every change to the promotion rules means you must deploy the whole application again. This is because the monolith ships as one piece. In the distributed design, only the Promotions service is affected. So you can deploy just that service again. This is one of many trade-offs you will weigh when you choose between a monolith and a distributed design.

Pick fewer characteristics

A system could support a huge number of architectural characteristics. It should not. Every characteristic you add makes the design more complex. The book gives three reasons to be careful.

  • They are hard to standardize. Different organizations use different names for the same thing. "Performance" and "responsiveness" might mean the same behavior. A shared vocabulary helps your team make a usable list. The book calls this a ubiquitous language.
  • They are synergistic. This means they affect each other and the domain. A more secure app usually needs encryption and extra checks. This costs some performance. Storing payment details of users also changes the security and data integrity characteristics.
  • They are overabundant. New ones appear all the time. A few years ago, a cloud provider could not give on-demand elasticity, so nobody designed for it. Even the number of categories has grown. Cloud constraints and capabilities are new additions.

Overengineering happens when you support too many characteristics. The design becomes complex for little or no benefit. A related trap is resume-driven development (RDD). It is fun to try new tools, and you should keep learning. But do not choose features to improve your own résumé instead of helping the business.

Synergy is a warning sign. No architect can make every architecture scalable, because limits like memory and bandwidth get in the way. Some characteristics also pull against each other. High performance and scalability are hard to get together. Be careful when you change one characteristic. Check what else it touches. Changes to the domain, such as component boundaries, can also change characteristics.

A short list has one more benefit. When you know which characteristics are truly critical, you can filter out the nice-to-haves. These only add complexity.

Explicit and implicit characteristics

An explicit characteristic is stated clearly in the requirements. An implicit characteristic is not written down. But it still shapes the design, because the context makes it obvious.

Think of it like mail and packages piling up outside a house. You do not need a note to know that nobody is home.

Security is often implicit. Nobody writes "do not build an insecure system" in a requirements list, but architects know it. A high-frequency trading firm buys and sells shares very fast. It may never say that transactions must finish in milliseconds. But anyone in that field knows it matters a lot.

Some implicit characteristics are subtle. One example is keeping the internal structure clean. Then careless coding will not wreck the system over time. Nobody writes "make it maintainable" in a requirements document, but it still matters.

Same quality, different weight

Consider a free office lunch-ordering system. Security matters a little. It stops coworkers from finding out that you order the same egg salad sandwich every day. Availability matters a little too. If the system breaks, everyone just gets their own lunch.

Now compare this with a payment system. There, security and availability matter enormously. The characteristic is the same. The weight depends on the domain.

Grouping characteristics into families

Architectural characteristics are like animals in a zoo. Some are low-level code traits, such as modularity. Others are big operational concerns, such as scalability and elasticity. There is no universal list. There is also no real standard for what the terms mean. Each organization explains them in its own way, and new ones keep arriving.

Mara's answer is to group them into families. Think of it like a zoo map that groups animals into houses and enclosures. The book's families are:

  • Process. This is where the software development process meets architecture. These cover the steps of building the software.
  • Structural. This is how the internal structure of the system looks. It includes how tightly components are joined (coupling). It also includes how integration points relate.
  • Operational. This is how architectural decisions affect what the people who run the system can do.
  • Cross-cutting. These do not fit neatly anywhere, and they touch the whole system. Like the platypus in a zoo, they refuse to sit in one enclosure.

Some characteristics appear in more than one family. The software world keeps changing, so the families keep changing too.

Here is a quick reference of characteristics you will often meet. They are grouped the way the book's zoo map groups them. The meanings are in everyday words, because exact terms vary between organizations.

Where characteristics come from

Characteristics do not appear from nowhere. The book names three sources.

  • The problem domain. Many needs come straight from the problem itself.
  • Environmental awareness. This is what you know about where you work. An architect in a fast-moving startup will favor agility, whether anyone says it or not. Here is another case. Say you must choose between a custom protocol and a standard one to join two subsystems. If the company plans many mergers, that favors the open standard. Context always matters.
  • Holistic domain knowledge. The domain is bigger than your own slice of it. Take a payment system. Knowing the finance world, its rules, and customer habits shows you more characteristics.

Turning business words into characteristics

Business people rarely say "scalability". They say "thousands of users". Your job as an architect is to look closely at that phrase. You can ask three questions.

  • How many users will there be in total? This is scalability.
  • How many will be active at the same moment? This is concurrency.
  • How fast will they arrive? This is elasticity.

The university promotion

Lafter runs a sign-up promotion at a university. There are 1,000 students and 10 hours to register. To keep the math easy, you might design for a steady flow of 100 sign-ups an hour. But real students are not steady.

The most eager ones rush in during the first hour. Many delay the work and then stay quiet for most of the day. Then a last-minute crowd floods in just before the window closes. The real design must handle a burst at the start. It must stay mostly idle, and then handle another burst at the end. Never underestimate how long a university student can delay.

Business experts also hide requirements inside plain English. Architects must read between the lines and turn those hints into characteristics. Take the requirement "we need this to be very popular at launch". It is a hint about elasticity and concurrency, even though nobody used those words.

Solutions are not requirements

Customers often arrive with a solution instead of a requirement. The US Air Force once asked for a fighter jet that could reach Mach 2.5. The technology of that time could not do it. So the designers asked why the speed mattered.

The answer was that these jets were expensive. The pilot needed to be able to escape a fight if necessary. With that knowledge, the team designed the F-16. Its top speed was Mach 2.1. But it was the most maneuverable and fastest-accelerating jet of its time.

The lesson is to keep asking "why?" like a curious child until you find the real need. A requirement describes the need. A solution describes one way to meet it, often a product or a tool. When someone says "we need to buy this software package", ask what problem it solves.

Composite characteristics

Sometimes a business person asks for a "reliable" system. Reliable is not one thing. It can mean the system is available. It can mean its screens behave the same way each time. It can also mean its data stays correct. The book calls a quality that combines others like this a composite characteristic.

To break a composite into parts, ask: can I measure this in a clear way? Performance is a composite too. You must be more specific before you can measure it. Agility is another composite. It is built from testability, deployability, modularity, and more.

The book gives an example of a measurable piece. It is first contentful paint, which is how long a web page takes to load on a mobile device. Once you can measure something, you can design for it and test it.

If you cannot measure a characteristic, you cannot tell whether you achieved it. Turn vague words like "fast" or "reliable" into numbers and specific checks.

Priorities depend on context

No two projects get the same list of characteristics. Each application picks its own set and ranks them in its own way. A free lunch-ordering app and a bank's payment system will have very different top priorities.

Context counts here too. A fast-moving startup will prioritize agility, whether or not anyone writes it down. A large enterprise has a lot at stake and will weigh things differently.

You may ask business users which characteristics they want. They will often say "all of them!" This is tempting, but it is a bad idea. Characteristics are synergistic, so every extra one makes the design harder. Your job is to turn business goals into a few characteristics that you can measure and build for.

Finding the balance

A design can go in three ways.

  • You skip the characteristics analysis. Later you find that the system cannot meet its real needs. Then you face expensive rework.
  • You balance domain needs against the necessary characteristics. You get the operational and structural goals you need, without overengineering.
  • You study too many characteristics and lose touch with the real needs of the project. You waste time that should go to building and maintaining the system.

The magical number seven

Here is a helpful rule for talks between architects and business analysts. Limit the list to seven characteristics. Why seven? Psychologists have long found that people handle information in chunks of about seven items. That is why early phone numbers had seven digits.

Seven gives enough variety to be useful. It is not so many that people freeze from too many options. The book cites George Miller's famous 1956 paper on this idea.

Trimming the list

The book gives a worksheet with 18 candidates for Lafter. They include performance, data integrity, deployability, responsiveness, testability, availability, fault tolerance, scalability, recoverability, elasticity, concurrency, and auditability. You trim the list to seven. Then you mark the top three.

Here is a sample result. It is not the book's answer. Scalability, availability, and data integrity might be at the top. The others stay as secondary concerns. The exact picks depend on the business. That is the whole point of the exercise.

Characteristics and components together

Architectural characteristics are close to capabilities. They describe the kinds of things the solution must support. Logical components are covered in more detail in Chapter 4. They are closer to behavior. They describe the design of the system that solves the core problem.

Architects combine the two to choose an architectural style. Lafter is no exception. You must balance what the business needs the app to do against what the architecture must be good at.

Not choosing a characteristic does not mean the design will be poor at it on purpose. It only shows priority. You trade one characteristic against another. You choose the one that matters most for this system.

Quick recap

  • Architectural characteristics are the capabilities a system needs to succeed. They are not features.
  • A characteristic becomes architectural when it changes the structure, such as splitting a monolith into services.
  • Keep the list short, because every extra characteristic adds complexity.
  • Some characteristics are implicit. Use your domain knowledge to find them.
  • Turn business phrases like "thousands of users" or "reliable" into measurable characteristics.
  • Ask "why?" until you find the real requirement behind a solution.
  • Limit the list to about seven. Then rank the top few for your project.
  • Group characteristics into process, structural, operational, and cross-cutting families.

Two Laws: Every Choice Has a Cost

Software architecture has no list of "best practices" that saves you from thinking. This chapter gives you two laws, a way to compare options, and a simple template to write down why you chose something.

A sneaker app that grew big

Picture a company called Two Many Sneakers. Its mobile app lets collectors buy, sell, and trade rare shoes. The book calls these collectors "sneakerheads". The app now has millions of listings and is doing very well.

The first design was simple. It had one trading service that talks to its own database. A service is a small program that does one job.

Now customers want real-time alerts. They want a message the moment a pair they are hunting goes on sale. The business also wants better fraud detection. That means analytics that spot strange behaviour and filter out bots.

The plan is simple. The trading service will tell two new services when something important happens. These are a notification service and an analytics service. But many questions appear as soon as you start to design it:

  • How should the services talk to each other?
  • Do the analytics and notification services need the same data, or different data?
  • Will the finance department want updates too? It cares about compliance, which means following rules and laws.

If finance wants updates, the design must be easy to extend. Some things are known and many are not. That is normal.

Some surprises are "unknown unknowns". These are problems that nobody could list in advance. They can appear in the middle of a project and spoil a neat plan.

Architects almost never get complete facts before they must choose. So make good choices with the facts you have. Work in small steps and be ready to change your mind. Nothing in architecture stays still.

Know which qualities matter most

Before you pick a design, decide which architectural characteristics matter most. These are the qualities the system must have. Examples are scalability, security, and upgradability. Scalability means the system can handle more users and more data.

Not every characteristic is equally important. Ask if each one is critical for success or only nice to have. Everyone likes upgradability. But is it the quality that decides if this project succeeds?

Some characteristics also work against each other. A system with strong encryption and secure connections is often slower. You cannot get the best of every quality at once. So you must choose.

The First Law: everything is a trade-off

A trade-off means you gain something and give up something else. Think of it like choosing a train ticket. A confirmed AC seat costs more money. A cheaper sleeper ticket costs you comfort. Either way, you pay in some form.

Software works the same way. A design with great scalability may make deployment or reliability harder. Deployment means putting the software on servers so users can use it. Every option has good and bad sides.

Your job has two parts:

  1. Know the trade-offs of every option you consider.
  2. Pick the option that best serves the characteristics that matter most.

This is the First Law of Software Architecture: everything in software architecture is a trade-off. It sounds obvious. But many architects forget it when they love a new tool. When someone praises an approach, ask: "What are the trade-offs?"

The book warns about two traps. In the first trap, a person always uses the tool that worked last time. In the second trap, a person gets excited about every new tool. The book calls this "shiny object syndrome". Such a person thinks a new technology will fix every problem.

New tools are fine. Just ask two questions. Do the good sides help you build a successful app? Can you live with the bad sides?

Rich Hickey said that programmers know the benefits of everything and the trade-offs of nothing. Architects need to know both.

Suppose you look at an architecture decision and see no trade-off. Then you have not looked hard enough. Look again.

Trade-off analysis, part one: queues

Back to the sneaker app. The team has chosen messaging. In messaging, one service sends a message and the others receive it later. This is called asynchronous. The sender does not wait for an answer. This choice is also a trade-off against synchronous calls, where the sender waits.

The trading service can tell the other services in two ways. The first way is queues. A queue is a point-to-point channel. The sender knows exactly who receives each message. To reach three services, the sender writes to three separate queues.

Think of it like sending a WhatsApp message to three people one by one. You choose every person before you send.

Queues give you these benefits:

  • Security. The trading service knows every listener. So no unknown service can quietly listen in.
  • Tailored messages. Each queue can carry a different message for a different consumer. A consumer is a service that receives messages.
  • Independent control. You can monitor each queue on its own. You can also scale each queue on its own.

Now the cost. The trading service is tightly coupled to its consumers. Coupling means one part depends on another. Here, the trading service must know how many consumers there are.

Suppose the compliance team asks for updates next month. Then the trading service needs a code change to add a third queue. So queues give you control, but they give up extensibility.

Trade-off analysis, part two: topics

The second way is a topic. A topic uses a broadcast model. The publisher sends a message to the topic. It does not care who is listening. Anyone who is interested can subscribe.

Think of it like an announcement on a railway platform. Everyone on the platform hears it. The announcer did not choose the listeners one by one.

The good side is clear. Suppose compliance wants updates. They subscribe, and the trading service does not change at all. That is low coupling.

There are real bad sides too:

  • You cannot tailor a message for each subscriber. Everyone gets the same message.
  • Scaling is all or nothing. There is only one thing to scale.
  • Anyone can subscribe without the publisher knowing. In some places, this is a security risk.

Choosing between the two

Imagine two requirements. The first says, "Security is the top priority." This points to queues. The publisher controls exactly who receives what.

The second says, "We will add more downstream services later. They need different data." This points to topics. New listeners cost nothing to add.

One answer cannot satisfy both. So you must decide which requirement matters more to the business.

Deciding: it depends on the business

So which one wins? The honest answer is "it depends". Look at what the business cares about most. If security is the top priority, queues are the safer choice.

Two Many Sneakers is growing fast. Many services are interested in its trades. So extensibility is its biggest need, and topics win.

Time matters too. Suppose you must launch quickly. Then a simple design may be better than one that promises extra availability. For example, a promise of "three nines" means the system works 99.9% of the time. That promise has little value if you have no customers yet.

The lesson is clear. Trade-off analysis does not find a perfect answer. It finds the answer that fits your situation best.

How to run a trade-off analysis

You can use formal methods to organise this work. Here are two of them:

  • The Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) walks stakeholders through business drivers, the "-ilities", and the proposed architecture. Then it tests the design against scenarios. One weak point is that it assumes the design will not change much.
  • The Cost Benefit Analysis Method (CBAM) looks at the cost of reaching a given "-ility".

Stakeholders are people who care about the system, such as users and managers. The "-ilities" are qualities like scalability and reliability.

The authors suggest you look at both methods. You can even combine them. ATAM can help with trade-off analysis. CBAM can help you get the best return on investment.

Remember that the method is only a tool. You may run the analysis many times as you learn more. The goal is an architecture that serves the business.

Think of trade-off analysis like buying a vehicle for your family. A sports bike is fast but carries very little. A big car carries the whole family but is slow in traffic. You do not ask which one is "best". You ask which one fits the trips you really make. You also ask what you are ready to give up.

What is an architectural decision?

Architects make many decisions. Some are big and some are small tool choices. Which ones are architectural? In most cases, any choice that affects the structure of the system is an architectural decision.

Most of these decisions are about structure, but not all of them. One example is the choice between a monolith and microservices. A monolith is one big application that you deploy as one unit. Microservices are many small services. This choice shapes everything that comes after.

Some decisions are about a characteristic. Suppose security is the top priority. You might decide to require encryption on every connection. That decision shapes the architecture.

Other decisions pick a tool or a process. They help you reach a characteristic in an indirect way. Both kinds count.

Architectural decisions are guideposts, not strict rules. They tell teams which limits they must respect. Teams are still free to work out the details.

A decision starts with a trade-off analysis. Then you weigh the good and bad sides against other limits. These are:

  • Business and end-user needs
  • Architectural characteristics
  • Technical feasibility, which means whether it can really be built
  • Time and budget
  • Development concerns

Only then do you decide. Decisions are one of the dimensions needed to describe an architecture.

Michael Nygard wrote the book Release It!. He says a decision is architecturally significant if it affects how the rest of the project runs. It is also significant if it changes any of these parts of the system:

  • The structure
  • The non-functional qualities
  • The dependencies
  • The interfaces
  • The build techniques

This is a useful test to remember.

The Second Law: why beats how

Imagine your team does a trade-off analysis and decides to add a cache. A cache is a fast temporary store that makes reading data quicker. The "what" is easy to see. There is now a cache in the system.

But what about the reasons? Which options did you reject? What pressures pushed you to this choice? How did the choice affect the team that built it?

Without these answers, future architects can see what you did. They can even see how you did it. This includes you, one year from now. But they cannot easily see why.

Without the why, they may waste time on options you already rejected. They may also miss a key factor behind the choice.

This gives us the Second Law of Software Architecture: why is more important than how. Write down the reasons for each decision. Then they stay safe as time passes.

The First Law says to weigh trade-offs. The Second Law says to write down what you decided and why. Together they are the main habit of good architects.

Architectural decision records (ADRs)

The tool for saving the why is the architectural decision record, or ADR. An ADR is a short document about one architecture decision. Write one for every important decision.

Over time, your ADRs grow into an architectural decision log. This log is the memory of your project. It is the documentation for the "decisions" dimension of architecture.

An ADR has seven sections:

  1. Title
  2. Status
  3. Context
  4. Decision
  5. Consequences
  6. Governance
  7. Notes

The authors say Governance and Notes are not in the standard ADR template. But they add a lot of value. Let us look at each section. First, a short story about a team in a common debate.

"Why do we need this?"

Two engineers have just finished comparing queues and topics. One is tired and wants to go back to coding. The other points out something. A year from now, a new person will ask why the team chose queues. Nobody will remember the details.

So the team writes an ADR. The tired engineer complains a little. But the document stops the same argument from happening again.

Title: short, numbered, noun-heavy

Every ADR starts with a title that names the decision. Keep it meaningful and short, like a newspaper headline. Most words should be nouns. Leave the details for later sections.

Start the title with a three-digit number. Begin at 001 and count up. This order shows which decisions came first.

If you ever pass 999, you must change many titles and file names. But the authors find three digits are enough.

Each ADR needs its own unique number. If you reuse a number, people get confused when they refer to decisions by number.

Status: where the decision stands

The status shows the stage of the decision. A decision is a process, not one single moment. You will meet these statuses:

  • Request for Comment (RFC). The ADR is a draft that needs feedback. The feedback often comes from other teams or an advisory board. RFCs usually affect several teams. They may also affect a cross-cutting concern like security. Always give an RFC a "respond by" date. Think of it like planning a family function. You make a plan and invite people. You also set a date, so you can book the hall.
  • Proposed. The comments are in. The ADR now waits for formal approval. You can still change the decision if someone finds a serious problem. You are close, but not finished.
  • Accepted. The decision is made. Everyone who had to approve has approved. This tells the implementing team they can start. If no outside feedback is needed, an ADR can go straight to Accepted.
  • Superseded. A newer decision has replaced this one. Most ADRs stay Accepted. But things change. The business may change its priorities, or new laws may arrive. The new ADR becomes Accepted. The old one is marked Superseded.

A survey service changes its database

A customer survey team starts with a relational database to store results. This is ADR 001, and its status is Accepted. Later, the team sees that the data is very varied and changes shape often. So the relational database no longer fits.

They run a new trade-off analysis and choose a document store. They write a new ADR. They mark the old one Superseded. Both ADRs link to each other.

Anyone who finds the old ADR can see it is out of date. They can follow the link to the new decision.

The two-way link is important. It turns your ADR log into a map of what was tried and why it changed. It is part of the project memory.

Do not edit an Accepted ADR to change the decision. Once accepted, the decision is basically locked. If it no longer fits, write a new ADR with the next number. Then mark the old one Superseded.

For example, say ADR 007 stops fitting after you reach ADR 013. If you edit 007, readers will think the new decision came first. So you write ADR 014 instead.

The only expected change to an Accepted ADR is its status moving to Superseded. Adding a few extra facts is fine. Changing the decision itself is not.

Context: the situation behind the decision

Every decision is made in a context, with limits. Think of it like choosing your breakfast. It depended on how hungry you were, what you planned for lunch, and what you wanted to eat. Architecture is the same.

The Context section explains what drove the decision. It lists every factor that shaped it. Technical reasons usually appear here. Cultural or political reasons belong here too. They help the reader see your point of view.

You may want to record the trade-off analysis itself. Then add a separate section called "Alternatives". List each option you considered, with its good and bad sides. This keeps the Context section clean and focused.

Decision: say it plainly, with a reason

The Decision section states the decision and gives the reason for it. Use a firm voice, with words like "we will use". Avoid weak words like "we think" or "we believe".

Suppose the ADR is still an RFC or Proposed. Then the decision is not final. But the section should still state the proposed decision clearly.

Always include the reason. The Second Law says why matters more than how. So the reason is the heart of this section.

You can also name the people or groups who approved it. For example, write "the marketing department requires...". This shows who was involved. The book calls this "covering your assets". It means protecting yourself by showing who agreed.

The dinner decision

Your best friend's birthday is coming. The context is the celebration and the limits around it. The alternatives are the restaurants on your list.

The decision is "We will go to the pan-Asian bistro." The reason is that its menu has vegetarian and gluten-free options. It also allows substitutions for people with diet needs.

A good Decision section reads the same way. It says what you chose and why it beat the others.

An ADR is not an opinion article. Write it like a good news report. Stick to the facts and keep your tone neutral. The reader should trust the record and not feel lectured.

Consequences: the good and the bad

Every decision has consequences. Think of it like a hard workout at the gym. Next day you may feel sore, and also a little proud. Architecture decisions have the same mix.

The Consequences section lists the expected effects, both good and bad. Then everyone can judge if the good is greater than the bad.

Ask yourself these questions:

  • How does this change the work of the implementing team?
  • Does it make testing easier or harder?
  • How will we know the work is done?
  • Does it add or remove infrastructure? What does that involve?
  • Does it affect cross-cutting concerns like security or observability?
  • What does it do to time and money? Does it cost more or save effort?
  • Does it create a one-way path that is hard to undo? For example, the book notes that with queues you cannot control the order of messages.

Observability means you can see what the system is doing inside. Cross-cutting concerns are topics that touch many parts of the system.

It is easy to miss consequences when you work alone. So ask others to review them. Different people see different effects.

Suppose a decision makes the system simpler or cheaper. Then say so. That is worth noting too.

The book gives sample consequences for the sneaker team:

  • Queues add new infrastructure that must be highly available. This means using clustering.
  • Queues mean tighter coupling between services.
  • Any extra downstream consumer will force changes to the trading service.

Think of a bigger kitchen at home. It is good for cooking. But it also means more cleaning, a higher electricity bill, and higher rent. A complete Consequences section lists both the joy and the bills.

Governance: keep the decision alive

A decision is good only if people really follow it. They must keep following it later. Think of a gym membership that you never use. It does nothing for you.

The Governance section explains how you will check that the organisation follows the decision, now and later. There are manual methods and automated methods:

  • Manual methods include pair programming and code reviews.
  • Automated methods include special tests that fail when someone breaks the rule.

Here, governance means keeping the architecture on course. It does not mean following government rules.

Notes: facts about the ADR

The last section records facts about the ADR itself. These are:

  • The original author
  • The approval date
  • Who approved it
  • The superseded date
  • When it was last changed
  • Who changed it
  • What changed

Your tool may already track dates. Still, put this information in the ADR. It repeats what the tool knows. But people find it more easily when it is inside the document.

Where to store ADRs, and names

You have many options. A common choice is plain-text files, such as Markdown or AsciiDoc. You keep them in version control like Git. Version control saves every change to a file. You get a full history for free.

The downside is that people who are not developers may not know version control. If you choose this way, keep the ADRs in their own repository. Do not mix them with source code.

A wiki is another option. Most wikis have a visual editor, so more people can read and write. Make sure the wiki tracks changes. You do not want someone to edit an ADR quietly and no one notices.

Whatever you choose, people must be able to add, edit, and search ADRs easily. Many good ADR efforts die because nobody can find the document when they need it.

Suppose you store ADRs as files. Then use the title as the file name, with the number. For example, take this title: "042: Use queues between the trading and downstream services". Its file name is 042-use-queues-between-the-trading-and-downstream-services.md.

The file name is the number, then the lowercase title with hyphens instead of spaces. Lowercase avoids problems across operating systems. When you sort the folder by name, the decisions appear in order.

Many tools exist for writing ADRs. Some store them inside the source code. Most tools expect one file format or folder layout. So try a tool before you commit to it.

The authors give simple advice. Start without special tools. Learn what your team needs. Only then look for a tool.

How far does an ADR reach?

ADRs can be narrow or wide. Some affect one team. Others affect a whole company.

A famous example is the "Jeff Bezos API mandate" at Amazon. It was a decision that all services must talk to each other only through APIs. It touched the whole organisation.

Cross-team ADRs need a lot of teamwork. They often need approval from a central architecture review board. They usually cover cross-cutting concerns. Examples are how services talk to each other, or which data transfer protocol to use.

ADRs about security or regulatory compliance often cross several teams. Sometimes they cross the whole organisation.

Why ADRs are worth it

ADRs take some effort, but the reward is big. They help you follow the Second Law. They save the "why" along with the "what".

They also build institutional knowledge, which is what an organisation knows and remembers. They help teams learn from one another. The format is only a suggestion, so you can change it.

Keep the ADR process as light as you can. Every extra section adds work. When the paperwork becomes heavy, people stop writing ADRs. Write short, clear entries. A short ADR that gets written is better than a perfect one that is never finished.

The Two Many Sneakers story ends well. Customers love the real-time alerts. The analytics help the security team find sneaker scams early.

The team learned that architecture has no best practices, only trade-offs. They also wrote down their decisions. So people can understand them, question them, and change them later.

Quick recap

  • Software architecture has no universal best practices. Every choice has trade-offs. This is the First Law.
  • Trade-off analysis compares the good and bad sides of each option. You judge them against the qualities the business needs most.
  • Queues give control and security, but they tie you to your consumers.
  • Topics are easy to extend, but every subscriber gets the same message.
  • The Second Law says why is more important than how. So write down the reason for each decision.
  • An ADR has seven sections: Title, Status, Context, Decision, Consequences, Governance, and Notes. Its title starts with a three-digit number.
  • The statuses are RFC, Proposed, Accepted, and Superseded.
  • Never rewrite an Accepted ADR. Write a new ADR that supersedes the old one.

Logical Components

Before you design the technical parts of a system, you must know its working parts. This chapter shows how to find them, give each a clear job, and keep them from getting tangled.

  • Coupling: how much components know about each other and depend on each other.
  • Afferent coupling (CA): the number of components that depend on one component.
  • Efferent coupling (CE): the number of components that one component depends on.
  • Law of Demeter: a rule that says a component should know as little as possible about others.

What is a logical component?

A logical component is a working building block of a system. All the components together describe the problem domain. The problem domain is the business problem that the software solves.

In most programming languages, a logical component is a folder in your source code. Say your order tracking code lives in the folder app/order/tracking. Then you have a logical component that you can call Order Tracking.

Logical and physical architecture

You can look at a system in two ways. The logical architecture shows the building blocks and how they work with each other. The physical architecture shows the technical details.

Those technical details include the architecture style, services, protocols, databases, user interfaces, and API gateways. A service is a small program that does one job. A protocol is a set of rules for how programs talk. An API gateway is a single front door to the services.

The logical view does not care if a component is a database or a service. It only cares what the component does and what it needs. The physical view then gives these components to real services and databases. It is one system seen in two ways.

Think of it like building a house. The logical view is the floor plan. It says the kitchen is next to the dining room and the bedrooms are upstairs. The physical view is the wiring, the pipes, and the brand of the stove. You need the floor plan before you decide where the pipes go.

Case study: online auctions

Adventurous Auctions sells rare trips at live auctions around the country. One trip may be a safari in Tanzania. Another may be a hike to Everest base camp. Now the company also wants to hold these auctions online.

The new system must do these things:

  • Include both in-person bids and online bids in every auction.
  • Scale up to meet demand, so hundreds or even thousands of people can join each auction.
  • Let online users register and give their payment details.
  • Let online users watch live video of the in-person auctions.
  • Let online users see all bids so far, both in the room and online.
  • Let online users bid on trips, just like the people in the room.
  • Find which online bidder bids the asking price first. This is called "winning the bid".
  • Let the auctioneer decide who bid first, if an online bidder and a person in the room bid at the same time.
  • When the auctioneer names an online user as the winner, charge the card and tell the winner. Then move to the next trip.

We will use this system to show every step of building a logical architecture.

The four-step loop

Making a logical architecture takes four steps. You use them for a brand-new system. A brand-new system is also called a greenfield system. You also use them to keep an existing system healthy. The loop runs as long as the system lives.

  1. Identify the initial core components.
  2. Assign requirements to those components.
  3. Analyze each component's roles and responsibilities.
  4. Analyze the architectural characteristics the components must support.

Many teams stop doing these steps after the first release. This is why good systems often become messy. Run the four steps again each time you add a feature or change the system. This keeps each component the right size and doing its job.

Step 1: Find the core components

Your first components will be rough guesses. At this point, you know little about the system. You will probably change the components later. So do not worry about how big or small each one is.

Think of these first components as empty jars. They are placeholders. You will fill them with responsibilities later.

Two common methods reduce the guessing. They are the workflow approach and the actor/action approach. Some other methods look good but cause trouble. We will see one of them soon.

The workflow approach

The workflow approach looks at the main journeys a user takes through the system. Do not try to list every small step at first. Start with the main steps. Then add more detail later. You can also model several workflows to find more starting components.

One bid in an auction

Follow one bidder through the system. The bidder registers, watches the video, places a bid, and sees the bid counted. Then the bidder sees the winner announced and pays.

Each main step suggests a component. You get Registration, Video Streamer, Bid Capture, and Payment. This is our own rough list, not the exact list from the book.

Video Streamer is a real component even if you buy video streaming from another company. It is still part of the logical architecture.

One step does not always match one component. Several steps can point to the same component if they are closely related. The name of each component also matters. A good name tells the reader what the component does in a few words.

The actor/action approach

This method helps when a system has several kinds of users. These users are called actors. First, list the actors. Next, list the main actions each actor takes. Then give each action to a new or an existing component.

A bakery ordering system

A bakery wants customers to browse, order, and pay online for pickup. There are four actors:

  • The customer.
  • The coordinator, who buys ingredients and plans the orders.
  • The baker, who bakes the items.
  • The system, which emails the customers.

Customers browse and order, so you need an Ordering component. The coordinator plans orders, so you need a Scheduling component. The baker reports finished items, so you need a Production component. The system tells customers their order is ready, so you need a Notification component.

You can use both methods together. Start with actors to find the actions. Then use a workflow to put the actions in the order they usually happen.

Event storming is a group workshop method from domain-driven design. It goes further than actor/action. It also covers commands, aggregates, and views. Actor/action gives you only the domain events and the actors from event storming.

The entity trap

The entity trap is a method you should avoid. It names components after the main data items in the system. For example, you may create a "Bid Manager" for bids and an "Order Manager" for orders. It is easy to fall into this trap, and it causes problems.

First, a name like "Bid Manager" is too vague. You cannot tell what the component does from its name. Second, such a component becomes a dumping place for everything about that data item.

The component then grows too big. It becomes hard to maintain, hard to scale, and hard to make fault tolerant. Fault tolerant means the system keeps working when a part fails.

Words like manager, supervisor, and handler in a component name are a warning sign. You may be in the entity trap. Check if the component has a clear and specific job.

This warning is a guide, not a strict law. Take a component that holds reference data, such as country codes and store codes. "Reference Data Manager" is a fine name for it. Its job is specific, even though it is general.

Names like "Order Manager" or "Response Handler" are too broad. They do not explain what the component really does.

Step 2: Give requirements to components

Now take each functional requirement or user story. A functional requirement says what the system must do. A user story says the same thing from a user's point of view. For each one, decide which component should own it.

Each component is a folder. The code for a requirement goes into that folder. Every requirement needs a home. If one does not fit any component, you may need a new component.

At the start, requirements come from talks with the business sponsor. For Adventurous Auctions, the first components came from what the company's CIO said about the basic workflow. Then you place each requirement in the component that fits it best.

Step 3: Check roles and jobs

As you give requirements to components, each component gains more jobs. This is normal. But a component can also take on too much. Imagine a Live Auction Session component that handles the bidding, the video, the payments, and the notifications. That is far too much work for one component.

This step helps you catch that problem early. You move some of the work to other components.

Have you ever written a class called Utility? Often it holds many unrelated helper functions. A logical component can become the same kind of junk drawer. If its name is vague and its contents are random, it is doing too much.

The key idea here is cohesion. Cohesion means how closely the operations of a component are related. A component with high cohesion has functions that all belong together. A component with low cohesion is a mixed bag of unrelated jobs.

When you review a component, ask if its functions are closely related. You may find one odd function that does not fit. Or you may find that the component does too much. In both cases, move that work to another component.

Splitting a mixed bag

Suppose a Live Auction Session component handles bid intake, the live video, and credit card payment. Bid intake and live video are about the live event. Taking a payment is about money. It belongs in a Payment component.

Move the payment work out. Now the session component is more focused. You have improved its cohesion by moving one job away.

Step 4: Check key characteristics

The last step checks that each component supports the characteristics that matter most for success. A characteristic is a quality the system must have, such as speed. Most often, you split a component to get better scalability, elasticity, or availability. Sometimes you join components whose functions are tightly linked.

Scalability means the system can handle more users. Elasticity means it can grow and shrink with the load. Availability means it is up and running when people need it.

For Adventurous Auctions, three characteristics are critical:

  • Scalability: the system must handle thousands of bidders per second.
  • Availability: the system must be up during live auctions.
  • Performance: the system must take a bid and get it to the auctioneer as fast as possible.

Now look at the Bid Capture component. It has four jobs:

  1. Accept bids from online users and from the auctioneer for live bidders.
  2. Find which online bid is the highest.
  3. Write all bids to a Bid Tracker database for record keeping.
  4. Tell the auctioneer the highest bid.

The book's fix gives the database job to a new component called Bid Tracker.

Writing to the database inside Bid Capture is a problem. Database connections and speed are limited. So writing every bid there slows the component down. This hurts scalability.

It also adds waiting time before the bid reaches the auctioneer. This hurts performance. And if the database goes down, Bid Capture may stop too. This hurts availability.

The fix is to move the tracking job into its own component. Bid Capture now sends bids to Bid Tracker. It does not wait for the database write to finish. Each component has one clear job. The system can take in more bids, and faster.

Ask three questions about each component. What happens if something it needs fails? How fast must each step run? How much load must each step handle? The answers show where to split a component.

Component coupling

As you find components, you must also see how they depend on each other. This is called component coupling. It means how much components know about each other and rely on each other. The more they interact, the more tightly coupled the system is. A tightly coupled system is harder to change.

Imagine drawing every dependency in a real system. Often it looks like the tangled electric wires on a street pole. Architects call this a "big ball of mud". It means so many components depend on each other that the diagram is a tangled mess. You want to avoid it. So watch the links between components closely.

There are two kinds of coupling to watch. They are afferent and efferent. The names sound hard, but the ideas are simple.

Afferent coupling: who needs me?

Afferent coupling is how much other components depend on one target component. You count the components that rely on it. It is also called fan-in or incoming coupling. Code analysis tools usually write it as CA.

Think of parents. Their children and even the family dog all depend on them. They need food, a safe home, and a ride to practice. The more things depend on you, the higher your afferent coupling.

The word afferent means "carrying toward". It is like the nerves that carry signals to your brain.

A profile component with two users

In Adventurous Auctions, two components need bidder profile information. They are the Auction Registration component and the Automatic Payment component. Both get it from the Bidder Profile component.

Two components depend on Bidder Profile. So Bidder Profile has an afferent coupling of 2.

Efferent coupling: whom do I need?

Efferent coupling is the opposite. It counts how many components one target component depends on. It is also called fan-out or outgoing coupling. Tools usually write it as CE.

Think of a child. A child depends on parents, teachers, friends, and classmates for different things. That child has a high efferent coupling.

Accepting a bid

A bidder named Kate places a bid. The Bid Capture component needs the Bid Streamer and the Bid Tracker to finish the job. It depends on two other components. So its efferent coupling is 2.

Measuring coupling

You can measure the coupling of one component in three ways. The first is total afferent coupling (CA), which counts incoming links. The second is total efferent coupling (CE), which counts outgoing links.

The third is total coupling (CT). It is the sum of the two, so CT equals CA plus CE. These numbers show which components have the highest and lowest coupling. They also show how coupled the whole system is.

A tiny three-component system

Checkout  -> Pricing
Checkout  -> Inventory
Reports   -> Pricing

Pricing:   CA = 2 (Checkout, Reports), CE = 0, CT = 2
Checkout:  CA = 0, CE = 2 (Pricing, Inventory), CT = 2
Inventory: CA = 1 (Checkout), CE = 0, CT = 1

Pricing has the most incoming links. So a change there spreads the furthest. The number is a clue about where to be careful.

Law of Demeter: know less

Developers are often told to build loosely coupled systems. But they are rarely shown how. One technique you can try is the Law of Demeter. It is also called the Principle of Least Knowledge.

The name comes from Demeter, the Greek goddess of farming. She gave grain to people. She did not need to know what they did with it.

Components work the same way. The more a component knows about other components, the more coupled it is. It also gets more coupled when it knows more about what must happen in the system. Reduce what it knows, and you reduce its coupling.

The Order Placement component

Imagine an Order Placement component that handles checkout. It also knows the exact steps for low stock. It checks the warehouse, marks the item, and emails the supplier.

In the book's version of this system, Order Placement has a total coupling of 5. That is high next to the other components. It is also not balanced.

Now move the low-stock knowledge into the Inventory Management component. Order Placement only asks Inventory if the item is available. Order Placement knows less, so its coupling drops. Inventory Management knows more, so its coupling rises.

Less knowledge means less coupling. More knowledge means more coupling.

Notice that the total coupling of the system did not change. You did not remove the knowledge. You only moved it to another component. The gain is that the load is now balanced. No single component is in charge of everything.

Think of it like a small restaurant. Suppose the waiter also cooks, washes dishes, and runs the cash counter. The waiter knows far too much about the business. Split the jobs, and each person knows only their own part. All the jobs are still needed. But nobody has to know the whole kitchen.

Loose coupling has a cost

Loose coupling is not free. Compare two designs for an order system. In the tightly coupled design, Order Placement knows every step. To learn what happens when a customer places an order, you read one component.

But there is a cost. If pricing or supplier ordering changes, Order Placement may need a change too.

In the loosely coupled design, the knowledge is spread across components. No single component knows all the steps. A change to pricing or supplier ordering no longer forces a change in Order Placement.

But now you must visit many components to understand the full workflow. Also, in the book's example, Order Placement now depends on four other components. A change in any one of them could break it.

This is the First Law of Software Architecture again. Everything in software architecture is a trade-off. Loose coupling reduces dependencies. But it spreads the workflow knowledge across the system. That makes the knowledge harder to manage and control.

Two components are coupled if a change in one might cause a change in the other. Use this test to find coupling you may have missed.

Final words on components

You can now find logical components and the links between them. This is the base for the rest of your architecture. A system seen as a set of components is easier to understand. You can see its overall shape and how it works.

The next steps cover the technical details. They are architecture styles, services, databases, and communication protocols.

Quick recap

  • A logical component is a working building block, usually a source code folder with a clear name.
  • Build a logical architecture in four repeating steps: find components, give requirements, check jobs, and check characteristics.
  • Use the workflow approach and the actor/action approach to find starting components.
  • Avoid the entity trap, because components named after data items become vague and too big.
  • Cohesion shows how related a component's functions are, so move odd functions elsewhere.
  • CA counts what depends on you, CE counts what you depend on, and CT is CA plus CE.
  • The Law of Demeter says less knowledge means less coupling.
  • Moving knowledge balances the load but does not remove it, and loose coupling spreads workflow knowledge.

Architectural Styles: A Simple Map

There are hundreds of architectural styles, and new ones appear every year. This chapter gives you a simple map to sort them. Then you can see what a style does well and where it causes pain.

In this chapter

  1. Why you need a map
  2. Question one: how is code divided?
  3. Question two: how is it deployed?
  4. The monolith: good and bad
  5. The distributed system: good and bad
  6. Side-by-side comparison
  7. No winners, only trade-offs
  • Architectural style: a known way to organize a whole system.
  • Partitioning: splitting a system into smaller pieces.
  • Domain: the business area your software is about, such as orders or payments.
  • Monolith: a system that you ship and run as one single unit.
  • Distributed system: a system made of many units that talk over a network.
  • Coupling: how strongly one part depends on another part.
  • Trade-off: you gain something good but you give up something else.

Why you need a map

Think of the houses in your city. Some are tiny, some are tall, and some use old village designs. Each one was shaped by the weather, the land, and the people. Software styles are like that too.

You will never remember every style, and you do not need to. You only need a few questions to place any style you meet. The book gives you two questions. The first is about how the code is divided. The second is about how the code is shipped.

Question one: how is code divided?

This is called partitioning. It means splitting a system into pieces. There are two main ways to do it.

Technical partitioning

In a technically partitioned system, you split the pieces by technical job. One piece talks to users. This is the presentation piece. One piece applies the business rules. This is the business or services piece. One piece talks to the database. This is the persistence piece.

This idea is called separation of concerns. Most people draw it as horizontal layers, one on top of the other.

Think of it like a big wedding kitchen. One group only cuts vegetables. One group only cooks. One group only serves. Each group has one technical job.

Domain partitioning

In a domain partitioned system, you split the pieces by the problem you solve. The "domain" is what your software is about. Examples are orders, customers, or payments.

Each domain gets its own bundle of code. Inside a bundle you may still have screens and a database. The business idea comes first. The technical parts come second.

Think of it like a food court. Each stall sells one kind of food, such as dosa, biryani, or chaat. Inside each stall there may be a cook and a cashier. But the stall is built around the food, not around the job.

Customer code, two ways

Imagine a shop app with a customer feature. A technically partitioned project may group code like this: app.presentation.customer and app.services.customer.

A domain partitioned project turns this around: app.customer.presentation and app.customer.services. Customer code exists in both. The first sorts by technical role. The second sorts by business area.

Technical partitioning works well when your team is split by skill. For example, you may have frontend experts, backend developers, and database administrators. Domain partitioning matches the real problem better. Neither one is always better. Your team and your problem decide.

Question two: how is it deployed?

Deployment means putting your software on servers so people can use it. The question is how many units you ship. Do you ship everything as one package? Or do you ship several packages that run separately?

Monolithic deployment

A monolithic system is delivered as one unit. The whole application runs as one process. A process is one running program.

In the Java world, this may be one JAR or WAR file. In the .NET world, it may be one executable file.

A small shop app

Your shop app has login, a shopping cart, and a report screen. In a monolith, all three live in one codebase. They are built into one file and run in one process. To deploy a fix to the cart, you must deploy the whole thing again.

Distributed deployment

A distributed system splits its logical pieces into several units. The units are usually smaller. Each unit runs in its own process. The units talk to each other over a network.

The same shop, split

Now login, cart, and report are three separate services. Each one has its own deployment. The cart asks login over the network to check who you are. You can deploy the cart again without touching login.

The monolith: good and bad

Monoliths have a bad name, but they have real strengths. Everything runs in one process, so they are simpler to build at first. Everything ships together, so finding an error is easier. You follow one stack trace from start to end. A stack trace is the list of steps the program took before it failed.

Monoliths are often cheaper too. This makes them a strong choice when you must reach the market quickly. The book names five strengths:

  • Simplicity: usually one codebase, which is easy to build and understand.
  • Cost: cheaper to build and run, because it needs less infrastructure.
  • Feasibility: good when you are in a hurry or trying an idea.
  • Debuggability: all code is in one place, so a stack trace leads you to the problem.
  • Reliability: few or no network calls, so fewer things can fail at first.

The trouble starts as the app grows. Many of those strengths become weak points:

  • Scalability: you scale all or nothing. If the report feature uses too much memory, you must scale the whole app.
  • Reliability: it deploys as one unit. So one bug that harms the service hits the whole monolith.
  • Evolvability: changes get harder as the app grows. With one codebase, you cannot give different domains different technology stacks.
  • Deployability: every change means deploying the whole application again. This adds risk.

The distributed system: good and bad

Distributed systems are strong where monoliths are weak. Each part is deployed on its own. So you can scale only the busy parts.

The parts are physically separate. So they are usually loosely coupled. This means a change in one part rarely breaks another. A failure in one service can often be kept inside that service. It does not crash everything.

Each deployment serves only a few components. So testing is much easier, even when the app grows. Low coupling also means high modularity. Modularity means the system is made of parts that you can change one by one.

Many small units are easy to deploy. This fits modern habits like continuous integration, continuous deployment, and automated testing.

But distributed systems have a high price. They depend on the network. The network is not reliable, not instant, and not free.

There is a famous list called "The Fallacies of Distributed Computing". L. Peter Deutsch and others at Sun Microsystems made it in the 1990s. It lists the false ideas developers often believe about networks. Read it before you start. The book names four costs:

  • Performance: many small services talking over the network can slow things down.
  • Cost: you need more servers, and you must set up and maintain network infrastructure.
  • Simplicity: distributed systems are the opposite of simple. This is true from understanding them to debugging them.
  • Debuggability: an error can happen in any service that touched a request. So tracing it is hard. You need careful logging, and usually log aggregation. Log aggregation means collecting logs from all services in one place. This adds cost.

It is easy to underestimate how hard distributed computing is. Teams often pick a distributed design because it sounds modern. Later they find they bought a lot of network complexity that they did not need.

The book also says there are domain-specific architectures for special problems. It does not cover them. When you read about a new style, ask which box it lives in. That one question tells you a lot about its strengths and weaknesses.

No winners, only trade-offs

You may want to rank these styles from best to worst. The book refuses to do this, and so should you. A style is a set of trade-offs.

Your choice depends on your domain, your team, and your budget. It also depends on the architectural characteristics your system needs. These are qualities such as speed, reliability, or the ability to change quickly. Match the style to these needs, not to the hype.

The book tells this as a fireside chat. A monolith and a distributed system talk to each other. The monolith says, "I am simpler, cheaper, and faster to start." The distributed system replies, "True, but I can keep growing when you cannot." Both are right. The real question is which one you will need next year.

  • Technical partitioning groups code by job, such as presentation, business rules, and persistence.
  • Domain partitioning groups code by business area.
  • A monolith ships as one unit and runs as one process. It is simple and cheap early, but it scales all or nothing.
  • A distributed system has separate units that talk over a network. It scales well and contains failures. But it costs more and is hard to debug.
  • The network is the hidden danger of distributed systems. Expect it to fail or be slow.
  • Every style is a set of trade-offs. No style is best for everyone. Pick the best fit for your situation.

Layered Architecture: Neat Stacked Layers

You have a simple project and a tight deadline. Many teams choose the layered architecture first, and for good reason. It is easy to understand and easy to build. We will follow a small sandwich shop to see how it works.

Ask the right question first

Before you pick an architecture, ask how long the system will live. Suppose you build something to throw away, like a quick experiment. Then you can skip most of the planning.

If the system will stay for years, choose the simplest architecture that still gives real organization. It must also not slow your delivery a lot. The layered architecture lives in that middle place.

Meet Naan & Pop

Sangita runs Naan & Pop. It is a small, cheerful restaurant that serves flatbread sandwiches and sodas. She wants a website where customers can order online. The budget is small. The restaurant is already open, so every day without the site means lost sales. She gives the development team three requirements.

  • Time to market. Put the site online fast, and keep it simple.
  • Separation of responsibilities. The company has part-time specialists, such as UI designers and database administrators. So each part of the system should be easy to give to the right person.
  • Simple, yet extensible. She wants to add to the online shop later. She also wants to reuse parts of the system.

Why not use a framework?

One developer says to use an existing framework and finish. A framework is a ready-made set of code to build on. That would meet the speed goal. But frameworks can be rigid, and Sangita wants room to grow.

Later she may want a mobile interface or a link to a delivery service. A pile of simple code can make that hard. The team needs a simple style that still keeps the parts of the app separate.

An idea from MVC

The team first thinks of the Model-View-Controller pattern. It is a famous design pattern. A design pattern is a proven answer to a common problem in writing code. MVC splits the work by purpose:

  • The model holds the business logic and the core data of the app.
  • The view is the user interface. It is the part people see and touch.
  • The controller handles the flow. It takes a request and uses the model to do the work. Then it picks the view to show the result.

MVC is about logical roles inside your code. Architecture must go further. It must deal with real things like browsers, phones, and databases. So the team keeps the same idea of separation and turns it into physical layers.

The three classic layers

A typical layered architecture has three main layers. They are stacked from top to bottom.

  • Presentation layer. This is the top layer. It talks to the user and shows the screens. It plays the same role as the view in MVC.
  • Workflow layer. It is also called the business rules layer. It takes each request from the layer above and applies the rules. Then it sends back a response.
  • Persistence layer. It is also called the data access layer. It gets and saves data in the database. It gives the results to the workflow layer.
  Browser or phone app
          |
  Presentation layer    (screens, forms, what the user sees)
          |
  Workflow layer        (business rules, order logic)
          |
  Persistence layer     (reads and writes the data)

In code, these layers become packages or namespaces. Java uses packages. .NET uses namespaces. For Naan & Pop, this looks like com.naanpop.orderapp.presentation, .workflow, .model, and .persistence. The folders on disk usually match this, because packages often map to the filesystem.

The layered architecture is a technically partitioned style. It is usually deployed as a monolith. This means the whole system, code and database, ships as one unit.

The three layers are common, but they are not required. Most apps have at least some of this split. The UI is apart from the core logic. The core logic is apart from the database.

The idea of layers is also very old. So the book thinks layers inspired MVC, and not the other way round.

How a request moves

Think of it like a relay race in which every runner touches the baton. A customer clicks "Place order". The presentation layer takes the request. It passes it down to the workflow layer, which applies the business rules.

If the order must be saved, the request goes down to the persistence layer and into the database. Then the answer travels back up the same path to the customer's screen.

A bonus points layer

Picture a hotel loyalty program. Each purchase can earn bonus points. The points depend on the membership level, the years of membership, and many other rules. One team kept all that math in a single "bonus" layer.

Every purchase went through it. So the calculations lived in one place. Layers are not only for the UI and the database. You can add a layer for any job that every request needs.

Where do domain pieces go?

Earlier in the book, the team found logical components for the problem. Examples are Delivery Address, Billing Address, Sales Promotion, Blacklisted Customers, and Frequent Diner Rewards.

But layers slice by technical ability, not by domain. So each logical component is split into its workflow and its entities. Those parts are then placed across the layers. Some components land in only one or two layers. Some span several.

Why find logical components first? Because they describe the problem. Mapping them onto any architecture means giving up something. The next chapter shows a more direct mapping, with trade-offs of its own.

Adding layers on purpose

The basic three layers are common, but they are not required. Teams add layers when they need a new responsibility. Two popular examples are:

  • A services layer. It gives other applications, such as business partners, a clean way to talk to your app.
  • An integration layer. It handles links to other systems, such as a third-party delivery service.

There is one golden rule. Every request passes through every layer. So a layer should hold something that happens to every request. A layer that matters only in a few cases will slow everything else down.

Layers in real deployments

The layered monolith describes the logical shape of the system. You can still deploy it in different physical setups. Each one has its own trade-offs.

  • Two-tier, also called client/server. Presentation, business rules, and persistence are in a single deployment unit. This unit talks to the database over a local network. Think of company accounting software that runs as a desktop app on a shared database.
    • Pros: simple, short time to market, and fewer tiers tend to give better performance.
    • Con: poorer scalability.
  • Three-tier, the classic web setup. The presentation layer is its own unit. It is often built with a different tech stack, such as HTML and JavaScript. An application server holds business rules and persistence, for example in Java. It uses a relational database. The book gives a high-scale streaming video service as the example.
    • Pros: good separation and better scalability.
    • Cons: more parts mean more complexity, which slows time to market.
  • Embedded or mobile. Because of physical limits, all the layers end up in one deployment. The database is included. This fits embedded systems and mobile apps, where a network connection may be weak or missing. Examples are a mobile game or a soda machine.
    • Pro: self-contained, with the fewest parts.
    • Cons: hard to extend, and hard to get high scalability or performance.

The book's point is simple. All of them have trade-offs, like everything in architecture.

Why teams like it

The layered architecture has many good points. These are the main reasons it keeps coming back.

  • Feasibility. It is simple and has few moving parts. So you can build small systems fast.
  • Popularity. It is simple. It maps closely to MVC. It is so common that teams build simple projects fast with it. Many companies already split people by skill, which matches the layers.
  • Specialization. You can split teams into specialists, such as UI designers, coders, and database experts. These specialists can share skills across projects.
  • Technical reuse. All your persistence code lives in one layer. So other parts of the system can find and reuse it. Several teams can share the same data layer.
  • Physical match. Layers often map to real separations. An example is a JavaScript front end, a Java back end, and a MySQL database.
  • Familiar shape. Developers already know the MVC idea. So the layered design feels natural.

A mobile version arrives

Later, Naan & Pop wants a separate mobile interface. The screens live in their own layer. So the team builds a new presentation layer for mobile. The workflow and persistence layers stay the same.

This is the layered architecture at its best. A new kind of user interface affects only one layer.

The catch: domain is spread out

Here is the big trade-off. Layers slice the system by technical ability. But your business logic is not technical. One business feature, such as "Place order", needs a screen, some workflow rules, and a database table. So that feature is spread across every layer.

The book describes this as two philosophies meeting. Domain-driven design separates by business area. Layers separate by technology. A layered system can host a domain. But the domain will likely have to split across the layers.

This makes technical changes easy and domain changes painful. If you change the screen technology or the database, you touch one layer. But suppose you add a new thing to sell, like pizza with custom toppings. This change can spread through the screens, the rules, and the data at once.

Layered architectures make technical changes easier but domain changes harder. Suppose your business keeps changing its products, rules, or processes. Then expect the layers to work against you. This is a sign to look at a style that suits domain change better.

Think of it like a tiered wedding cake. You can change the icing without touching the sponge. But suppose you want a new flavour. It changes the batter, the cream, and the decoration all at once. Then you must work on every tier.

Strengths and weaknesses

Let us sum up the good and bad points. The layered architecture is one of the oldest recognizable styles.

Strengths:

  • Feasibility: it is simple, so it suits tight time and budget.
  • Technical partitioning: common abilities, like a shared persistence layer, are built once. Many teams reuse them.
  • Data-heavy work: data processing sits in a single database that is built for it.
  • Performance: a well-designed layered monolith makes no network calls.
  • Quick builds: small systems are fast to build.
  • Small size: keeping the system small avoids many of the weaknesses below. The book calls this "lean and mean", which means small and efficient.

Weaknesses: many teams choose this style by habit. They like its simplicity and long history. They do not ask if it fits. The book lists five weak points:

  • Deployability: deployments get more complex as the monolith grows and gains more behaviour.
  • Big ball of mud: everything connects to everything. Without careful control, the system becomes a tangled, highly coupled mess.
  • Scalability: it is like one bucket that keeps filling up. In the end, a resource such as memory runs out.
  • Elasticity: a single process struggles with a sudden rush of users.
  • Testability: high coupling and a big codebase make testing harder over time.

  • The classic layers are presentation, workflow, and persistence. You can add layers like services or integration.

A layered monolith can take you far. But after some time, changes become hard to coordinate. The modular monolith is still one deployment, but its code is organized around the business, not around technology.

  • Deployment: the step where you put the application on a server so people can use it.
  • Domain: the area of business your software works in, such as banking or retail.
  • Subdomain: a smaller part of a domain, such as Payment inside an online store.

  • Public API: the list of functions that other modules are allowed to call.

When layers become a problem

Remember Naan & Pop? Its layered app worked well. Then the owners wanted to add pizza to the menu. That sounds like a small change. But it touches many parts of the ordering side.

  • A new menu listing.
  • Topping customization.
  • Pricing rules.
  • Delivery rules, because nobody wants a cold pizza.
  • How orders are stored.

The recipe and inventory areas barely notice, because a recipe is still just ingredients plus steps. In a layered app, each of these parts lives in a different layer. So the change crosses the desk of every specialist.

A team member says it plainly. When a layered design spreads the business domain across all layers, every domain change needs a lot of coordination. The layered design was fast to start, and it still has its place. But now the team must grow the architecture so that such changes become easier.

What is a modular monolith?

A modular monolith is still one deployment. It usually uses one database, just like a layered monolith. The big difference is how the code is split. You do not split by technical job. You split by business area, using modules.

Every business lives in some domain, such as banking, education, or retail. A big domain breaks into smaller parts called subdomains. The book uses the two words in the same way. You can do the same, as long as both mean business areas.

An online store

Your online store is one domain. Inside it you may have subdomains for Order Placement, Payment, and Inventory Management. A modular monolith makes each subdomain a module. The whole store is still deployed as one unit. Usually it is one codebase, and each module sits in its own namespace.

So what is a module? It is a way to organize code into an independent unit. That unit handles one piece of functionality. Some languages give you packages or namespaces to help. A namespace is a named box that keeps related code together. Every layer in a layered design is also a module. The difference is the basis of the split. One is split by technical job. The other is split by business area.

Vertical slices, not layers

Think of a vada pav. When you eat it, one bite goes through the bun, the vada, and the chutney together. A modular monolith works the same way. A layered design has horizontal layers, cut by technical role. A modular monolith has vertical slices, cut by business area. Each slice sits in one module. It holds all the business functions for that area.

For example, order placement, order completion, and order delivery can all sit in one Order Placement module. The people who work on that module can own the full slice. They own everything from the screen down to the data.

Cross-functional teams

Vertical slices change the shape of teams. You do not have one team for UI and another team for databases. Instead you have cross-functional teams. Each such team owns one or more domains. It has all the skills it needs to deliver changes in that domain. This makes domain changes easier to coordinate, because one team owns the whole change.

Cross-functional teams are not just a fancy business phrase. They can be hard to build when your people are used to working in only one technical area.

Layers inside modules

You may ask: does each module still have layers? Yes, it can. A module still has to accept a request, apply business logic, and store data. So it may still have presentation, workflow, and persistence parts. Persistence means saving data.

The key difference is that these layers are now an implementation detail of the module. That means they are the private business of that module. From far away, you see the business split into subdomains. The whole architecture is partitioned by domain, even if the inside of a module looks layered.

Keeping modules separate

Here is the hard part. Everything sits in one codebase. So it is very easy for one module to reach into another. Say the ordering code needs to know if the kitchen still has mushrooms. Someone may call the inventory code directly. If this happens everywhere, every module depends on every other module. The code becomes a tangled mess with no clear parts. The modularity is gone.

The aim is to keep modules loosely coupled. That means a change in one module does not affect the others. A good module offers a public API. This is the set of functions that other modules may call. Everything else stays private. Treat each module like a separate service that hides its inside. It is not truly separate, because it is still shipped in the same deployment.

Asking inventory politely

The Order module needs to know if mushrooms are in stock. It does not touch the internal tables or classes of the Inventory module. Instead, it calls the public API of the Inventory module and asks, "Do we have mushrooms?" The inventory team can change its inside tomorrow. If the public API stays the same, ordering keeps working.

The auto-import feature of your IDE is not your friend here. An IDE is the tool where you write code. It is easy to import a class from another module by mistake. Check the imports during code review.

Ways to protect module boundaries

Good intentions are not enough. You need tools that keep the boundaries in place. The book describes several options. You can combine them.

  • Language features. Java has the Java Platform Module System (JPMS). It keeps modules isolated from each other. .NET has namespaces with the internal keyword for a similar purpose.
  • Separate subprojects. Put each module in its own folder with its own build file. Build tools such as Gradle support these multimodule projects. Some teams even use separate repositories. They join everything at build time.
  • Governance tools. These tools check your architecture rules. ArchUnit for Java and ArchUnitNET for .NET can fail your build if one module reaches into another. For TypeScript and JavaScript, ts-arch does a similar job.

You do not have to pick only one. You can use one or several together.

You still deploy one monolith. So you bring the modules together at build time. A monolithic deployment does not need a monolithic codebase. These techniques also help in a layered app. So they are worth learning for any style.

Modular database too

Separate code is not enough if all the data is still mixed. Suppose every module reads and writes the same tables. Then you have only moved the tangle into the database. The rule is simple: every module should access only its own tables.

In practice, you make one schema for each module. A schema is a named group of tables. Data that belongs to a module lives only in that module's tables. You can see the separation in the database itself, as different tables and maybe different schemas.

A single database has one good side. It is simple. You do not worry about distributed transactions or eventual consistency. Most developers are also comfortable with it. Modularizing the data is what you do when you want modularity at every level.

A schema per module

orders_schema      -> orders, order_items
recipes_schema     -> recipes, recipe_steps
inventory_schema   -> ingredients, stock_levels

Note that order_items may store the ID of a recipe item. This is not a foreign key reference. It does not join to the recipes table. Suppose the Order module needs the name or price of the recipe. Then it calls the API of the Recipe module with that ID.

Watch out for SQL joins across module boundaries. A join combines rows from two tables. It is easy to join tables that belong to different modules by mistake. Each such join quietly ties the two modules together again. Storing an ID from another module is fine. Joining into the tables of that module is not.

Start with code first

Most developers are not used to thinking in vertical business slices. Modularizing the database is an even bigger step. You do not have to do everything at once. Start by modularizing the code. Later, the team will be used to thinking in modules. Then see if it helps to take the idea into the database. Grow the architecture over time. Do not try to make it perfect on day one.

It is also common to start with a layered monolith. As the application grows, you refactor it into a modular one. Refactoring means changing the code structure without changing what it does. This is a normal path.

Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths:

  • Domain partitioning. Components follow business areas. Teams can become experts in one domain.
  • Domain-based alignment. Cross-functional teams match the business, not technical roles.
  • Performance. Like most monoliths, it is fast. No network calls run between modules. Data processing happens in one place.
  • Maintainability. Changes stay inside one domain. So the code is easier to maintain, if changes do not spill across domains.
  • Testability. A change is limited to one module, so testing is easier. A team that knows its subdomain well can build integration, smoke, and end-to-end tests for it.

Weaknesses:

  • Hard to reuse. To share logic between modules, you must move it out as a shared dependency. This adds coupling.
  • One set of characteristics. The whole application gets one set of architectural characteristics. This is true even if one business area needs something different.
  • Weak boundaries. It is easy to blur the lines between modules by mistake. The database is even harder to govern than the code.
  • Operational limits. It is still a monolith. So elasticity and fault tolerance are hard to achieve.

The Going Green problem

Going Green is a fast-moving startup. It buys old cell phones, music players, and other small electronics. Then it recycles them or sells them again. The business earns money when it resells the devices with the highest value. Newer devices are usually in better shape and worth more. The company earns more when it adds assessment rules for new devices quickly.

The architects designed a system with three parts. Each part needs different capabilities. The part that matters here is the device assessment service. It works out what a device is worth. New device configurations arrive at least a few times a month. Sometimes they arrive a few times a week.

The team must also make sure that support for a new device does not break support for old devices. So the problem has two sides. The system must change fast, and it must stay stable.

The team chose the microkernel architecture. This style makes it easier to design and add capabilities using plugins. A plugin is a small separate piece of code that adds one capability to the system.

Two parts: core and plugins

The name comes from the design of operating systems. The kernel of an operating system is very small. It offers only the most basic functions. Everything else connects around it. A microkernel architecture has the same shape. It has two primary parts:

  • The core. It holds the basic capabilities and the stable logic of the system.
  • Plugins. There can be any number of them. They add or change behaviors on top of the core.

The device assessment core

The core knows the general rules for assessing any device. Examples are its age, its condition, and its model number. For each type of device, the core gives the work to a plugin made for that device. The plugin runs the rules for that device. Then it returns the estimated resale value. To support a new device, you write a new plugin. You do not change the core.

Partitioning and deployment

Most microkernel systems are technically partitioned. The core can be split into layers, such as presentation and business logic. But the style can also be partitioned by domain, depending on how you design the plugins.

Usually the deployment is monolithic. In some cases a distributed model makes more sense. So the style sits between monolithic and distributed. It often appears in hybrid architectures where customization matters. Inside the core, you are free to organize things your way. You can use layers or domain-style components.

Plugin support is not enough

Much software supports plugins. Examples are IDEs, web browsers, and build tools. But plugin support does not make a system a microkernel. The deciding factor is the core.

There is no official dividing line. So the book suggests you judge how "microkernel-like" a system is. This is often called "microkern-ality". Two questions help:

  • How useful is the core alone, without any plugins?
  • How volatile is the core? In other words, how often does it need to change?

In extreme microkernels, the core can do very little useful work until plugins are installed. The classic case is a core that is small and stable. It is only really useful with plugins.

Microkernel and microservices are not the same thing. The similar names are only a coincidence. Microkernel comes from the design of operating systems. Microservices are named for their small, separate deployment units in a distributed system.

Plugin contracts

The core is the place where plugins connect. The link is usually an interface. An interface is a contract. It says which methods a plugin must provide. The plugin implements the interface. The core calls the plugin through it. The core does not need to know the inside details of the plugin.

In a typical microkernel, talk happens between the core and each plugin. It does not happen between plugins. A plugin such as PluginA neither knows nor cares about the others. If plugins ever need to talk, the core has to sit in the middle.

A/B testing

Plugins are common in A/B testing. A/B testing means you try two versions of a feature and compare them. The architects keep the old behavior in PluginA. They add the new behavior in PluginB. Then they decide which one to call. Neither plugin needs to know that the other exists.

Encapsulated or distributed plugins

There are two broad ways to run plugins. They match the two deployment models from Chapter 5.

  • Encapsulated plugins live inside a monolith. You build each one as a native component of the platform. Examples are a JAR for Java, a DLL for .NET, or a gem for Ruby. They run in the same process as the core. They use the same language.
  • Distributed plugins run as separate services. The core reaches them through web endpoints, event queues, and similar ways. Because they are separate, they can be written in different languages. The only need is that the core can call them over a network.

Calls to distributed plugins can be synchronous or asynchronous. In a synchronous call, the core waits for an answer. In an asynchronous call, the core sends a message and keeps working. Chapter 11 explains this choice in more detail.

For monoliths, asynchronous plugin calls often use threads. For distributed plugins, they often use messaging. Either way, distributed plugins can use any technology that you can reach over a network.

The choice of Going Green

The team decides to define an interface called DeviceInterface. Each device plugin must implement it. After that, to support a new device, you implement the interface. You also customize the valuation logic for that device.

The team compares the two physical options. In a monolith, plugins load into the same process. This is fast. But a new device means restarting the core to load the new plugin.

A distributed setup has these good sides:

  • No restart is needed, so availability is better.
  • Each plugin can be deployed on its own. This is simpler than redeploying the whole service.
  • It scales better.
  • Future plugins can use other languages.

The cost is network calls. They are slower than calls inside one process.

The team chose distributed plugins for scalability. They chose synchronous calls. The service was already responsive enough. So asynchronous messaging would only add complexity.

A new device at 2 p.m.

A new model of tablet appears in the market. In the monolithic version, the team builds the plugin. Then it restarts the whole assessment service to load the plugin. This causes a short outage. In the distributed version, the team deploys only the tablet plugin. The core keeps running. The other devices never notice.

Should plugins talk to each other?

Plugins can communicate through the core. But you should be careful. Plugins normally talk only to the core. If one plugin needs another, the core must act as a middleman. It becomes an integration layer.

This is possible. The Eclipse IDE does it, so that language tools like compilers and debuggers can work together. But talk between plugins has real costs.

  • It needs consistent contracts between the core and every plugin. Later, these contracts need versions. Eclipse has transitive dependencies between components. This is one reason it is complex. A transitive dependency means A needs B, and B needs C.
  • It creates dependencies between plugins. Every plugin you depend on must be present at runtime. This hurts availability.

PluginX needs PluginY

Suppose PluginX calls PluginY through the core. Now you update PluginY, and the contract between them changes. PluginX may break even though nobody touched it. The core can prevent this only if it manages versions and dependencies. That is extra work. You would not have it if the plugins stayed independent.

Where does the UI go?

It depends. In a monolithic microkernel, the UI is often part of the core. In a distributed microkernel service, other parts of the system usually handle the UI.

The microkernel can also support the UI itself. This helps when you need custom screens for iOS, Android, and the web. Each screen type is its own plugin-like endpoint.

Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths:

  • Custom behavior. It is made for customization, one plugin at a time.
  • Evolvability. You can slowly move the system away from old behaviors. Evolvable designs keep the old behavior only for a short time. This is different from adaptable designs. Plugins make this change clean.
  • Partitioning. The architecture handles customization, not just careful design. If the system is built around plugins, the boundary is clear.
  • Adaptability. You can keep the old plugins while you add new ones.
  • Simple structure. There are only two basic parts. So developers understand it quickly.

Weaknesses:

  • Mismatched volatility. The core should change very little once it is built. If it changes all the time, you probably chose the wrong style. Or you put the wrong things in plugins. A core that changes too often is one of the most common mistakes.
  • Sharing between plugins. It is tempting to share libraries between plugins. But this creates coupling and deployment problems. Be careful.
  • Chatty plugins. When plugins talk to each other a lot, the trade-offs pile up. Some successful systems do this, such as Eclipse. They pay for it with high complexity.
  • Performance. With distributed plugins, network calls can slow things down. It depends on the protocol and on how much data moves between the core and the plugins.

Microservices: Many Small Parts

Business needs change very fast, and your system must keep up. Microservices is a style made for this. It uses many small parts that you can change, grow, and fix on their own.

  • Bounded context: a fence around a service and all of its data.
  • Granularity: how big or small the job of a service is.
  • Orchestration: one central service tells the other services what to do and when, like a manager giving tasks.
  • Choreography: services pass the work to each other without a manager, like relay runners passing a baton.
  • Shared library: a bundle of common code that is copied into each service.
  • Workflow: one business request that needs more than one service to finish.

Case study: MonitorMe

Think of a company called StayHealthy. It makes patient-monitoring systems. Doctors and nurses use the software to watch heart rate, oxygen level, temperature, and blood sugar. They also see if the patient is asleep or awake. When a value crosses a set limit, a person gets an alert at once.

The new version is called MonitorMe. It must read from eight equipment sources. It must keep five minutes of history for every vital sign. It must run one full copy in each hospital. Each hospital can have up to 500 patients.

You are the architect here. Before you choose a style, you need a list of what matters most. The book gives its own top five for MonitorMe:

  • Testability: how easy and complete the testing is.
  • Responsiveness: how fast a medical person hears about a problem.
  • Fault tolerance: the system keeps working when one part breaks.
  • Data integrity: health data must be correct.
  • Concurrency: the system watches many vital signs at the same moment.

The book marks four things as not critical: deployability, abstraction, scalability, and workflow. The requirements do not ask for many changes. Also, the system covers only one hospital at a time. Keep this list in mind. You will see why microservices looks good for these needs.

Someone on your team says, "Microservices is the perfect fit!" Do not agree at once. Ask why. A good architect asks what the style does, what it costs, and if the cost is worth it here.

What is a microservice?

A service is a piece of software that you deploy on its own. It does one business job or one technical job. A service can still be big. A "Monitor All Vital Signs" service may handle heart rate, blood pressure, and temperature. It is still just a service.

A microservice is different. The word micro is about what the service does. It is not about the number of lines of code. A microservice has a single purpose, is deployed on its own, and does one thing well. A "Monitor Heart Rate" service is a microservice. A service for all vital signs is not.

The single-purpose test

The book gives a short list. These jobs have a single purpose, so they can be microservices:

  • Add a movie to my to-watch list.
  • Pay for an order with a credit card.
  • Work out the shipping cost for an order.

These jobs are complex and have many different functions, so they are not microservices:

  • Generate sales forecasts and financial reports.
  • Submit and process a loan application.

Here is our own tip, not the book's. If you need many "and" words to describe the job, it is probably not single-purpose.

Each service owns its data

The second main trait is data ownership. Each microservice is the only one that can read and write its own data. Other services must ask the owner for the data. They must not go into its tables.

Why is the rule so strict? It keeps change under control. Imagine fifty microservices read the same table. One team changes the table. Forty-nine other services may break on the same day. Nobody wants that.

The service and its own data together form one unit. This unit is called a physical bounded context. Think of it as a fence around the service and its data. Inside the fence, the owner can change things freely. Outside, everyone must ask. Remember that the fence holds the service and all of its data.

Sleep Status data

The Monitor Heart Rate service needs to know if the patient is asleep. It does not touch the Sleep Status tables. It asks the Sleep Status service for the answer. Now the Sleep Status team can change its data layout at any time. The heart rate service does not need any change.

If a service reaches into the data of another service, treat it as a warning sign. The fence is what lets each service change on its own.

How small is small?

Granularity means how big or small the scope of a service is. It is not about physical size. A huge codebase can be coarse-grained (wide scope). A tiny codebase can be fine-grained (narrow scope).

Take blood pressure monitoring in MonitorMe. You can design it in three ways:

  • Option 1: one service does all monitoring and alerting for blood pressure.
  • Option 2: two services. One captures and records readings. One analyzes them and raises alarms.
  • Option 3: four services. Each does one job: capture, record, analyze, and alert.

No option is right by default. Option 1 is coarse. Option 3 is fine. This chapter teaches you how to choose.

Going too fine has a name. It is the Grains of Sand antipattern, which means a common bad design habit. The services become as tiny as grains of sand. They must talk to each other all the time to finish one business task. This causes tight coupling, slow replies, and reliability problems. Going too coarse is the opposite mistake. Big services cost more to maintain, test, and scale. That defeats the purpose.

Two kinds of forces help you find the right size. Granularity disintegrators push you to split a service into smaller ones. Granularity integrators push you to join services together. We will use three basic vital signs to see both forces. They are blood pressure, temperature, and heart rate.

Forces that make services smaller

  • Cohesion. A good microservice has functions that belong together. If the functions have little in common, split the service.
  • Fault tolerance and availability. In a big service, one function can crash and bring down all the others. Put the weak function in its own service. Then the rest keep working.
  • Access control. It is hard to protect the sensitive parts of a big service. If you move medical history into its own service, it is easier to limit who can reach it.
  • Code volatility. This means how often code changes. It is also called volatility-based decomposition. Suppose one part changes every week and the rest rarely change. Then every release forces you to test everything. If you separate the fast-changing part, testing stays small.
  • Scalability and throughput. Scalability means the system can handle more load. Throughput means how many requests it handles in a given time. Different parts need different capacity. A heart rate sensor sends a reading every second. Temperature may send one every five minutes. Separate services let each one grow at its own speed.

Heart rate and temperature

Heart rate data comes once a second. Temperature data comes once every five minutes. Suppose both are in one service. That service must handle the heavy traffic of both. If you split them, the heart rate service can be built for heavy traffic. The temperature service can stay small and cheap.

The book's choice for basic vital signs

Heart rate and blood pressure are the critical signs. They must keep working even if temperature monitoring breaks. Heart rate readings come every second. The other readings come every five minutes. The data of each sign is stored separately. The book picks three separate services. The reasons are better fault tolerance, data that already fits separate bounded contexts, and separate scaling for different input rates.

Smaller services also start faster. When a small service restarts, its function comes back sooner for the people who need it.

Forces that make services bigger

  • Database transactions. A transaction is a group of database changes. Either all of them succeed, or all of them are undone. Suppose one request updates several microservices. You cannot put all those updates in one transaction. Each service saves or undoes its own part. Perfect data consistency may matter more than anything else. In that case, join the services so the work happens in one transaction.
  • Data dependencies. If you split a service, you must split its data too. This is painful when the data is tied together. For example, one table may point to another table with a key. Or a customer's details may be spread over many tables. Tied data is one of the most common reasons to keep services together.
  • Workflow and choreography. Suppose one business request needs several services to talk to each other. That is coupling. It hurts performance, because every hop adds network, security, and database delay. It hurts scalability, because all services in the chain must grow together. It hurts fault tolerance, because one dead service breaks the whole request. If these qualities matter a lot, join the chatty services.

The chapter gives a memory aid. Start with coarse-grained services. Move to fine-grained ones when you know the system well. It is easier to split a service you understand than to join many tiny ones you do not.

Finding the right size is always a balance. You weigh each force against the others. You decide which trade-offs matter most. When the trade-offs are big, ask your product owner or business stakeholder. They know which risks the business can accept.

Sharing common code

Some code is needed everywhere. Examples are logging, metrics streaming, user authorization, and date formatting. The book lists more for MonitorMe: observability, logging, auditing, and security. Auditing means a record of who got which alert and when. Security means only approved staff can reach a monitoring service.

A monolith is one big application that you deploy as one unit. In a monolith, you write this code once and all parts use it. In microservices, each service is deployed on its own. So sharing needs more thought.

Imagine the three vital-sign services of MonitorMe all need the same "alert a medical professional" logic. You could copy the code into each service. This is called replication. It works only for code that never changes. When someone fixes a bug in one copy, the other copies become old. So you choose one of two ways to share.

Option A: a shared service

A shared service is its own microservice. Other services call it over the network. The alert logic lives in one place. Each monitoring service calls it when something goes wrong.

Option B: a shared library

A shared library is a packaged bundle of code. A JAR file in Java or a DLL in C# are examples. The book says this is the more common way. The library is added to each microservice when you compile the code. After deployment, every service has its own copy. It calls the code directly, with no network call.

Workflows: one request, many services

A workflow happens when one business request needs more than one microservice. The request usually starts in a user interface. Think of a nurse named Juan who asks, "How is the patient doing today?" The system must get temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure from three services. Juan wants one answer, not three. So something must coordinate the three services. There are two ways to do this. Orchestration is centralized. Choreography is decentralized.

Why split services if it creates workflows? Disintegrators like scalability, fault tolerance, code volatility, and access control can be worth the extra work. The book lists other MonitorMe workflows. One is finding which vital signs are monitored for a patient. Another is registering a new patient. A new patient needs a name, identity, demographics, and medical history. Each may be its own service.

Orchestration: one manager

In orchestration, one central microservice calls every other service in the right order. This central service is the orchestrator. It handles errors. It joins the results into one answer. Think of a wedding planner who tells the caterer, the decorator, and the band when to start.

The orchestrator also tracks the current state of the workflow. So it always knows what comes next. That is a lot of work. The chapter jokes that the job probably pays well.

The good points:

  • The workflow lives in one place, so it is easy to understand and change.
  • The orchestrator knows where each request is. So it is simple to restart a failed request from the right step.
  • Error handling is in one place. Other services do not need to know how to recover.

The bad points:

  • It is slower. Every step talks to the orchestrator. The orchestrator often saves state to a database.
  • It can become a bottleneck, because every request passes through it.
  • It is tightly coupled to the services it calls.
  • It is a single point of failure. If the orchestrator stops, the whole workflow stops.
  • The usual fix is to run several copies of the orchestrator. One copy can take over if another fails.

Choreography: no manager

In choreography, there is no central manager. Each service does its part. Then it calls the next service in the chain. This continues until the workflow is done. Think of a relay race. Each runner knows who gets the baton next.

Blood pressure as a relay

Suppose blood pressure monitoring is split into four services. The first captures a reading and passes it on. The second records it and passes it on. The third analyzes it. If needed, it gives the result to the fourth, which sends the alert. No one is in charge. Each service only knows who comes next.

Do not turn one service into a hidden manager. Suppose one service starts to direct all the others. Then you have moved to the Front Controller pattern, where one service acts as the central boss. That pattern suits orchestration. It defeats the purpose of choreography. The same trap appears when one service must track the workflow state.

The good points:

  • There is no central bottleneck. So responsiveness and performance are often better.
  • Services are loosely coupled, because none depends on a central boss.
  • Each service scales to its own demand, apart from the others in the chain.
  • Fast and scalable systems often use choreography.

The bad points:

  • Error handling is spread out. Each service must handle its own failures. This can create a lot of extra messages.
  • Recovery is hard. A user may retry a request that failed halfway. Nobody knows where to restart, because no single service directs the work.
  • Tracking state is hard too. Architects often pick one service to own the state. It is usually the first service in the chain. The other services report their progress to it.

Here is a quick way to choose. You may need clear control, easy recovery, and simple change management. Then lean toward orchestration. You may need high speed and separate scaling, and you can accept harder recovery. Then lean toward choreography.

The trouble ticket workflow

A medical facility files a trouble ticket. A field technician gets it on a phone, fixes the problem, and marks it fixed. The book picks choreography. The workflow is simple and has few error cases. So orchestration would be too much. Choreography needs less talk between services.

Strengths of microservices

Every style has strengths. Microservices is strong in these areas:

  • Maintainability. Each service is small and focused. You can quickly find the code you need to change.
  • Testability. The scope of a service is small. You can test it fully without testing the whole system.
  • Deployability. Each service is deployed on its own. Releases are less risky than with big monoliths. You can deploy more often, even daily.
  • Evolvability. To add a feature, you can create a new service. You test it and deploy it next to the others.
  • Scalability. You scale only the functions under load, not the whole system. This saves money.
  • Fault tolerance. When one service fails, only its function is down. Users can still use everything else.

Weaknesses of microservices

Some traits make microservices a poor choice. Watch for these:

  • Complexity. Microservices is one of the most complex styles. You must make hard choices on many topics. They are granularity, transactions, workflows, contracts, shared code, communication protocols, team layout, and deployment. That is a long list.
  • Performance. Every hop costs time. There is network latency, which is the time to reach the service. There is security latency, which is the time to check again who the user is. There is data latency, which is the delay when each service makes its own database calls. Chatty services become slow very fast.
  • Complex workflows. Your functions may be tightly coupled. If you split them into separate services, you get a messy system that is hard to change and hard to run. The book calls it a "big ball of distributed mud."
  • Monolithic databases. Microservices needs each service to own its data. If your data cannot be split, avoid this style.
  • Teams split by technology. Your company may have separate teams for user interface, backend, and database. Then microservices will struggle. This is an example of Conway's Law. The law says a system tends to copy the communication pattern of the organization that builds it. Microservices works best with cross-functional teams. Each team owns its services from the screen to the database.

The book gives these verdicts:

  • An online auction system is well suited. It needs high scalability, elasticity, concurrency, and independent functions.
  • A trouble ticket system for field technicians is also well suited.
  • A company that enters a new line of business with constant change might be a fit. You need more information.

Why doing things together is faster

Picture a diner with one cook. He makes the grilled cheese, then the fries, then the milkshake, one after the other. Every order waits for every step. The diner can serve only a few customers each hour.

Now add three workers, one for each dish. They all cook at the same time. The order finishes in about half the time. The diner can serve about twice as many people in the lunch rush.

This is the main idea of EDA. You split the work into separate services. A service is a small program that does one job. Each service starts its job as soon as something important happens.

The services share information through an event channel. They do not wait for answers from each other. Faster orders make happier customers. The chapter calls this responsiveness. More orders per hour means the system can handle more load. The chapter calls this scalability.

Think of it like a chai stall with a tiffin counter next to it. The chai maker does not wait for the snack seller to finish. Both start when the customer places an order. In EDA, services also hear about the order and start work together.

Meet Der Nile

Der Nile is a large online shop in Germany. It sells many things, from diapers to hair-growth supplements. Business is growing fast, and the ordering system is under too much load.

Orders take too long. Delivery times keep getting longer. Der Nile wants a new ordering system that can grow. You are the architect.

The old system runs one step after another. This is the slow diner pattern. Your first question is: what can happen at the same time?

What is an event?

An event is something that happens, especially something important. A concert, a wedding, or a big promotion at work are events in real life. In software, a user action often creates an event. Examples are placing a bid in an auction, filing an insurance claim, or buying something.

In EDA, an event is how one service tells the rest of the system that something just happened. Events are the main way that information moves between services.

An event usually carries data, such as every detail of an online order. This data is called its payload. Sometimes an event carries only a key, such as an order ID. Then any service that needs more detail must look it up in a data store. A data store is a place where data is saved.

An order is placed

A customer clicks "buy". The ordering service can publish an event that says an order was placed. To publish means to send the event to the event channel.

The payload might hold the order ID, the items, the price, and the shipping address. A payment service, an inventory service, and an email service can each react to this one event. None of them needs to know about the others.

Events and messages are different

Events and messages both carry information. But they have different jobs. An event tells everyone about an action that a service just did. The sender says, "A customer just placed an order." It does not care who listens.

A message is a command or a request. For example, "Apply the payment for this order" or "Give me shipping options for this customer". A message goes to one receiver.

Two rules show the difference:

  1. Events go out through topics. A topic is a public channel that anyone can listen to. Messages go to one service through a queue. A queue is a private line for one receiver.
  2. An event always describes something that has already happened. A message asks for something that still needs to be done.

Here is a quick test. If the sentence is in the past tense, it is probably an event, such as "Order Placed". If it is an order or instruction, it is probably a message, such as "Ship this order".

Initiating and derived events

An initiating event starts with a customer or an end user. It begins a business process. Clicking "buy" is a common example.

A service handles the initiating event. Then it often tells others what it did. These follow-up events are called derived events. They come from the first event.

One service can produce several derived events from one input. Anything that a service causes or creates can become a derived event.

One payment, many results

In the book's exercise, a customer pays by credit card. This creates a "Credit Card Charged" initiating event. A Fraud Detection service and a Credit Limit service both react to it.

Each service can publish several derived events, one for each result. Two examples are "fraud suspected" and "limit exceeded". These two names are only our illustration.

The book also says that in Der Nile, the Payment service publishes two different derived events. This is normal. Each derived event lets other services react without anyone waiting in a queue.

Why publish events nobody reads?

Here is a puzzle. If nobody listens to an event, why publish it? The reason is that you never know who will need it next month.

When you publish every important action, you get extensibility. This means you can add new features without changing the old parts. Extensibility is one of the architectural characteristics from Chapter 2.

Adding analytics easily

The Email Notification service at Der Nile sends an email when an order ships or arrives. After each email, it publishes a "Customer Notified" derived event.

Months later, the business wants to know which hours of the day have the most emails. You build a new Notification Analytics service. It listens to the existing event. You do not touch the email service, the order service, or anything else.

Every important action deserves a derived event. The cost is small. The reward is a system that grows new features by listening, not by changing old code.

Asynchronous and synchronous talk

EDA is fast mainly because it uses asynchronous communication. People often say "async" for short. Let us compare it with the more common style.

Synchronous (sync) communication means the sender waits for a reply. It cannot do anything else until the reply comes. A normal REST or HTTP API call works this way. A phone call is a good example. You dial, wait for the ring, and wait for the person to answer before you can talk.

Asynchronous communication means the sender does not wait. It sends the information and moves on. This is like sending a WhatsApp message. You send it and continue your day. The reply comes whenever it comes.

Async is also called fire-and-forget. The event is sent, which is the "fire" part. Then the sender moves on, which is the "forget" part.

The sender and receiver are less tied to each other. So the system is fast and loosely coupled. "Loosely coupled" means one part can change without breaking the others. Architects draw async links as dotted lines and sync links as solid lines. EDA uses async to send and receive events.

A quick checkout

Order Placement takes 600 milliseconds to check and save an order. Payment takes 1,200 milliseconds to charge the card.

With async, the customer waits about 600 milliseconds. The rest happens in the background. With sync, the customer waits about 1,800 milliseconds. That is nearly two seconds. The gap grows when you add more steps.

Async also helps availability. Availability means the system keeps working when parts fail. Suppose the Payment service is down. The async Order Placement service does not care. The customer still sees "order placed".

With sync, Order Placement cannot work properly while Payment is down. The customer sees an error and must try again.

The cost: harder error handling

Remember the First Law of software architecture: everything is a trade-off. The big problem with async is error handling.

With sync, a bad card is reported straight back to the customer. The customer fixes it and sends it again. With async, the customer thinks everything is fine, because nobody has told them otherwise. But the order cannot go ahead until they fix the payment problem.

In the book's example, the Payment service must raise the error. Then an Email Notification service must tell the customer to update the card. That means more moving parts. So error handling becomes much more complex.

Where does the data live?

Data is a hard topic in EDA. Suppose every service shares one database. Then the services are tied to that database. Suppose every service owns its own data, as in microservices. Then services often must ask each other for data with sync calls. That ties them together in a different way.

Either way, data becomes a point of coupling. EDA tries hard to avoid coupling. The chapter describes three database topologies. A topology is a way to arrange parts. So these are three ways to arrange data.

Monolithic database

All services share one database. The big advantage is simple. A service can query the database when it needs data that it does not own. For example, Order Placement can read current stock and shipping options directly. It needs no extra call.

The price is high. Suppose you change the structure of one table. It is hard to know which services will break. Testing and releasing become risky guesses. The system becomes brittle, which means it breaks easily.

The shared database is also a single point of failure. If it fails, everything fails. It may also not scale as the system grows.

Domain-partitioned databases

Here each business domain gets its own database. A domain is a group of related functions. All services in that domain share the database.

The Order Placement, Payment, and Inventory services all belong to the Order Placement domain. So they share one database. Services in other domains cannot touch that database directly. They must make a synchronous call instead. This couples them.

The book calls this a balanced, middle solution within EDA.

Database-per-service

Every service gets its own database. This makes a tighter physical bounded context than the domain approach. A bounded context is a fence around a service and its data. Changing the database is easy, because only the owning service is affected. Fault tolerance and scalability also improve. Fault tolerance means the system keeps working when something fails.

The catch is cost, in both money and speed. It is also hard to split data into tiny bounded contexts. Tables are tied together by foreign keys, triggers, views, and stored procedures.

When a service needs data that it does not own, it must make a synchronous call to the owner. This creates a lot of coupling and chatter. It slows the system down.

Choosing a topology is never a free upgrade. Shared databases tie services to the data. Separate databases tie services to each other. Know which coupling you are accepting.

Choosing a topology

Suppose the system must be as fast as possible. You expect from 20 to 300,000 users at the same time. A monolithic database can block growth at that scale.

Domain-partitioned databases are a middle choice. Database-per-service scales best and survives failures best. But its synchronous data calls make it the slowest, and it costs the most.

Now think of a medical monitoring system that must never fully fail. There you would give fault tolerance a high weight. The right answer depends on which trade-offs your business can accept. The book leaves this as an exercise. So treat these points as our reading of the scorecards.

EDA and microservices: six differences

EDA and microservices look alike at first. Both are distributed, which means the parts run on many machines. Both are good for scalability, agility, elasticity, and fault tolerance. Here are six differences. They are counted down from the smallest to the biggest.

  • 6. Performance. EDA uses async work and does many things at once. So it is very fast. Microservices have bounded contexts and very small services. They often need synchronous calls and lose a lot of time waiting. In the book's star ratings, EDA gets five stars for performance and microservices get two.
  • 5. Physical bounded contexts. Microservices cannot work without them. EDA can use them but does not need them. Data sharing is common in EDA, and ownership is less strict.
  • 4. Data granularity. Granularity means how big or small a part is. Microservices need each service to own its own small piece of data. EDA lets you choose a monolithic database, domain-partitioned databases, or database-per-service.
  • 3. Service granularity. Microservices are small and have a single purpose. EDA services are called event processors. They can be any size.
  • 2. Events and requests. EDA is built on reacting to things that already happened. This triggers more events. Microservices are built on handling requests and commands about things that still need to happen.
  • 1. Communication style. This is the biggest difference. EDA mostly uses async communication. Microservices usually use synchronous calls, often REST. EDA can sometimes call synchronously, for example to get data it cannot reach. Microservices can use async when a command needs no reply. These are exceptions, not the normal way.

Mixing both: event-driven microservices

Nothing stops you from combining the two styles. The result is called event-driven microservices. Using database-per-service alone does not make a system event-driven microservices. You need two ideas from microservices in the design:

  • Single-purpose services.
  • Physical bounded contexts.

Fixing a half-changed design

Imagine an EDA where Order Submission does four jobs. It accepts an order, checks it, charges the payment, and adjusts stock. This is fine for EDA. It is not fine for microservices, because one service does four jobs.

The fix is to split it into single-purpose services. Each one publishes its own events.

There is another problem. The Email service reads the Order Submission database directly. This breaks the fence. Instead, Email should ask the owning service for order details. That service is now called Order Placement. It is the renamed Order Submission service, after the split. Now each service stays in its own bounded context.

EDA strengths

  • Maintainability. Services are highly decoupled. Each one is fairly independent, so it is easier to maintain.
  • Performance. Work is mostly async and happens in parallel. This makes the system very fast.
  • Scalability. Each service scales on its own. The event channel also holds extra events when a slow part cannot keep up.
  • Evolvability. Every action can publish a derived event. This gives easy places to attach new features.
  • Fault tolerance. If one service goes down, the other services in the workflow keep running.

EDA weaknesses

  • Complexity. EDA uses async work, parallel processing, and several database topologies. Each one has trade-offs. So EDA is hard to design and run.
  • Testability. Async and parallel behaviour is hard to test. It is also hard to prove that a change in one service or event has not broken something else.
  • Synchronous calls. Your system may need many synchronous calls. Or services may need to wait for each other. Then EDA is a poor fit.
  • Databases. Whatever topology you pick, services end up coupled. They are tied either to the database or to each other. There is no perfect option.

The book adds that it is easy to find where to change code. But testing and deployment are risky and hard.

Think of it like a fast sports car. This is our own analogy, not the book's. It is quick and fun to extend. But it is harder to keep running smoothly and to test fully.

The full Der Nile system

The book's finished Der Nile system shows only the happy-path events. Happy path means the case where nothing goes wrong. This keeps the picture easy to read. Real life would add error events too. The flow goes like this:

  1. A customer's order starts at Order Placement. It publishes "Order Placed".
  2. Payment, Inventory, and Email react to that event.
  3. Payment publishes "Payment Applied".
  4. Fulfillment publishes "Order Fulfilled".
  5. Shipping publishes "Order Shipped".
  6. Email publishes "Customer Notified".
  7. Inventory also publishes "Inventory Adjusted". A Warehouse service uses it to publish "Stock Replenished".

Quick recap

  • An event is something that just happened, and it carries a payload of data.
  • Events go to anyone listening through topics. Messages go to one service through a queue.
  • Initiating events start a process. Derived events are follow-ups, and publishing them keeps the system extensible.
  • Async, fire-and-forget talk makes EDA fast and loosely coupled, but error handling is harder.
  • Pick one of three database topologies: monolithic, domain-partitioned, or database-per-service.
  • EDA and microservices differ mostly in communication style and in events versus requests.
  • Combine them to get event-driven microservices when you need both.
  • EDA is fast, scalable, and evolvable, but complex and hard to test.

Six Bonus Lessons

The book is over, but your journey as an architect is just starting. This appendix has six extra topics that the chapters had no room for.

The six topics are:

  1. Writing code as an architect.
  2. What the job expects.
  3. People skills that make designs last.
  4. Drawing clear diagrams.
  5. Balancing deep and wide knowledge.
  6. Practising with katas.

1. Keep writing code

Good architects still write source code. It keeps your skills strong. It also shows you how your decisions work with real users. But architecture work can take all your hours. So you should write code in the right places.

Stay off the critical path

The critical path is the chain of work that decides when the product is ready. Do not become the only person who owns it. Leave the framework code and the most important parts to your team. Then nobody is stuck if architecture work pulls you away.

Write proof-of-concept code

A proof of concept (PoC) is a small program that tests whether an idea works. Suppose you cannot choose between two options. Build a tiny version of each. Then see which one causes less trouble.

Write the code as if it will be released. Do this unless you are sure it will be thrown away. Prototypes often end up in production.

Other ways to stay useful

  • Pay down technical debt. Technical debt is the pile of needed fixes that a team has delayed. Clear it a little at a time. Your team will be happy. If you get called away, it will not block anyone.
  • Help during production outages. An outage is when a live system breaks. Help find the root cause. This is the real problem behind the symptom. Then make the code changes that get the system working again. You also see how your architecture is really built.
  • Do many code reviews. In a code review, someone else reads your code before it is merged into the main code. Nobody loves them. But they keep the code in line with your decisions.

Think of a building inspector who has never held a hammer. He can talk about wiring all day. But he may miss a cable that is fixed the wrong way.

2. What architects must do

Companies describe the role in different ways. But these expectations appear almost everywhere.

  • Make decisions. Define decisions and design principles. Design principles are the basic rules that your system follows. Use them to guide technology choices.
  • Keep up with trends. Trends change fast. You need to see what is coming.
  • Analyze all the time. Ask if the design still works as business and technology change. This is called architectural vitality.
  • Check that rules are followed. Make sure teams follow approved decisions. This is called architectural governance. A great design is useless if nobody follows it.
  • Know a little about a lot. You do not need to be an expert in every platform. But you should know what exists.
  • Lead people. Leadership skills are at least half of the job. Developers and architects usually like technical problems more than people problems. But the architect must guide the team through the build. The book quotes Gerald Weinberg: it is always a people problem, even if it does not look like one at first.
  • Know the business domain. This is the area of business that your software serves. Without it, you cannot understand the real problem.
  • Handle office politics. A product owner may say your plan costs too much. Other developers may prefer their own way. You must explain your case.

The database argument

A product owner wants the cheapest database. You know it will fail during the holiday rush. Do not just say "no". Bring a short test that shows what breaks. Also show what it would cost in lost sales. Now the talk is about money, not opinions.

3. People skills for lasting designs

Soft skills are people skills. They sound easy. But they take years of practice and mistakes to learn.

Show results, do not argue

Every environment is different. So an internet search will not settle an argument about your system. Run the options in a setup close to production. Then show the results.

Showing beats arguing. Show first, then talk.

Fight only for key points

Fight hard for what the architecture cannot live without. Let the rest go. People who choose their fights with care earn respect.

Speak about business value

Stakeholders are the people who care about the result. They rarely care about fault tolerance. They care about time to market, rules and law compliance, and company mergers and acquisitions. Translate your concerns into their words.

Explaining a message queue

Do not say, "We need a message queue for fault tolerance." Say this instead: "If payments are down for an hour, we still accept orders. So no sales are lost." The idea is the same. But now the business is on your side.

Bring developers into decisions

The book gives two leadership rules:

  1. If developers do not know why you decided something, they are less likely to agree.
  2. If developers were not involved, they are less likely to follow the decision.

So always give the reason for your decisions. Make sure the whole team understands it. Ask for their opinions. You can also invite them to a risk-storming session. This is a group exercise to find risks in a design.

Another way is to use the Request for Comment status in an ADR. An ADR is an Architecture Decision Record. It is a short document that records a decision and the reason for it.

Split the problem

Sometimes someone demands all or nothing. Then split the problem into parts. Does every part of the system really need a 400-millisecond response time? Does every part need 99.999% availability? Some parts will, and some will not.

The book borrows an idea from Sun Tzu: if the enemy is united, separate them. Once you see which piece is hard, talking becomes easier.

Be clear and short

The book has "four Cs": be Clear, be Concise, Communicate, and Collaborate. You need all four to earn trust.

Be available

Keep a block of time free early in the morning or late in the afternoon. Use it for your team's questions. Then you are not stuck in meetings all day.

4. Diagrams people read

Chapter 3 introduced ADRs. They are the best way to record the analysis behind a decision. A diagram is the other common tool. It shows what the system looks like.

A diagram can show structure, topology, communication, dependencies, and integration points. Topology means how the parts are arranged. Diagramming fills whole books. So here are quick tips.

  • Keep it simple. Too many lines make a tangled mess that nobody can read. The book calls this the hairball effect.
  • Always add a title. Most diagrams show one view of the system. The title should say which view.
  • Use one-way arrows. A double-headed arrow confuses readers. They cannot tell if it means two-way talk or is just the default.
  • Use real labels, not acronyms. Only insiders know the acronyms. Full words also save extra documents.
  • Use solid lines for synchronous, dotted lines for asynchronous. Synchronous means blocking. The caller waits for an answer, like a phone call. Asynchronous means nonblocking. The caller moves on, like a WhatsApp message.
  • Use the same shapes and colours each time. Random shapes only add noise.
  • Always add a key. The key explains each shape and line.
KEY
  ====>  synchronous call (the caller waits)
  - - ->  asynchronous call (the caller moves on)

[Checkout Web App] ====> [Order Service]
[Order Service]    ====> [Payment Service]
[Order Service]    - - -> [Email Service]
[Order Service]    ====> [Orders Database]

The database cylinder is about the only shape that everyone agrees on. For every other shape, add a key.

5. Depth and breadth

Technical depth means all the topics where you have real expertise. Staying an expert takes time, because technology keeps changing.

Technical breadth includes your expertise. It also includes what you know about other solutions and some of their trade-offs. As an architect, knowing five ways to solve a problem is better than knowing one way deeply.

Picture a pyramid. Early in your career, the pyramid shows everything you know of the world's information. You then move from junior developer to tech lead. The "stuff you know" grows. It includes languages, frameworks, tools, and platforms. You can also help and mentor other developers.

Then you become the main expert. A middle layer forms, called the "stuff you know you don't know". You know where your knowledge ends. It takes a lot of effort to get there. You must also keep your hands-on skills sharp.

New architects start with a tech lead's pyramid. So make an effort to widen it on purpose.

Widening your knowledge

Say you are a .NET expert. Volunteer for a Java project. Or take a user interface that uses a lot of JavaScript. Or take a hard data problem. Each one adds breadth. You also start to see patterns that repeat across tools.

Your expertise will fade a little over time. That hurts. But you trade depth for breadth, and breadth helps you see trade-offs.

6. Practise with katas

Bruce Lee made a point about practice. Drilling one kick ten thousand times is better than throwing ten thousand different kicks once. A kata is a practice form from martial arts. An architectural kata is a practice exercise. It acts out the design of a real system, so you can repeat the process.

How a kata runs

  1. Form teams. Use groups of three to five people. Odd numbers help, because a majority can settle arguments.
  2. Get a problem. A moderator keeps time and guides the session. Each team gets a project. The moderator plays the "customer". Teams ask the moderator questions to make things clear. Each team works on a different kata. The authors sometimes give the same kata to two teams to compare results.
  3. Design. Follow the book's process. Analyze architectural characteristics. Find logical components. Choose an architectural style. Document decisions. You can use any technology. But respect real limits like budget and hiring. The focus is architecture and trade-off analysis. The team also draws a rough picture of its solution.
  4. Present and face questions. Teams defend their design against hard but fair questions. When you listen to another team, your job is to ask questions. Choosing an overall winner is optional.

A parking app kata

The moderator says, "Design a parking app for three city garages." Your team asks how many cars arrive at the busiest hour. You sketch two options and present both. The other teams question the weakest part. You leave with a better sense of trade-offs.

Rules that make katas work

  • Mix the teams. Colleagues who work together every day should usually be split up. The goal is to learn to work with architects you do not know. Treat the rules as flexible. Let the moderator decide anything the rules do not cover.
  • Keep supplies simple. Poster paper or a whiteboard is enough.
  • Choose the time to suit. A kata can take as little as 45 minutes. It can also take several weeks. The authors have run katas for real companies that gave teams eight weeks.
  • Give balanced feedback. Ask helpful questions. Do not talk only about strengths or only about flaws.

Where to go next

You made it to the end. The book's website, headfirstsoftwarearchitecture.com, has what comes next. The book also suggests some further reading.

Quick recap

  • Keep writing code, but stay off the critical path.
  • Architects make decisions, watch architectural vitality, enforce governance, and handle office politics.
  • Show results instead of arguing, fight only for key points, and speak business value.
  • Diagrams need a title, a key, one-way arrows, and real labels.
  • In diagrams, use solid lines for sync and dotted lines for async.
  • Build breadth as well as depth, so you know the trade-offs between options.
  • Practise katas in small teams. Present, face questions, and give balanced feedback.

That is the whole book. If you remember only one thing, remember this: architecture means choosing which trade-offs you can accept, and writing down why you chose them.
