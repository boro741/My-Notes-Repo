How to Get the Most Out of This Book

Before we touch a single architecture term, let's talk about your brain. This book was built around how people actually learn, and a few minutes of setup will make the rest of the ride much easier.

Who this book is for

This book is for you if you want to understand what software architecture is all about, and you would rather have a lively dinner-party conversation than sit through a dry academic lecture. If you already answered yes to both, keep reading.

It's probably not for you if you are brand new to the tech industry. Architecture makes more sense after you have built a few things yourself. It's also not for seasoned architects who only want a reference shelf book. And it's definitely not for anyone who would rather lick a 9-volt battery than try a new way of learning.

Your brain is picky

Your brain loves novelty. It scans for the unusual, and it filters out the routine. Picture a day hike where a tiger jumps out of the bushes. Your neurons fire, your heart pounds, and your brain files that moment under "very important, never forget." Now picture studying for an exam in a warm, quiet room. Your brain sees a tiger-free zone and quietly tosses the boring stuff, including the chapter on architectural decisions.

The trick is to make the material feel like it matters. There are two routes. The slow route is repetition: keep hitting the same idea until your brain gives up and says, "Fine, this must be important." The faster route is to give your brain more kinds of work to do. Pictures, stories, questions, and activities all fire up different parts of your head at once.

Think of your brain as a bouncer at a club. It only lets in guests who look interesting. Bring the interesting stuff: a surprising picture, a joke, a question that makes you go "wait, what?" and the bouncer lets it through.

What the authors did to help

The book leans on a handful of learning ideas that have research behind them:

  • Visuals with labels built in. Words placed inside a picture are easier to remember than words in a caption at the bottom of the page.
  • A conversational voice. Your brain pays more attention when it feels like someone is talking to it, even if that someone is a book.
  • Stories and people. Your brain is wired to notice people, so the book uses characters, companies, and dialogue.
  • Deliberate repetition. The same concept shows up more than once on purpose, in different forms, so it sticks in more than one spot.
  • Emotion. Surprise, curiosity, fun, and that "I rule!" feeling when a puzzle clicks all help memory. You don't need a sad story about a dog.

Your part of the deal

The authors did their part. Here is what they suggest you do with yours:

  • Slow down. The more you understand, the less you have to memorize.
  • Stop and think. When the book asks a question, actually answer it in your head before peeking at the answer.
  • Do the exercises with a pencil. Writing things down counts as physical activity for your learning.
  • Read the "There Are No Dumb Questions" sidebars. They are part of the core content, not optional extras.
  • Read something light right before bed. Your brain does a lot of processing after you close the book, and new heavy material can crowd that out.
  • Talk it out loud. Try explaining an idea to someone else. You will find the gaps fast.
  • Drink water. Even mild dehydration makes thinking fuzzier, often before you feel thirsty.
  • Listen to your brain. If you start skimming or forgetting what you just read, take a break. Shoving more in will not help.
  • Feel something. Make up captions for the pictures. Groaning at a bad joke still beats feeling nothing.
  • Apply it every day. Architecture is a skill, and skills come from practice.

How the book is put together

This is a learning book, not a reference book. Start at the beginning, because later chapters assume you saw the earlier ones. The book follows a simple pattern: break a big idea into pieces, understand each piece on its own, and then build the pieces back together. Complex ideas show up only after the simple ones are solid.

It also mixes learning styles (step-by-step, big picture, example-first) and gives both sides of your brain work, so you can stay focused longer. Every chapter has exercises with solutions. Try the problem first, and peek only if you get stuck.

It also uses the 80/20 approach. It covers the parts of software architecture you will use most, instead of trying to be a full encyclopedia. The examples are deliberately generic business problems, because abstract theory is hard to hold onto. Some of the "Brain Power" exercises have no single right answer, and that is on purpose. The crossword puzzles are the only optional part of the book.

The exercises are not add-ons. They are part of the core content. Reading about swimming does not teach you to swim, and reading about architecture does not teach you to architect.

Two do-it-yourself chapters

Chapters 9 and 12 are different. They are entirely exercise-based, and you play the software architect from start to finish: you pick the architectural characteristics, build a logical architecture, and make the decisions, including which architectural style to use. Chapter 9 is about TripEZ (said like "trapeze"), an online trip-management dashboard that makes travel easier, especially for road warriors. Travelers see and manage all their reservations, organized by trip, in a browser or on a phone. Chapter 12 is about Make the Grade, a standardized testing system. Students at Dataville Public School in specific grade levels all take the same test, which shows how well the students, teachers, and school are doing. Both are a good way to test how much of the rest of the book has stuck.

Watching people lift weights at the gym does not make you stronger. You have to pick up the bar. This book is the gym, and the exercises are the reps.

Quick recap

  • Your brain filters out boring content, so you have to give it a reason to keep the material.
  • Pictures with words inside them, a conversational voice, and emotion all help learning stick.
  • Slow down, answer the questions, do the exercises, and talk the ideas out loud.
  • Take breaks when you feel overloaded, and drink some water while you are at it.
  • The book builds ideas in pieces, then joins them, and it repeats key concepts on purpose.
  • The two do-it-yourself chapters are where you practice being the architect end to end.

Software Architecture, Demystified

Architecture is the structure that holds a software system up. It is the stuff that is painful to change later, and it decides whether your system grows, survives failures, and keeps up with the business. This chapter gives you a simple map: four dimensions, and the line between architecture and design.

Architecture is the blueprint

Think about a typical house on your street. Its architecture is the structure: its shape, how many rooms and floors it has, its dimensions. The building plan holds all the lines and boxes you need to actually build it. Structural things like walls, floors, and the roof are hard and expensive to change once the house is standing. They are the important stuff.

Software has the same kind of blueprint. Instead of walls and stairs, it has user interfaces, services, databases, and the ways they talk to each other. A software architecture diagram is the building plan. Like a house plan, it sets guidelines and constraints, and it shows the vision for the finished product.

Architecture also works like a garden. The overall layout of the garden is the architectural style. Each cluster of similar plants is an architectural component. Each individual plant is a class file that does the actual work. Weather changes the garden the way new technology, platforms, and deployment environments change a system. And if you ignore the garden for long enough, weeds move in. In software, those weeds are structural decay.

Architecture has four dimensions

A room is described by three numbers: length, width, and height. Software architecture is described by four dimensions:

  • Architectural characteristics. What the system needs to support, such as scalability, testability, and availability.
  • Architectural decisions. The big choices with long-term or significant effects, such as the kind of database, the number of services, and how those services talk to each other.
  • Logical components. The building blocks of the system's functions and how they interact, such as inventory management or payment processing in an online store.
  • Architectural style. The overall physical shape of the system, the same way a Victorian or ranch style describes a house.

A common mistake is describing an architecture with only one dimension. Saying "our architecture is microservices" tells you the style and nothing else. It leaves big questions open. What qualities are critical? What are the main components? What major decisions have you made about how to build it?

The four pieces form a puzzle

Think of each dimension as one piece of a puzzle. Each piece has its own shape, but they all have to fit together to make a complete picture. The style has to line up with the characteristics you chose and with the decisions you made. The logical components have to line up with the characteristics, the style, and the decisions too.

So when you change one piece, check the others. If you decide you need very high scalability, that choice should show up in the style you pick, the components you define, and the decisions that follow.

You can't skip a dimension. Every architecture needs all four to be described properly.

Characteristics are the foundation

Characteristics are the capabilities that are critical or important to the success of the system. Without them, you cannot make architectural decisions or analyze trade-offs. That is why they come first.

Imagine choosing between two homes. One is roomy but sits next to a busy motorway. The other is small but on a quiet street. Which matters more to you, size or noise? You can't pick until you know what you care about.

Software works the same way. Say you need to choose a database. Should it be relational, simple key/value, or a complex graph database? The answer depends on which characteristics matter most. A graph database is great if you need very fast searches across connected data, which we'll call performance. A relational database is often the better fit if you need to keep relationships between data correct, which we'll call data integrity.

You may also hear these qualities called nonfunctional requirements, quality attributes, or simply "the ilities," because so many of them end in -ility. The book prefers "architectural characteristics," because the term describes the character of the architecture and what it has to support.

The database choice

(These two scenarios are our own illustrations of the book's point.) A team is building a shop that recommends products by following links between people, orders, and items. Search speed across those links matters more than anything else. A graph database fits that need. Now imagine a bank tracking account balances, where a lost or mismatched number is a disaster. A relational database, which is built to keep related data consistent, fits better. Same kind of decision, different characteristics, different answer.

Decisions are guideposts for the team

Architectural decisions are choices about the structure of the system that have long-term or significant effects. Once made, they act as constraints. They tell the development team how to plan and build.

Think about a home. Should it have one floor or two? Should the roof be flat or peaked? These are structural calls that are hard to undo. In software, a typical decision is this: the user interface must never talk straight to the database. It has to go through services. That one rule shapes how the UI team works and how every other part of the system reaches the data.

Expect a lot of them. A system can easily have several dozen documented architectural decisions, and bigger, more complicated systems have more. Good decisions focus on the significant themes, not every small detail.

Logical components are the rooms

If the house is made of rooms, the system is made of logical components. Each component performs a function, such as processing the payment for an order, managing item inventory, or tracking orders. A component should have a clear role and a clear responsibility. You should be able to say in one sentence what it does.

In code, a logical component usually lives in a directory or namespace. That gives your team a place to put the source files for each business function.

Where the payment code lives

Suppose the directory app/order/payment has the matching namespace app.order.payment. That pairing tells everyone that this logical component is Payment Processing. The source code that lets customers pay for an order lives there and uses that namespace.

app/
  order/
    payment/     Payment Processing component

A quick way to keep the terms straight: the domain is the problem you are solving, the "what." The system's functionality is how you solve it, the "how."

Architectural style is the overall shape

Houses come in styles such as Victorian, ranch, or Tudor. The style tells you a lot about the structure. Ranch homes usually have one floor. Colonial and Tudor homes often have chimneys. Contemporary homes often have flat roofs.

Software styles work the same way: each defines the overall shape of the system and comes with its own set of characteristics. The book covers five common styles later, with a chapter for each. A few teasers:

  • Microservices scale very well and give you a high level of agility, meaning you can respond quickly to change.
  • Layered architecture is less complex and less costly.
  • Event-driven architecture offers high scalability and is very fast and responsive.

Getting the style right early matters. Imagine starting construction on a one-story ranch and halfway through deciding you want a three-story Victorian instead. That is a major undertaking, and it blows the budget and the move-in date. Software is no different. Moving a big layered monolith to microservices is a serious project, not a weekend refactor. The book promises to show you later how to pick a style based on the characteristics that matter to you, because you can't choose a style without knowing what is important to you. A style may be one of the options, or a combination of them, so match it to the characteristics you need.

Changing a house's style after the foundation is poured costs far more than changing the paint. The same is true of an architectural style. Picking it on day one, based on what the system needs, saves you a very expensive rebuild.

Architecture versus design

Architecture is less about appearance and more about structure. Design is less about structure and more about appearance. In a room, the wall color, the furniture layout, and the carpet are design. The room's size and the location of its doors and windows are architecture, because they are part of the structure.

A typical business application follows the same split. The architecture is how web pages talk to backend services and databases to read and save data. The design is what each page looks like: the colors, the field placement, and which design patterns the developers use.

An order system through two lenses

Imagine a company replacing its outdated order system with a custom one. Customers place orders, view or cancel them, and pay with a credit card, a gift card, or both.

From a design view, you might draw a UML class diagram (UML is a standard way to draw software classes and how they relate). It shows how the payment classes interact. It says nothing about how the files are organized or where they are deployed.

From an architecture view, you might decide on a separate service for each payment type, plus an orchestrator service that coordinates the payment process. Now you are talking about services, how they communicate, and what they deploy as.

Architecture and design are a spectrum

Some decisions are clearly architectural, such as choosing an architectural style. Others are clearly design, such as moving a field on a screen or changing a field's type inside a class. Most decisions fall somewhere in between.

Knowing where a decision sits matters because it tells you who should own it. Some decisions belong to the development team, such as how to design the classes for one feature. Some belong to the architect, such as picking the right style for the whole system. Others should be made together, such as deciding whether to split a service into two or merge two back together.

Three questions to place a decision

To figure out where a decision falls, ask three questions. The more strategic, costly to change, and trade-off-heavy a decision is, the more architectural it is.

Question 1: Is it strategic or tactical?

Strategic decisions are long term and shape future choices. Tactical decisions are short term and mostly stand on their own, although they may follow a broader strategy. Deciding how big to make your new home is strategic, because it limits how many rooms you can have. Picking a lighting fixture is tactical, because it does not change the size of your dining table. To judge this, ask three things. How much thought and planning does it need? A decision that takes a few minutes to an hour is tactical, while one that takes days or weeks is probably strategic. How many people are involved? A choice you can make with one colleague is tactical, while one that needs many meetings with lots of stakeholders is strategic. Is it a long-term vision or a short-term action? If you will live with it for years, it leans architectural.

Question 2: How hard is it to build or change?

Martin Fowler, a renowned software architect and author, once wrote that "software architecture is the stuff that's hard to change." The harder a decision is to undo, the closer it sits to the architecture side. Moving from a traditional n-tiered layered architecture to microservices takes a lot of time and effort, so it sits at the far architecture end. Rearranging fields on a user interface screen is quick, so it sits at the far design end. Building an addition onto a house is architecture. Adding an area rug is design.

Question 3: How significant are the trade-offs?

A trade-off is a pro and a con you weigh when you make a decision. When you choose a city to live in, the trade-offs are big: jobs, cost, and commute, for example. Choosing the color of your living room rug has small trade-offs. Decisions with significant trade-offs need more time and analysis, and they tend to be architectural. Decisions with minor trade-offs can be made quickly, and they tend to be design.

Almost every decision has a trade-off, even the small ones. If you think you have found one that doesn't, look a little harder.

Putting the three questions to work

Here is a scenario the book uses. Say you decide to use asynchronous messaging between the Order Placement service and the Inventory Management service. Your goal is a faster feeling when customers place orders, since the customer shouldn't have to wait while the business updates its inventory. Now run the decision through the three factors.

  • Strategic or tactical? Tactical. Not many people need to be involved, and it does not need long-term planning. That pushes it toward design.
  • Level of effort? Low. Sending a message to another service is pretty standard stuff. That also pushes it toward design.
  • Trade-offs? Significant. A queue makes placing an order feel faster, but inventory may not update in a timely way, which can cause back-order situations. That pulls it toward architecture.

Average the three and the decision lands near the middle, a bit on the architecture side. So an architect should probably be consulted or involved. Notice that you needed all three factors to see this. Any one alone would have given a misleading answer. The point of the exercise is to tell development teams when to bring in an architect and when to decide on their own.

Quick recap

  • Software architecture is the structure of a system: the parts that are hard and expensive to change.
  • Architecture has four dimensions: characteristics, decisions, logical components, and style. You need all four.
  • The four dimensions fit together like a puzzle, so a change in one should be checked against the others.
  • Design is about appearance and detail. Architecture is about structure. Most decisions sit on a spectrum between the two.
  • Decide whether a choice is strategic, how hard it is to change, and how big its trade-offs are. Those answers tell you who should own it.
  • Characteristics are the foundation: without them you cannot make decisions or weigh trade-offs.
  • Choose your architectural style early, because switching styles later is a major rebuild.

Sillycon Symposia is a startup with a Bay Area feel. It runs technology conferences, but with a twist: every event is mixed with comedy, because a room full of laughing people is a room full of people who stay engaged. The company plans to build Lafter, a social network for technologists who share its conferences and jokes.

The business team wrote a requirements document. Here is the short version. Users register for usernames and approve a privacy policy. They can post a "Joke," which is a long-form post, or a "Pun," which is a short-form one. Followers react with a "HaHa" for strong approval or a "Giggle" for a milder one. Speakers from Sillycon events get a special icon and can host forums about their content. Messages can run up to 281 characters, and users can also link to outside content. The expected audience is hundreds of speakers and thousands of users. The extra context matters just as much: the company works in many countries, has a very small support staff, and expects "bursty" traffic, meaning it will be packed during live conferences and quiet in between.

The cubicle lesson

Alex has just been named the architect for Lafter. Sam thinks the design is so simple that they can jump straight into it. Mara disagrees. You can only skip analysis for the very simplest applications, and Lafter is not one of them. You need to analyze architectural characteristics and logical components before you choose an architectural style. You can build almost any application in almost any style, but some styles fit better than others. Choosing the style first is putting the cart before the horse.

Mara also says architects need to join the business analysts early and often. What looks like a tiny difference to a business person can be a big deal, or very hard to support, in the architecture.

Sam then suggests the agile approach: start small and keep iterating until the whole system exists. Mara points out that iteration doesn't work the same way for characteristics. It is very hard to make a system highly scalable if it was never designed to scale.

Adding a basement after the foundation is poured, the walls are up, and the roof is on is possible, but it is a painful project. Architectural characteristics are like the foundation. You can't easily bolt them on later.

What an architectural characteristic is

The thing you are writing software about is the domain. It is the problem you are solving, such as selling shoes or sharing jokes. Designing for the domain will take up much of your effort. But an architect also has to analyze the architectural characteristics, which describe the operational and structural qualities the system needs.

The book defines an architectural characteristic in three parts. First, it is a nondomain design consideration: it is not a business feature. Second, it influences the structure of the system. Third, it is critical or important to the success of the system. Put simply, architectural characteristics are the capabilities a system has to have for the project to succeed.

Structural design has two halves: logical components, which represent the domain, and architectural characteristics. Put them together and you have the structural considerations for an architecture.

A quick note on names. Many teams call these "nonfunctional requirements," but the book dislikes that: they are functional, they just don't concern the domain, and the label downplays them. "System quality attributes" sounds like something checked at the end of a project. "Cross-cutting requirements" is the least bad, but the word "requirement" ties it to domain behavior.

Requirements say what the application should do. Characteristics say how well it has to do it, and why certain choices were made. For example, performance is often critical but rarely appears in a requirements document. You have to dig for it.

A characteristic becomes architectural when it changes the structure

Security matters for almost every project. Every system needs baseline precautions while it is being designed and coded. But security becomes an architectural characteristic when the architect has to make a special structural effort to support it.

The Lafter promotions feature

Lafter needs a feature that markets upcoming promotions and applies rules for when each promotion applies. You could build this as a monolith, meaning one deployable unit with one matching database. Or you could build it as a set of independent services.

In the monolith, every change to the promotion rules means redeploying the whole application, because the monolith ships as one piece. In the distributed design, only the Promotions service is affected, so you can redeploy just that service. This is one of many trade-offs you will weigh when choosing between a monolith and a distributed design.

Pick fewer characteristics, not more

A system could support a huge number of architectural characteristics. It shouldn't. Every characteristic you add makes the design more complex, and there are plenty of tempting choices. The book gives three reasons to be careful.

  • They are hard to standardize. Different organizations use different names for the same thing. "Performance" and "responsiveness" might describe the same behavior. A shared vocabulary, which the book calls a ubiquitous language, gives your team a fighting chance at a usable list.
  • They are synergistic. Characteristics affect each other and the domain. Making an app more secure usually adds encryption and checks, which costs some performance. Storing users' payment details changes the security and data integrity characteristics too.
  • They are overabundant. New ones appear all the time. A few years ago, on-demand elasticity from a cloud provider was not an option, so nobody designed for it. Even the number of categories has grown, with additions such as cloud constraints and capabilities.

Overengineering happens when you support too many characteristics and complicate the design for little or no benefit. A related trap is resume-driven development (RDD). It is fun to play with new tools, and you should keep learning. But choosing a system's features to pad your résumé instead of helping the business is a bad reason to add complexity.

Synergy is a warning sign. No architect can make every architecture scalable, because physical limits like memory and bandwidth get in the way. Some characteristics even pull against each other: high performance and scalability are a hard pair to get together. Be careful when you change one characteristic, and check what else it touches. Changes to the domain, such as component boundaries, can shift characteristics too.

One more upside of a short list: knowing which characteristics are truly critical acts as a filter. It weeds out nice-to-haves that only add complexity.

Explicit and implicit characteristics

An explicit characteristic is stated clearly in the requirements. An implicit characteristic is not written down, but it shapes the design anyway, because the context makes it obvious. Picture a pile of mail and packages building up outside a house. You don't need a note to figure out that nobody is home.

Security is often implicit. Nobody writes "do not build an insecure system" in a requirements list, but architects know it. A high-frequency trading firm may never say that transactions must finish in milliseconds, but anyone in that field knows it matters a lot. Some implicit characteristics are subtle, such as keeping the internal structure clean so that sloppy coding doesn't wreck the system over time. Nobody puts "make it maintainable" in a requirements document, but it still matters.

The same trait, different weight

Consider a free office lunch-ordering system. Security matters a little, mostly so coworkers don't discover that you order the same egg salad sandwich every day. Availability matters a little, too. If the system breaks, everyone just gets their own lunch. Now compare that with a payment system, where security and availability matter enormously. The characteristic is the same. The weight depends on the domain.

Sorting characteristics into families

Architectural characteristics are like animals in a zoo. They range from low-level code traits, such as modularity, to big operational concerns, such as scalability and elasticity. There is no universal list, and no real standard for what the terms mean. Each organization interprets them for itself, and new ones keep arriving. Mara's solution is to group them into families, the way a zoo map groups animals into houses and enclosures. The book's families are:

  • Process. Where the software development process meets architecture. These cover the mechanics of building the software.
  • Structural. How the internal structure of the system looks, including how tightly components are coupled and how integration points relate.
  • Operational. How architectural decisions affect what the people running the system can do.
  • Cross-cutting. Characteristics that don't fit neatly anywhere, and touch the whole system. Like the platypus in the zoo, they refuse to sit in one enclosure.

Some characteristics show up in more than one family. And since the software world keeps changing, the families keep changing too.

Here is a quick reference of characteristics you will meet often, grouped the way the book's zoo map groups them. The definitions are in everyday words, because the exact terms vary from one organization to the next.

Where characteristics come from

Characteristics do not appear out of thin air. The book names three sources.

  • The problem domain. Many needs come straight from the problem itself.
  • Environmental awareness. What you know about where you work. A fast-moving startup architect will favor agility, spoken or not. Or say you must pick between a custom protocol and a standard one to join two subsystems. If the company plans lots of mergers, that tips you toward the open standard. Context always matters.
  • Holistic domain knowledge. The domain is bigger than your slice of it. For a payment system, knowing the finance world, its regulations, and customer habits reveals more characteristics.

Translate business speak into characteristics

Business people rarely say "scalability." They say "thousands of users." Your job as an architect is to dig into that phrase. How many users will there be, which is scalability? How many will be active at the same moment, which is concurrency? How fast will they arrive, which is elasticity?

The university promotion

Lafter runs a sign-up promotion at a university with 1,000 students and 10 hours to register. To keep the math easy, you might design the system for a steady trickle of 100 sign-ups an hour. But real students are not steady. The most eager ones rush in during the first hour. Many procrastinate and then stay quiet for most of the day. Then a last-minute crowd floods in just before the window closes. The real design has to handle a burst at the start, stay mostly idle, and handle another burst at the end. Never underestimate a university student's ability to procrastinate.

Business experts also hide requirements inside plain English. Architects have to read between the lines and turn those hints into characteristics. A requirement like "we need this to be very popular at launch" is a hint about elasticity and concurrency, even though nobody used those words.

Solutions are not requirements

Customers often arrive with a solution instead of a requirement. The US Air Force once asked for a fighter jet that could reach Mach 2.5. The technology of the time could not deliver it. So the designers asked why the speed mattered. The answer was that these jets were expensive, so the pilot needed to be able to flee a fight if necessary. With that knowledge, the team designed the F-16. Its top speed was Mach 2.1, but it was the most maneuverable and fastest-accelerating jet of its time.

The lesson is to keep asking "why?" like an annoying toddler until you find the real need. A requirement describes the need. A solution describes a particular way to meet it, often a product or a tool. When someone says "we need to buy this software package," ask what problem it solves.

Composite characteristics

Sometimes a business person asks for a "reliable" system. Reliable is not one thing. It can mean the system is available, that its screens behave consistently, and that its data stays correct. Qualities like these combine into what the book calls a composite characteristic.

To break a composite down, ask: can I objectively measure this? Performance is a composite too, since you have to get more specific before you can measure it. Agility is another composite, built from testability, deployability, modularity, and more. The book's example of a measurable piece is first contentful paint, which is how long a web page takes to load on a mobile device. Once you can measure something, you can design for it and test it.

If you cannot measure a characteristic, you cannot tell whether you achieved it. Turn vague words like "fast" or "reliable" into numbers and specific checks.

Priorities depend on context

No two projects get the same list of characteristics. Each application picks its own set and ranks them differently. A free lunch-ordering app and a bank's payment system will have very different top priorities. Context counts here too: a fast-moving startup will prioritize agility whether or not anyone writes it down, while a large enterprise has a lot at stake and will weigh things differently.

When you ask business users which characteristics they want, they will often say "all of them!" It is tempting, but it is a bad idea. Since characteristics are synergistic, every extra one makes the design harder. Your job is to translate business goals into a few characteristics you can actually measure and build for.

Finding the balance

There are three ways a design can go. If you skip the characteristics analysis, you end up with expensive rework once you discover the system can't meet its real needs. If you balance domain needs against the necessary characteristics, you get the operational and structural goals you need without overengineering. If you retreat to an ivory tower and analyze too many characteristics, you waste time that should have gone to building and maintaining the system.

The magical number seven

One helpful rule for the conversation between architects and business analysts is to cap the list at seven characteristics. Why seven? Psychologists have long found that people handle information in chunks of about seven items. That is why early phone numbers had seven digits. Seven is enough variety to be useful, but not so many that people freeze up from too many options. The book cites George Miller's famous 1956 paper on this idea.

Trimming the list

The book gives a worksheet with 18 candidates for Lafter, including performance, data integrity, deployability, responsiveness, testability, availability, fault tolerance, scalability, recoverability, elasticity, concurrency, and auditability. You trim the list to seven, then check off the top three. An illustrative result (not the book's answer) might be scalability, availability, and data integrity at the top, with the others kept as secondary concerns. The exact picks depend on the business, which is the whole point of the exercise.

Characteristics and logical components work together

Architectural characteristics are close to capabilities. They describe the kinds of things the solution has to support. Logical components, covered in depth in Chapter 4, are closer to behavior. They describe the design of the system that solves the core problem. Architects combine the two to choose an architectural style. Lafter is no exception. You have to strike a balance between what the business needs the app to do and what the architecture must be good at.

Not choosing a characteristic does not mean the design will be poor at it on purpose. It is a signal of priority. You are trading one characteristic off against another, and choosing the one that matters most for this system.

Quick recap

  • Architectural characteristics are the capabilities a system needs to succeed. They are not the same as features.
  • A characteristic becomes architectural when it changes the structure of the system, such as splitting a monolith into services.
  • Keep the list short, because characteristics are synergistic and every extra one adds complexity.
  • Some characteristics are implicit. Use your domain knowledge to spot them, since nobody will list them for you.
  • Translate business phrases like "thousands of users" or "reliable" into measurable characteristics.
  • Ask "why?" until you reach the real requirement behind any proposed solution.
  • Cap the list around seven, then rank the top few based on the context of the project.
  • Group characteristics into process, structural, operational, and cross-cutting families. Some belong to more than one.
  • Characteristics come from three places: the problem domain, your environment, and wider domain knowledge.

The Two Laws: Everything Is a Trade-Off

Software architecture has no cheat sheet of "best practices" that will save you from thinking. This chapter gives you two laws to live by, a way to weigh options against each other, and a simple template for writing down why you picked what you picked. Your future self will thank you.

A sneaker app that outgrew itself

Picture a company called Two Many Sneakers. Its mobile app lets collectors (the book calls them "sneakerheads") buy, sell, and trade rare shoes. Millions of listings later, the app is booming. The first architecture was simple: one trading service that talks to its own database. Customers now want real-time alerts when a pair they've been hunting goes up for sale. The business also wants stronger fraud detection, which means analytics that spot strange behavior and filter out bots.

The plan is for the trading service to tell two new services, a notification service and an analytics service, whenever something interesting happens. Simple enough. But the moment you start to design it, questions pile up:

  • How should the services talk to each other?
  • Do the analytics and notification services need the same data, or totally different data?
  • Will the finance department (which cares about compliance) want updates too? If so, the design has to be easy to extend.

Some things are known and plenty are not. That is normal. Some of it is "unknown unknowns": surprises nobody could list in advance, which can show up mid-project and wreck a tidy plan. Architects almost never get a clean, complete set of facts before they have to choose. The trick is to make good choices with the facts you have, stay iterative, and stay ready to revise, because nothing in architecture stays static.

Start with the characteristics that matter

Before you pick a design, figure out which architectural characteristics matter most. These are the qualities the system must have, like scalability (handling growth), security, or upgradability. Not every characteristic is equally important. Ask yourself whether each one is critical to success or just a nice-to-have. Everyone wants upgradability, but is it the thing that makes or breaks this project?

You will also find that some characteristics fight each other. A system with heavy encryption and secure connections will often be slower. You can't max out every quality at once, so you need to choose.

The First Law: everything is a trade-off

Here is the first big idea in this chapter. A trade-off is a compromise: you gain something and give up something else. Think about phone storage. You can store more photos by buying a bigger phone, which costs more, or you can compress the photos, which lowers their quality. Either way, you pay somewhere.

Software works the same way. A solution that allows great scalability might also make deployability or reliability harder. Every option has upsides and downsides. Your job is twofold:

  1. Know the trade-offs of every option you consider.
  2. Pick the option that best serves the characteristics that matter most.

This is the First Law of Software Architecture: everything in software architecture is a trade-off. It sounds obvious, but many architects forget it when they fall in love with a shiny tool. Whenever someone praises an approach, your reply should be, "What are the trade-offs?"

The book calls out two traps. Some people always reach for the tool that worked for them last time. Others catch "shiny object syndrome" and think a new technology will fix everything. New tools are fine. Just ask whether the upsides help you build a successful app and whether you can live with the downsides. As Rich Hickey put it, programmers know the benefits of everything and the trade-offs of nothing. Architects need both.

If you look at a decision in software architecture and cannot find a trade-off, you have not looked hard enough. Keep digging.

Trade-off analysis, round one: queues

Back to the sneaker app. The team assumes messaging, which is asynchronous (a trade-off against synchronous calls in its own right). Within messaging, one option for the trading service to notify others is queues. A queue is a point-to-point channel: the sender knows exactly who receives each message. To reach three services, the sender writes to three separate queues. It is like a group text where you pick every recipient before you hit send.

Here is what queues give you:

  • Security. The trading service knows every listener, so a rogue service can't quietly tune in.
  • Tailored messages. Each queue can carry a different message for a different consumer.
  • Independent control. Each queue can be monitored, and even scaled, on its own.

And here is the cost: the trading service is tightly coupled to its consumers, because it has to know how many there are. If the compliance team asks for updates next month, the trading service needs a code change to add a third queue. Queues give you control, but they give up extensibility.

Trade-off analysis, round two: topics

The other option is a topic. A topic uses a broadcast model. The publisher sends a message to the topic and does not care who is listening. Anyone interested subscribes. It works like posting a photo on a social network: everyone who follows you sees it, and you never picked them one by one.

The upside is clear. Compliance wants in? They subscribe, and the trading service does not change at all. That is low coupling.

The downsides are real too:

  • You can't tailor a message for each subscriber. Everyone gets the same thing.
  • Scaling is all-or-nothing, since there is only one thing to scale.
  • Anyone can subscribe without the publisher knowing. In some settings, that is a security risk.

Picking between the two

Imagine two requirements. First, "Security is paramount." That pushes you toward queues, because the publisher controls exactly who receives what. Second, "We will add more downstream services later, and they need different data." That pushes you toward topics, because new listeners cost nothing to add. You cannot satisfy both with one answer, so you must weigh which requirement matters more to the business.

Deciding: it depends, and the reason is the business

So which one wins? The honest answer is "it depends." Look at what the business cares about most. If security is the top priority, queues are the safer bet. Two Many Sneakers is growing fast and has loads of services interested in its trades, so extensibility is its biggest priority, and topics win. Time matters too. If you need to launch quickly, a simpler design may beat one that promises extra availability. (There is little point in a guarantee of "three nines" of uptime if you have no customers yet.)

The lesson is that trade-off analysis is not about finding a perfect answer. It is about finding the answer that fits your situation best.

How to run a trade-off analysis

You can use formal methods to structure this work. The Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) walks stakeholders through business drivers, the "-ilities," and the proposed architecture, then tests it against scenarios. Its weakness is that it treats the architecture as frozen. The Cost Benefit Analysis Method (CBAM) focuses on the cost of achieving a particular "-ility." The authors suggest that you look at both, and even combine them: ATAM can help with trade-off analysis, and CBAM can help you get the best return on investment.

Whatever method you use, remember that the process is a means to an end. You may run the analysis several times as you learn more. The goal is an architecture that serves the business.

Think of trade-off analysis like buying a car. A sports car is fast but has tiny trunk space. A minivan hauls the family but handles like a couch. You don't ask which car is "best." You ask which car fits the trips you actually take, and what you're willing to give up to get there.

What counts as an architectural decision?

Architects make many decisions, from the big picture to small tool choices. Which ones are architectural? In most cases, any choice that affects the structure of the system is an architectural decision. Most, but not all, of them revolve around structure. Whether to build a monolith (one big deployable application) or microservices (many small services) is one. Choosing a one-story or two-story house is a similar kind of choice: it shapes everything that comes after.

Some decisions are about a characteristic. If security is paramount, you might decide to require encryption on every connection, and that decision shapes the architecture. Others pick a tool or process because it indirectly helps you hit a characteristic. Both count.

Architectural decisions work as guideposts, not strict rules. They tell teams the constraints they must respect while leaving room to figure out the details.

A decision starts with a trade-off analysis. Then you weigh the pros and cons against other constraints: business and end-user needs, architectural characteristics, technical feasibility, time and budget, and even development concerns. Only then do you decide. Decisions are one of the dimensions needed to describe an architecture.

Michael Nygard, author of Release It!, describes an architecturally significant decision as one that affects how the rest of the project runs, or that changes the structure, non-functional qualities, dependencies, interfaces, or build techniques of the system. That is a useful test to keep in your head.

The Second Law: why beats how

Imagine your team does a trade-off analysis and decides to add a cache, a fast temporary store that speeds up reads. The "what" is easy to spot: there is now a cache in the system. But what about the reasons? Which options did you reject? What pressures pushed you there? How did the choice affect the team that had to build it?

Without those answers, future architects, including you a year from now, can see what you did and even how you did it. What they cannot easily see is why. Without the why, they may waste time re-exploring options you already rejected, or miss a key factor that drove the choice.

That gives us the Second Law of Software Architecture: why is more important than how. Record the reasoning behind each decision so it survives the passage of time.

The First Law tells you to weigh trade-offs. The Second Law tells you to write down what you concluded and why. Together they are the core habit of good architects.

Architectural decision records (ADRs)

The tool for capturing the why is the architectural decision record, or ADR. An ADR is a short document describing one architectural decision. Write one for every significant decision, and over time they build into an architectural decision log. That log is the memory of your project. It is the documentation that supports the "decisions" dimension of architecture.

An ADR has seven sections: Title, Status, Context, Decision, Consequences, Governance, and Notes. The authors say Governance and Notes are not part of the standard ADR template, but they add a lot of value. Let's walk through them with a team that is stuck in a familiar debate.

The "why do we need this?" conversation

Two engineers have just finished comparing queues and topics. One is exhausted and wants to get back to coding. The other points out that a year from now, a new hire will ask why the team chose queues. Nobody will remember the details. So the team writes an ADR. The engineer who wants to get back to coding grumbles, but the document turns out to be the thing that stops the same argument from happening again.

Title: short, numbered, and noun-heavy

Every ADR starts with a title that names the decision. Keep it meaningful and concise, like a news headline. Most of the words should be nouns, and you should leave the detail for later sections. Start the title with a three-digit number, beginning at 001 and counting up. That sequence tells readers which decisions came first. If you ever pass 999, you would have to revise a bunch of titles and filenames, but the authors find three digits plenty.

Each ADR needs a unique number. Reusing one causes confusion when people refer to decisions by number.

Status: where the decision stands

The status shows where the decision is in its life. A decision is a process, not a single moment. These are the statuses you will meet:

  • Request for Comment (RFC). The ADR is a draft that needs input, often from other teams or an advisory board. RFCs usually affect several teams or a cross-cutting concern like security. Always give an RFC a "respond by" deadline. It is like planning a dinner out: you have a plan, you invite people, and you set a date so you can book the table.
  • Proposed. Comments are in. The ADR now waits for formal approval. You can still change the decision if someone finds a deal-breaker. You are close, but not there yet.
  • Accepted. The decision is made, and everyone who needed to sign off has. This tells the implementing team that they can start. If no outside feedback is needed, an ADR can go straight to Accepted.
  • Superseded. A newer decision has replaced this one. Most ADRs stay Accepted, but things change: the business shifts priorities, or new regulations arrive. The new ADR becomes Accepted, and the old one is marked Superseded.

A survey service changes its database

A customer survey team starts with a relational database for storing results. That is ADR 001 and it is Accepted. Later, the team finds the data is wildly varied and changes shape often, so the relational database no longer fits. They run a new trade-off analysis and choose a document store. They write a new ADR, and they mark the old one Superseded. Both ADRs link to each other. Anyone who finds the old ADR can see it is outdated and follow the link to the new decision.

The bidirectional link matters. It turns your ADR log into a map of what was tried and why it changed. It is part of the project's memory.

Do not edit an Accepted ADR to change the decision. Once accepted, the decision is essentially locked. If it no longer fits, write a new ADR with the next number and mark the old one Superseded. Say ADR 007 stops fitting after you have reached ADR 013. Editing 007 would make readers think the new decision came first, so you write ADR 014 instead. The only expected change to an Accepted ADR is its status moving to Superseded. Adding a few extra facts is fine. Changing the decision itself is not.

Context: the circumstances behind the decision

Every decision is made in a context, with constraints. Your breakfast choice depended on how hungry you were, your lunch plans, and whether you wanted more fiber. Architecture is no different. The Context section explains what drove the decision and every factor that shaped it. Technical reasons usually show up here, but cultural or political ones belong too, because they help the reader see where you were coming from.

If you want to document the trade-off analysis itself, add a separate section called "Alternatives." List each option you considered, along with its pros and cons. Keeping that work in its own section stops the Context from becoming a dumping ground.

Decision: say it plainly, and say why

The Decision section states the decision and justifies it. Use an authoritative voice, with phrases like "we will use." Avoid hedges like "we think" or "we believe." If the ADR is still an RFC or Proposed, the decision is not made yet, but the section should still state the proposed decision clearly.

Always include the reason. The Second Law says "why" matters more than "how," so the justification is the heart of this section. You can also name the people or groups who signed off, such as "the marketing department requires...". That is a way of showing who was involved, often called covering your assets.

The dinner decision

Your best friend's birthday is coming up. The context is the celebration itself and the constraints around it. The alternatives are the restaurants on your list. The decision is "We will go to the pan-Asian bistro." The justification is that its menu has vegetarian and gluten-free options and allows substitutions for anyone with dietary needs. A good Decision section reads the same way: what you chose, and why it beat the others.

An ADR is not an opinion piece. Write it the way a good journalist writes a news story: stick to the facts and keep your tone neutral. The reader should trust the record, not feel like they are being lectured.

Consequences: the good and the bad

Every decision has consequences. If you lifted heavy weights yesterday, you may be sore today, and maybe a little proud. Architecture decisions have the same mix. The Consequences section lists the expected effects, positive and negative, so everyone can judge whether the good outweighs the bad.

Good questions to ask yourself include:

  • How does this change the work of the implementing team? Does it make testing easier or harder? How will we know the work is done?
  • Does it add or remove infrastructure, and what does that involve?
  • Does it affect cross-cutting concerns like security or observability?
  • What does it do to time and money? Does it cost more or save effort?
  • Does it create a one-way path that is hard to undo? For example, the book notes that using queues means you can't control the order of messages.

Consequences are easy to miss when you work alone, so ask others to review them. Different viewpoints reveal different effects. If a decision makes the system simpler or cheaper, say so. That is worth highlighting too.

For the sneaker team, the book's sample consequences are: queues add new infrastructure that must be highly available (so clustering), they mean tighter coupling between services, and any extra downstream consumer will force changes to the trading service.

Choosing a bigger kitchen is a good decision for cooking, but it also means more cleaning, a higher electric bill, and a bigger lease. A complete Consequences section lists both the fun and the bills.

Governance: keeping the decision alive

A decision is only good if it is actually carried out and stays that way. Think of a gym membership you never use. The Governance section explains how you will make sure the organization follows the decision now and later. Options include manual methods like pair programming and code reviews, and automated methods like specialized tests that fail when someone breaks the rule. Governance here is about keeping the architecture on course, not about regulatory compliance.

Notes: metadata about the ADR

The last section records facts about the ADR itself: the original author, the approval date, who approved it, the superseded date, when it was last modified, who changed it, and what changed. Even if your tool already tracks timestamps, put this information in the ADR. Yes, it repeats what the tool knows, but it is easier for someone to find when it lives in the document.

Where to keep ADRs and how to name them

You have plenty of options. A common choice is plain-text files, such as Markdown or AsciiDoc, stored in version control like Git. You get a full history for free. The downside is that non-developers may not know how to work with version control. If you go this route, keep the ADRs in their own repository instead of mixing them with source code.

A wiki is another option. Most wikis use a visual editor, so more people can read and write. Make sure the wiki tracks changes, because you do not want an ADR quietly edited with no one noticing.

Whatever you pick, make sure people can easily add, edit, and search ADRs. Many well-meaning ADR efforts die because nobody can find the document when they need it.

If you store ADRs as files, use the title as the filename, including the number. For example, a decision titled "042: Use queues between the trading and downstream services" becomes 042-use-queues-between-the-trading-and-downstream-services.md: the number, then the lowercase title with hyphens instead of spaces. Lowercase avoids trouble across operating systems. Sorting the folder by name then puts the decisions in order.

Many tools exist for writing ADRs, some of which store them right in source code. Most of them assume a particular file format or folder layout, so try a tool before you commit. The authors' best advice is simple: start without fancy tooling, learn what your team needs, and only then look for a tool.

How far does an ADR reach?

ADRs can be narrow or broad. Some affect one team. Others affect a whole company. A famous example is the "Jeff Bezos API mandate" at Amazon, a decision that all services must talk to each other only through APIs. It touched the entire organization. Cross-team ADRs usually need a lot of collaboration and often the blessing of a central architecture review board. They tend to cover cross-cutting concerns, such as how services talk to each other or which data transfer protocol to use. ADRs about security or regulatory compliance often cut across several teams or the whole organization.

Why bother? The benefits of ADRs

ADRs take some effort, but the payoff is big. The book's case is that they let you follow the Second Law by capturing the "why" along with the "what." They build institutional knowledge, and they help teams learn from one another. The format is a suggestion, so feel free to tweak it.

Keep the ADR process as light as possible. Every extra section adds work, and when the paperwork gets heavy, people stop writing ADRs at all. Focus on concise, clear entries. A short ADR that gets written beats a perfect one that never gets finished.

The Two Many Sneakers story ends well. Customers love the real-time alerts, and the analytics help the security team spot sneaker scams early. The team learned that there are no best practices in architecture, only trade-offs. Their decisions were documented, so they can be understood, questioned, and revised later.

Quick recap

  • There are no universal best practices in software architecture. Every choice comes with trade-offs, which is the First Law.
  • Use trade-off analysis to compare options, weighing both upsides and downsides against the characteristics that matter most to the business.
  • Queues give you control and security but lock in your consumers. Topics give you easy extensibility but a one-size-fits-all message.
  • The Second Law says why is more important than how, so record the reasoning behind each decision.
  • An ADR has seven sections (Title, Status, Context, Decision, Consequences, Governance, Notes) and a numbered title with a three-digit prefix.
  • Statuses such as RFC, Proposed, Accepted, and Superseded show where a decision stands. An Accepted ADR is essentially locked, so change a decision by writing a new ADR that supersedes the old one.

Logical Components: The Building Blocks

Before you can design a system's technical parts, you need to know its functional pieces and how they lean on one another. Logical components, revisited

A logical component is a functional building block of a system. Together, the components describe the problem domain, which is the business problem the software solves. In most programming languages, logical components show up as directories in your source code. If your code for tracking an order lives in a folder such as app/order/tracking, then you have a logical component you could call Order Tracking.

Logical versus physical architecture

There are two ways to look at a system's architecture. The logical architecture shows the building blocks and how they interact. The physical architecture shows the technical details: the architecture style, services, protocols, databases, user interfaces, API gateways, and so on.

The logical architecture is independent of the physical one. The logical view does not care whether a component is a database or a service. It only cares what the component does and what it depends on. The physical view takes those logical components and assigns them to real services and databases. Two views, one system.

Think of a house. The logical view is the floor plan that says "kitchen next to dining room, bedrooms upstairs." The physical view is the wiring, plumbing, and the brand of the oven. You need the floor plan before you decide where the pipes go.

Setting the scene: online auctions

Adventurous Auctions sells once-in-a-lifetime trips, like a safari in Tanzania or a hike to Everest base camp, at live auctions around the country. Now the company wants to take those auctions online too. The new system has to:

  • Combine in-person and online bids in every auction.
  • Scale up to meet demand, so hundreds or even thousands of people can join each auction.
  • Let online users register and store their payment details.
  • Let online users watch live video of the in-person auctions and see all bids so far, in the room and online.
  • Let online users bid on trips, just like the people in the room.
  • Decide which online bidder hits the asking price first (that is "winning the bid"). If an online bidder and someone in the room bid at the same moment, the auctioneer decides who was first.
  • When the auctioneer announces an online user as the winner, charge the card, notify the winner, and move to the next trip.

We will use this system to walk through every step of building a logical architecture.

The four-step loop

Creating a logical architecture takes four steps. The same loop starts a brand-new (greenfield) system and keeps an existing one healthy, so it runs for as long as the system lives:

  1. Identify the initial core components.
  2. Assign requirements to those components.
  3. Analyze each component's roles and responsibilities.
  4. Analyze the architectural characteristics the components must support.

Teams often skip this loop after the first release, and that is why systems turn into messes. Every time you add a feature or change the system, run through the loop again. It keeps the components the right size and makes sure they still do their jobs.

Step 1: Identify the initial core components

Your first guesses will be rough. You know little about the system at this point, and the components will probably get reshuffled later. Do not agonize over the size of each one. The point is to have a starting set that you can refine. Think of these first components as empty jars. They are placeholders that you will fill with responsibilities later.

Two common approaches help reduce the guesswork: the workflow approach and the actor/action approach. Some other approaches look tempting but lead to trouble, which we will get to shortly.

The workflow approach

The workflow approach looks at the major journeys a user takes through the system. Skip the tiny details at first. Start with the main processing steps, then drill down. You can also model several workflows, which can produce more starting components.

A bid on an auction

Walk through one bidder's journey: register, watch the video stream, place a bid, have the bid counted, see the winner announced, pay. Each major step suggests a component, such as Registration, Video Streamer, Bid Capture, and Payment. (This is our own rough walk-through, not the book's exact list.) A component named Video Streamer is still a real component even if you buy the streaming from a third-party service, because it is still part of the logical architecture.

A step does not always map to one component. Several steps can point to the same component if they are closely related. Also, the name of each component matters. A good name tells the reader what the component does in a few words.

The actor/action approach

This approach is handy when the system has several kinds of users, called actors. First, list the actors. Then list the main actions each one takes. Finally, assign each action to a new or existing component. It works well as a fast way to see what the system must do for each person who uses it.

A bakery's ordering system

A bakery wants customers to browse, order, and pay online for pickup. The actors are the customer, the coordinator who buys ingredients and schedules orders, the baker who bakes the items, and the system that emails customers. Customers browse and order, so an Ordering component. The coordinator schedules, so a Scheduling component. The baker reports finished items, so a Production component. The system tells customers their order is ready, so a Notification component.

You can combine the two approaches. Start with actors to find the actions, then use the workflow to put those actions in the order they usually happen. Event storming, a workshop technique from domain-driven design, goes further by also modeling commands, aggregates, and views. Actor/action gives you the domain events and actors from that technique, and stops there.

The entity trap

The entity trap is the approach you should avoid. It models components after the main data entities in the system, such as a "Bid Manager" for bids, an "Order Manager" for orders, and so on. It is easy to fall into, and it causes trouble.

First, names like "Bid Manager" are too vague. You cannot tell what the component does from its name. Second, these components turn into dumping grounds for everything related to the entity. They grow too big, and then they are hard to maintain, scale, and make fault tolerant.

Words like manager, supervisor, and handler in a component name are a warning sign that you may be in the entity trap. Check whether the component has a clear, specific job.

The warning is a rule of thumb, not a law. A component that manages reference data, such as country codes and store codes, might reasonably be called "Reference Data Manager" because it has a specific, general job. Names like "Order Manager" or "Response Handler," on the other hand, are too broad to explain what the component really does.

Step 2: Assign requirements to components

Next, take each functional requirement or user story and decide which component should own it. Because each component is a directory, the code that meets a requirement goes in that component's directory. Every requirement needs a home. If one does not fit any component, you may need a new one.

Early on, requirements come from conversations with the business sponsor. The first set of components for Adventurous Auctions, for example, came from what the company's CIO said about the basic workflow. You then fit each requirement into the component that fits it best.

Step 3: Analyze roles and responsibilities

As you assign requirements, each component collects responsibilities. That is normal, but a component can also grow too much. Imagine a Live Auction Session component that manages the bidding, the video, the payments, and the notifications. It has taken on far too much. The purpose of this step is to catch that early and move some responsibility to other components.

Have you ever written a class called Utility? In many codebases there is a file called Utility that holds a pile of unrelated helper functions. A logical component can become the same kind of junk drawer. If a component's name is vague and its contents are random, it is doing too much.

The key idea here is cohesion. Cohesion is the degree and manner to which a component's operations are interrelated. A component with high cohesion has functions that all belong together. A component with low cohesion is a grab bag. When you review a component, ask whether its functions are closely related. If one function is an odd outlier, or the component is doing too much, move the outlier elsewhere.

Splitting a grab bag

Suppose a Live Auction Session component handles bid intake, the live video feed, and taking a credit card payment. Bid intake and the video feed are about the live event. Taking a payment is about money and belongs with a Payment component. Move that payment work out, and the session component is more focused. You have improved cohesion by offloading one responsibility.

Step 4: Analyze architectural characteristics

The last step checks that each component supports the characteristics that are critical to success. Most of the time this means splitting a component to improve scalability, elasticity, or availability. Sometimes it means merging components whose functions are tightly linked.

For Adventurous Auctions, the critical characteristics are these: the system must handle thousands of bids per second (scalability), it must stay up during live auctions (availability), and it must accept a bid and pass it to the auctioneer as fast as possible (performance).

Take the Bid Capture component. Its responsibilities are to accept bids from online users and from the auctioneer's live bidders, work out which online bid is highest, write every bid to a Bid Tracker database for record keeping, and tell the auctioneer the highest bid. The book's fix gives that write-to-the-database job to a new component called Bid Tracker.

Writing to the database inside Bid Capture is a problem. Database connections and throughput are limited, so writing every bid there slows the component down and hurts scalability. It also adds wait time before the bid reaches the auctioneer, which hurts performance. And if the database goes down, Bid Capture may stop too, which hurts availability.

The fix is to move the tracking job into its own component. Bid Capture then sends bids onward without waiting for the database write. Each component now does one thing well, and the system can take in more bids, faster.

When you analyze characteristics, ask what happens when a dependency fails, how fast each step must run, and how much load each step must handle. Those questions reveal where to split a component.

Component coupling

As you identify components, you also need to see how they depend on each other. This is component coupling: how much components know about and rely on one another. The more they interact, the more tightly coupled the system is, and the harder it is to change.

If you draw every dependency in a real system, you often get a tangle that looks like a bowl of spaghetti. Architects call this a "big ball of mud." You want to avoid it, which means paying close attention to the interactions and the dependencies between components.

There are two kinds of coupling to watch: afferent and efferent. The names are formal, but the ideas are simple.

Afferent coupling: who depends on me?

Afferent coupling is the degree to which other components depend on a target component, counted as the number of components that rely on it. It is sometimes called fan-in or incoming coupling, and code analysis tools usually label it CA. Think of parents: they are depended on by their children, the family dog, and anyone else who relies on them for food and a ride to practice. The more things depend on you, the more afferent coupling you have. The word afferent means "carrying toward", like the nerves that carry signals to your brain.

A profile service with two callers

In Adventurous Auctions, both the Auction Registration component and the Automatic Payment component need bidder profile information from the Bidder Profile component. Two components depend on it, so the Bidder Profile component has an afferent coupling of 2.

Efferent coupling: whom do I depend on?

Efferent coupling is the opposite. It measures how many components a target component depends on. It is also called fan-out or outgoing coupling, and tools usually label it CE. Think of a kid who depends on parents, teachers, friends, and classmates for different things. Their efferent coupling is high.

Accepting a bid

When a bidder named Kate places a bid, the Bid Capture component needs the Bid Streamer and the Bid Tracker to finish the job. It depends on two other components, so its efferent coupling is 2.

Measuring coupling

You can measure coupling three ways. For a given component, total afferent coupling (CA) counts the incoming dependencies and total efferent coupling (CE) counts the outgoing ones. Total coupling (CT) is the sum of the two for a component, or CA plus CE. Together these show which components have the highest and lowest coupling, and how coupled the whole system is.

A tiny three-component system

Checkout  -> Pricing
Checkout  -> Inventory
Reports   -> Pricing

Pricing:   CA = 2 (Checkout, Reports), CE = 0, CT = 2
Checkout:  CA = 0, CE = 2 (Pricing, Inventory), CT = 2
Inventory: CA = 1 (Checkout), CE = 0, CT = 1

Pricing has the most incoming dependencies, so a change there ripples furthest. The number is a clue about where to be careful.

The Law of Demeter: less knowledge, less coupling

Developers are often told to build loosely coupled systems, but rarely shown how. One technique to try is the Law of Demeter, also called the Principle of Least Knowledge. The name comes from the Greek goddess of agriculture, who supplied grain to mortals without knowing what they did with it. She was loosely coupled to the mortal world.

Components work the same way. The more a component knows about other components and about the steps the system takes, the more coupled it is. Reduce what it knows, and you reduce its coupling.

The order placement component

Imagine an Order Placement component that handles checkout. It also knows the exact steps for a low-stock situation: check the warehouse, flag the item, email the supplier, and so on. In the book's version of this system, Order Placement has a total coupling of 5, which is high and lopsided next to its neighbors. Move the low-stock knowledge into the Inventory Management component instead. Order Placement now only asks Inventory whether the item is available. Order Placement knows less, so its coupling drops. Inventory Management knows more, so its coupling rises. Less knowledge, less coupling; more knowledge, more coupling.

Notice that the total coupling in the system did not change. You did not remove the knowledge. You only moved it to another component. The gain is that the load is now balanced, and no single component is taking charge of everything.

Imagine a restaurant where the waiter also cooks, washes dishes, and runs the cash register. The waiter knows far too much about the business. Split the jobs, and each person knows only their part. The restaurant still needs all of those jobs done, but no single person has to know the whole kitchen.

A balancing act: the cost of loose coupling

Loose coupling is not free. Compare two designs for an order system. In the tightly coupled design, the Order Placement component knows every step. If you want to understand what happens when a customer places an order, you read one component. But if pricing or supplier ordering changes, the Order Placement component may need changes too.

In the loosely coupled design, knowledge is spread across components. Changing pricing or supplier ordering does not force a change to Order Placement. But to understand the full workflow, you now have to visit several components. And in the book's example, Order Placement now depends on four other components, so a change in any one of them could break it.

This is the First Law again: everything is a trade-off. Loose coupling reduces dependencies, but it spreads workflow knowledge across the system, which makes that knowledge harder to manage and control.

Two components are coupled if a change in one might force a change in the other. Use that test to spot coupling you may have missed.

Closing thoughts on components

Once you can identify logical components and the dependencies between them, you have the foundation for the rest of your architecture. A system seen as a set of components is easier to understand, so you can see its overall structure and how it works. The next steps move into the technical details: architecture styles, services, databases, and communication protocols.

Quick recap

  • A logical component is a functional building block, usually represented by a source code directory, and it should have a clear, descriptive name.
  • Build a logical architecture in four repeating steps: identify components, assign requirements, analyze responsibilities, and analyze characteristics.
  • Use the workflow approach and the actor/action approach to find your starting components, and combine them when that helps.
  • Avoid the entity trap. Components named after entities, especially with words like manager, tend to become vague and overloaded.
  • Cohesion measures how related a component's functions are. If a component is a grab bag, move the outliers elsewhere.
  • Afferent coupling (CA) counts what depends on you, efferent coupling (CE) counts what you depend on, and total coupling (CT) is CA plus CE.
  • The Law of Demeter says less knowledge means less coupling. Moving knowledge balances the load but does not remove it, and looser coupling spreads workflow knowledge across more components.

Architectural Styles: The Map and the Compass

There are hundreds of architectural styles, and new ones show up every year. Each one exists for a reason and has its own point of view on how a system should be built. This chapter gives you a simple map for sorting them, so you can tell at a glance what a style is good at and where it will hurt you.

Why you need a map at all

Think about the houses on your street. Some are tiny and cozy, some are tall and skinny, and some look like they came from a different continent. Each one was shaped by the climate, the land, and the people who lived in it. Software architecture styles work the same way. You will never memorize all of them, and you do not need to. You just need a few questions that help you place any style you meet.

The book gives you two questions. The first is about how the code is divided up. The second is about how the code gets shipped. Answer both and you know a lot about what a style will feel like to build and run.

Question one: how is the code divided?

This is called partitioning, which just means splitting a system into pieces. There are two main ways to do it.

Technical partitioning

In a technically partitioned system, the pieces are split by technical job. You get a presentation piece that talks to users, a business or services piece that applies the rules, and a persistence piece that talks to the database. This is called separation of concerns, and most people picture it as horizontal layers stacked on top of each other.

Think about a restaurant. The host seats you, the server takes your order, the cook makes the food, and the busser clears the table. Each person has one technical job. You would not want the busser cooking your steak, and the cook probably should not be the one seating guests.

Domain partitioning

In a domain partitioned system, the pieces follow the problem you are solving. The "domain" is simply the thing your software is about, such as orders, customers, or payments. Each domain gets its own bundle of code, and inside that bundle you may still have screens and databases. The business idea comes first, and the technical details are just how it gets built.

Now picture a food court. Each stall focuses on one kind of food, like pizza, salads, or burgers. Inside each stall there may be a cook and a cashier, but the stall itself is organized around the food, not around the job title.

The same customer code, two ways

Imagine you are building a shop app with a customer feature. A technically partitioned project might group code like this: app.presentation.customer and app.services.customer. A domain partitioned project flips it: app.customer.presentation and app.customer.services. In both, customer code exists, but the first sorts by technical role and the second sorts by business area.

Technical partitioning works well when your team is organized by skill, such as frontend experts, backend developers, and database administrators. Domain partitioning lines up better with the actual problem. Neither one is automatically better. Your team shape and your problem decide.

Question two: how is the system deployed?

Deployment is about how many units you ship. Is everything delivered together as one package, or is it split into several packages that run separately?

Monolithic deployment

A monolithic system is delivered as one unit, and the whole application runs as one process. Think of a single JAR or WAR file in the Java world, or a single executable in the .NET world.

A small shop app

Your shop app has login, a shopping cart, and a reporting screen. In a monolith, all three live in one codebase, get built into one file, and run in one process. Deploying a fix to the cart means redeploying the whole thing.

Distributed deployment

A distributed system splits its logical pieces into several units, usually smaller. Each unit runs in its own process and talks to the others over a network.

The same shop, split up

Now login, cart, and reporting are three separate services. Each has its own deployment. The cart talks to login over the network to check who you are. You can redeploy the cart without touching login.

The monolith: the good and the bad

Monoliths get a bad reputation, but they earn their place. Because everything runs in one process, they are simpler to build at first. Because everything ships together, tracing an error is much easier: you follow one stack trace from start to finish. They are often cheaper too, which makes them a strong choice when you need to get a product to market quickly. The book names five things monoliths are good at:

  • Simplicity: usually one codebase, easy to develop and understand.
  • Cost: cheaper to build and run, since they need less infrastructure.
  • Feasibility: great when you are rushing to market or experimenting.
  • Debuggability: all the code is in one place, so an error stack trace leads you right to the problem.
  • Reliability: few or no network calls, so fewer things to go wrong (at least at first).

The trouble shows up as the app grows. Many of those strengths turn into weak spots:

  • Scalability: scaling a monolith is all or nothing. If your reporting feature eats memory, you have to scale the entire app to deal with it.
  • Reliability: because it deploys as one unit, a bug that degrades the service hits the whole monolith.
  • Evolvability: changes get harder as the app grows, and with one codebase you cannot give different domains different technology stacks.
  • Deployability: any change means redeploying the whole application, which adds risk.

The distributed system: the good and the bad

Distributed systems shine where monoliths struggle. Because each part is deployed on its own, you can scale only the busy parts. Because the parts are physically separate, they tend to be loosely coupled, which means changing one rarely breaks another. A failure in one service can often be contained rather than crashing everything. Each deployment serves only a few components, so testing is much easier, even as the app grows. That low coupling also means high modularity. And lots of small units are easy to deploy, which fits modern habits like continuous integration, continuous deployment, and automated testing.

But distributed systems come with a price tag, and it is a big one. They depend on the network, and the network is not reliable, not instant, and not free. A famous list called "The Fallacies of Distributed Computing," compiled in the 1990s by L. Peter Deutsch and others at Sun Microsystems, lists the false assumptions developers often make about networks. Read it before you start. The book calls out four specific costs:

  • Performance: many small services chatting over the network can slow things down.
  • Cost: more servers, plus network infrastructure to set up and maintain.
  • Simplicity: distributed systems are the opposite of simple, from understanding them to debugging them.
  • Debuggability: an error could happen in any service that touched a request, so tracing it is tricky. It takes careful logging and usually log aggregation, which adds to the cost.

It is easy to underestimate how hard distributed computing is. Teams often choose a distributed design because it sounds modern, then discover they have bought a lot of network complexity they did not need.

Every architectural style sits in one of four quadrants: technically or domain partitioned, crossed with monolithic or distributed. Each quadrant carries both pros and cons. (The book also notes there are domain-specific architectures built for particular problems, but it does not cover them.) When you read about a new style, ask which box it lives in. That one question tells you a lot about what it will be strong at and what it will struggle with.

No winners, only trade-offs

You may be tempted to rank these styles from best to worst. The book refuses to do that, and so should you. A style is a set of trade-offs. Your choice depends on your domain, your team, your budget, and the architectural characteristics your system must have, such as speed, reliability, or the ability to change quickly. Match the style to those needs, not to the hype.

The book stages this as a fireside chat. Picture a monolith and a distributed system as two people in a debate. The monolith says, "I'm simpler, cheaper, and faster to start." The distributed system replies, "Sure, but I can keep growing when you can't." Both are right, and the real question is which one you will need next year.

Quick recap

  • Architectural styles can be sorted with two questions: how is the code divided, and how is it deployed.
  • Technical partitioning groups code by job, such as presentation, business rules, and persistence. Domain partitioning groups code by business area.
  • A monolith ships as one unit and runs as one process. It is simple and cheap early on, but it scales all or nothing.
  • A distributed system splits the pieces into separate units that talk over the network. It scales well and contains failures, but it is expensive and hard to debug.
  • The network is the hidden villain of distributed systems. Assume it will fail or slow down.
  • Every style is a set of trade-offs. There is no universally best style, only the best fit for your circumstances.

Layered Architecture: Stacking Responsibilities Neatly

Got a simple project and a deadline breathing down your neck? The layered architecture is often the first style people reach for, and for good reason. It is easy to understand, easy to build, and it turns familiar design ideas into a whole-system shape. Let's follow a small sandwich shop and see how it works.

Start with the right question

Before you pick any architecture, ask how long the thing will live. If you are building something disposable, like a quick experiment, you can skip most of the ceremony. If the system will stick around, choose the simplest architecture that still gives you some real organization, and that does not slow your delivery to a crawl. That sweet spot is where the layered architecture lives.

Meet Naan & Pop

Sangita runs Naan & Pop, a small, cheerful restaurant that serves flatbread sandwiches and sodas. She wants a website where customers can order online. The budget is small, and the restaurant is already open, so every day without the site is a day of lost sales. She gives the development team three requirements.

  • Time to market. Get the site online fast, and keep it simple.
  • Separation of responsibilities. The company has part-time specialists, such as UI designers and database administrators, so each part of the system should be easy to hand to the right person.
  • Simple, yet extensible. She wants to build on the online presence later and reuse parts of the system.

Why not just grab a framework?

One developer suggests using an existing framework and calling it done. That would hit the speed goal. But frameworks can be rigid, and Sangita wants room to grow. Maybe later she wants a mobile interface, or a link to a delivery service. A pile of simple code can make that hard. The team needs a simple style that still separates the parts of the app.

Borrowing from MVC

The team's first thought is the Model-View-Controller pattern, which is a famous design pattern. A design pattern is a tried-and-true answer to a common problem in writing code. MVC splits the work by purpose:

  • The model holds the business logic and the core data of the app.
  • The view is the user interface, the part people see and touch.
  • The controller handles the flow. It takes a request, uses the model to do the work, and picks the view to show the result.

MVC is about logical roles inside your code. Architecture has to go further, because it must deal with real things like browsers, phones, and databases. So the team takes the same spirit of separation and turns it into physical layers.

The three classic layers

A typical layered architecture has three main layers, stacked from top to bottom.

  • Presentation layer. The top layer. It talks to the user and shows the screens. It plays the same role as the view in MVC.
  • Workflow layer (also called the business rules layer). It takes each request from the layer above, applies the rules, and sends back a response.
  • Persistence layer (also called the data access layer). It fetches and saves data in the database and hands results to the workflow layer.
  Browser or phone app
          |
  Presentation layer    (screens, forms, what the user sees)
          |
  Workflow layer        (business rules, order logic)
          |
  Persistence layer     (reads and writes the data)
          |
       Database

In code, these layers become packages or namespaces (packages in Java, namespaces in .NET). For Naan & Pop, that looks like com.naanpop.orderapp.presentation, .workflow, .model, and .persistence. The layout on disk usually mirrors this, since packages often map to the filesystem.

The layered architecture is a technically partitioned style, typically deployed as a monolith (the whole system, code and database, ships as one unit). The three layers are common, not required. Most apps have at least some of that split: the UI apart from the core logic, and the core logic apart from the database. The idea of layers is also very old, so the book suspects it inspired MVC, not the other way round.

How a request moves through the stack

A layered system is a bit like a relay race where every runner touches the baton. When a customer clicks "Place order," the presentation layer takes the request. It passes it down to the workflow layer, which applies the business rules. If the order needs saving, the request keeps going down to the persistence layer and into the database. Then the answer travels back up the same path to the customer's screen.

Adding a bonus points layer

Picture a hotel loyalty program. Each purchase can earn bonus points, depending on membership level, years of membership, and a pile of other rules. One team kept all of that math in a single "bonus" layer. Every purchase flowed through it, so the calculations lived in one place. Layers are not only for the UI and the database. You can add a layer for any job that every request needs.

Where do the domain pieces go?

Earlier in the book the team found logical components for the problem, such as Delivery Address, Billing Address, Sales Promotion, Blacklisted Customers, and Frequent Diner Rewards. But layers slice by technical capability, not by domain. So each logical component is split into its workflow and its entities, and those parts are then laid over the layers. Some components land in only one or two layers, and some span several. Why bother finding logical components first? Because they describe the problem. Mapping them onto any architecture means trading something away. The next chapter shows a more direct mapping, with trade-offs of its own.

Adding layers on purpose

The basic three layers are common, but they are not required. Teams add layers when they need a new responsibility. Two popular examples are:

  • A services layer that gives other applications, such as business partners, a clean way to talk to your app.
  • An integration layer that handles links to other systems, such as a third-party delivery service.

There is one golden rule. Because every request passes through every layer, a layer should hold something that happens to every request. A layer that only matters to a few cases will just slow everything else down.

Layers in the real world

The layered monolith describes the logical shape of the system. You can still deploy it in different physical setups, and each one has its own trade-offs.

  • Two-tier, also called client/server. Presentation, business rules, and persistence are bundled into a single deployment unit, which talks to the database over a local network. Think of corporate accounting software that runs as a desktop app on a shared database. Pros: simple, short time to market, and fewer tiers tend to mean better performance. Con: poorer scalability.
  • Three-tier, the classic web setup. The presentation layer is its own unit, often built with a different tech stack (say HTML and JavaScript). An application server holds business rules and persistence (say in Java), and it uses a relational database. A high-scale streaming video service is the book's example. Pros: good separation and better scalability. Cons: more parts means more complexity, which slows time to market.
  • Embedded or mobile. Because of physical limits, all the layers, database included, end up in one deployment. This fits embedded systems and mobile apps where a network connection may be flaky or missing, like a mobile game or a soda machine. Pro: self-contained, with the fewest parts. Cons: hard to extend, and hard to get high scalability or performance.

The book's point: they all have trade-offs, like everything in architecture.

Why teams love it

The layered architecture has a lot going for it. Here are the main reasons it keeps coming back.

  • Feasibility. It is simple, with few moving parts, so you can build small systems fast.
  • Popularity. It is simple, it maps closely to MVC, it is so common that teams build simple projects fast in it, and many companies already split people by skill, which matches the layers.
  • Specialization. Teams can be split into specialists, such as UI designers, coders, and database experts, and those specialists can share skills across projects.
  • Technical reuse. If all your persistence code lives in one layer, other parts of the system can find and reuse it. Several teams can share the same data layer.
  • Physical match. Layers often map to real separations, such as a JavaScript front end, a Java back end, and a MySQL database.
  • Familiar shape. Developers already know the MVC idea, so the layered design feels natural.

A mobile version appears

Later, Naan & Pop wants a separate mobile interface. Because the screens live in their own layer, the team builds a new presentation layer for mobile. The workflow and persistence layers stay put. That is the layered architecture at its best: a new kind of user interface affects only one layer.

The catch: the domain gets smeared

Here is the big trade-off. Layers slice the system by technical capability, but your business logic is not technical. A single business feature, such as "Place order," needs a screen, some workflow rules, and a database table. So that feature is spread across every layer.

The book puts it as a meeting of two philosophies. Domain-driven design focuses on separating by business area, while layers separate by technology. A layered system can host a domain, but the domain will likely have to split across the layers.

That makes technical changes easy and domain changes painful. Swapping the screen technology or the database touches one layer. But adding a new kind of thing to sell, like pizza with custom toppings, can ripple through the screens, the rules, and the data at once.

Layered architectures make technical changes easier but domain changes harder. If your business keeps changing its products, rules, or processes, expect the layers to fight you. That is a sign to consider a style better suited to domain change.

Think of a layered cake. You can swap the frosting without disturbing the sponge. But if you want to add a whole new flavor that changes the batter, the cream, and the decoration all at once, you will be making a mess across every tier.

Superpowers and kryptonite

Let's sum up the strengths and weaknesses.

Superpowers (it is one of the oldest recognizable styles): feasibility, since simplicity suits tight time and budget; technical partitioning, so common capabilities, like a shared persistence layer, are built once and reused across teams; data-intensive work, because data processing sits in a single database optimized for it; performance, since a well-designed layered monolith makes no network calls; quick builds for small systems; and "lean and mean", because keeping the system small dodges much of the kryptonite.

Kryptonite: many teams default to this style for its simplicity and long history without asking if it fits. The book lists five weak spots:

  • Deployability: deployments get more complex as the monolith grows and keeps gaining behavior.
  • Big ball of mud: everything connects to everything, so without careful governance it becomes a highly coupled mess.
  • Scalability: one bucket that keeps filling up. Eventually a resource, like memory, runs out.
  • Elasticity: a single process struggles with sudden bursts of users.
  • Testability: high coupling and a big codebase make testing harder over time.

  • Its classic layers are presentation (UI), workflow (business rules), and persistence (data access). Teams add layers like services or integration when they need them.
  • Every request flows down through the layers and back up, so each layer should hold something every request needs.

Modular Monoliths: Slicing by Business

A layered monolith can take you a long way, but there is a point where it starts to creak. When changes need lots of coordination across teams, you may need a different kind of monolith. The modular monolith keeps the single deployment you already know, but organizes the code around the business instead of the tech stack.

When the layers start to creak

Remember Naan & Pop? Its layered app worked well. Then the owners wanted to add pizza to the menu. That sounds like a small change, but it mostly hits the ordering side: a new menu listing, topping customization, pricing rules, delivery rules (nobody wants a cold pizza), and how orders get stored. The recipe and inventory areas barely notice, because a recipe is still just ingredients plus steps. In a layered app, each of those touchpoints lives in a different layer, so the change crosses every specialist's desk.

A team member puts it bluntly: when the layered architecture spreads the domain across all layers, any change to the domain becomes a coordination project. The layered design was fast to launch, and it still has its place. But the team now needs to mature the architecture so that changes like this get easier.

What is a modular monolith?

A modular monolith is still one deployment, usually backed by one database, just like a layered monolith. The big difference is how the code is split. Instead of partitioning by technical concern, you partition by business domain, using modules.

Every business lives in some domain, such as banking, education, or retail. Big domains break into smaller pieces called subdomains. The book uses the terms interchangeably, and you can too, as long as both mean business concerns.

An online store

Your online store is one domain. Inside it you might have subdomains for Order Placement, Payment, and Inventory Management. A modular monolith organizes the codebase so that each subdomain is a module. The whole store still deploys as one unit, typically one codebase with each module in its own namespace.

So what is a module? At its heart, a module is just a way to organize code into an independent unit that handles one piece of functionality. Some languages give you packages or namespaces to help. In fact, every layer in a layered design is technically a module too. The difference is what the module is divided by: technical job in one case, business area in the other.

Vertical slices, not horizontal layers

Here is a good image for the shift. When you eat a burger, you usually take one bite that goes through every layer at once: bun, cheese, patty, and all. A modular monolith works the same way. Instead of horizontal layers cut by technical role, you have vertical slices cut by business concern. Each slice is encapsulated in a module and holds all the business functionality for one area.

For example, order placement, order completion, and order delivery would all sit inside an Order Placement module. The people who work on that module can own the full slice, from the screen down to the data.

Cross-functional teams

Vertical slices lead to a change in team shape. Instead of a team that only does UI and another that only does databases, you get cross-functional teams. Each team owns one or more domains and has everything it needs to deliver changes in that domain. That makes domain changes far easier to coordinate, because one team takes full ownership.

Cross-functional teams are not business-word bingo. They can be hard to build when your people are used to working in only one technical stack.

Layers still exist inside modules

Here is a common question: doesn't each module still have layers? Yes, it can. A module still has to take in a request, apply business logic, and store data, so you may still use presentation, workflow, and persistence parts inside it. The key difference is that these layers become an implementation detail of the module. Zoom out and what you see is the business split into subdomains. The whole architecture is domain partitioned, even if the inside of a module looks layered.

Keeping modules from tangling

Here is the tricky part. Because everything sits in one codebase, it is very easy for one module to reach into another. Say the ordering code needs to know whether the kitchen still has mushrooms. Someone might just call the inventory code directly. Do that across the board and soon every module references every other module. You have a big ball of mud again, and the modularity is gone.

The philosophy is to keep modules loosely coupled, so that changing one does not affect the others. Good modules expose a public API, which is the set of functions other modules are allowed to call. Everything else stays private. A handy way to think about it: treat each module like a separate service that hides its insides, even though it is not really separate and still ships in the same deployment.

Asking inventory politely

The Order module needs to know if mushrooms are in stock. Instead of touching the inventory module's internal tables or classes, it calls the Inventory module's public API and asks, "Do we have mushrooms?" The inventory team can rework its internals tomorrow, and as long as the public API stays the same, ordering keeps working.

Your IDE's auto-import feature is not your friend here. It is very easy to accidentally import a class from another module without noticing. Watch imports during code review.

Ways to enforce module boundaries

Good intentions are not enough. You want tools that keep boundaries in place. The book describes several options, and you can combine them.

  • Language features. Java has the Java Platform Module System (JPMS), which isolates modules from each other. .NET offers namespaces with the internal keyword for a similar purpose.
  • Separate subprojects. Put each module in its own folder with its own build file. Build tools such as Gradle support these multimodule projects. Some teams even use separate repositories, then stitch everything together at build time.
  • Governance tools. Architecture testing tools like ArchUnit for Java and ArchUnitNET for .NET can fail your build if one module reaches into another. For TypeScript and JavaScript, ts-arch does a similar job.

These options are not either-or. Use one or several together.

You are still deploying a monolith, so you will bring the modules back together at build time. A monolithic deployment does not have to mean a monolithic codebase. And these techniques help even in a layered app, so they are worth learning no matter which style you pick.

Modularizing the database too

Separate code is not enough if all your data is still tangled. If every module reads and writes the same tables, you have simply moved the ball of mud into the database. The rule is simple: every module should access only its own tables.

In practice, you define a schema, which is a named group of tables, for each module. Data that belongs to a module lives only in that module's tables. The separation shows up in the database itself, as different tables and possibly different schemas.

One upside of a single database is simplicity: you do not worry about distributed transactions or eventual consistency, and most developers are comfortable with it. Modularizing the data is what you do when you want modularity at every level.

A schema per module (illustrative)

orders_schema      -> orders, order_items
recipes_schema     -> recipes, recipe_steps
inventory_schema   -> ingredients, stock_levels

Notice that order_items may store a recipe item's ID. This is not a foreign key reference, and it does not join to the recipes table. When the Order module needs the recipe's name or price, it calls the Recipe module's API with that ID.

Watch out for SQL joins across module boundaries. It is easy to slip up and join tables that belong to different modules. Each join quietly ties the two modules together again. Storing an ID from another module is fine. Joining into that module's tables is not.

Start with code, then go deeper

Most developers are not used to thinking in vertical business slices, and modularizing the database is an even bigger leap. You do not have to do everything at once. Start by modularizing the code. Once the team gets the hang of thinking modularly, see whether it helps to take that all the way into the database. Evolve the architecture over time rather than trying to get it perfect on day one.

It is also common to start with a layered monolith and refactor it into a modular one as the application grows. That is a normal path.

Superpowers and kryptonite

Superpowers:

  • Domain partitioning. Components follow business concerns, and the key strength is that teams can specialize in a domain.
  • Domain-based alignment. Cross-functional teams line up with the business rather than with technical roles.
  • Performance. Like most monoliths, it is fast. No network calls run between modules, and data processing happens in one place.
  • Maintainability. Changes stay inside a domain, so the code is easier to maintain, as long as changes do not spill across domains.
  • Testability. A change is limited to one module, so testing is easier. A team that knows its subdomain well can build integration, smoke, and end-to-end tests for it.

Kryptonite:

  • Hard to reuse. Sharing logic between modules means extracting it as a dependency, which adds coupling.
  • One set of characteristics. The whole application gets one set of architectural characteristics, even if one business area needs something different.
  • Fragile boundaries. It is easy to blur module lines by accident, and the database is even harder to govern than the code.
  • Operational limits. It is still a monolith, so elasticity and fault tolerance are hard to achieve.

On process characteristics (maintainability, testability, deployability) it does a lot better than layered, because a change to one module affects only that module and can be tested in isolation, which lowers deployment risk. It costs a bit more than layered because it needs planning, vigilance, governance, and tooling.

A modular monolith is like an apartment building with clean walls. Each unit has its own door, its own plumbing, and its own wiring. Neighbors can knock on each other's doors through the front entrance, which is the public API, but nobody gets to knock out a wall. Building it takes more planning than a single open loft, but remodeling one apartment no longer means rewiring the whole building.

In return, they give cross-functional teams room to work independently and move faster. Whether that trade is worth it depends on your domain and your team.

  • A modular monolith is still one deployment, but it is partitioned by business domain and subdomain, not by technical layer.
  • Each module is a vertical slice that holds the business functions for one area, and it can have layers inside as an implementation detail.
  • Modules talk only through public APIs. Direct reaches into another module's internals turn modules back into a ball of mud.
  • Enforce boundaries with language features, separate subprojects, and architecture tools like ArchUnit or ts-arch.
  • Extend modularity to the database: each module owns its tables, avoids cross-module joins, and stores IDs instead of foreign keys.
  • The style is strong on domain change, performance, and testing (3 stars for most process traits, 4 for simplicity), but it is harder to reuse code in, and scalability, elasticity, and fault tolerance get just 1 star each.

Microkernel Architecture: Plug In and Customize

Some systems need to be customized one capability at a time, and some need new behaviors added without touching what already works. The microkernel architecture is built for exactly that. Once you understand its two parts, you will start spotting it everywhere, from code editors to recycling apps.

The Going Green problem

Going Green is a fast-moving startup that buys old cell phones, music players, and other small electronics, then recycles them or resells them. The business makes money when it resells the highest-value devices it receives. Newer devices are usually in better shape and worth more, so the faster Going Green can add the assessment rules for each new device, the more money it earns.

The architects designed a three-part system, and each part needs different capabilities. The one that matters here is the device assessment service, which works out what a device is worth. New device configurations arrive at least a few times a month, sometimes a few times a week. The team also has to make sure that adding support for new devices does not break support for the old ones. Rapid change, combined with stability, is the heart of the problem.

The team's answer is the microkernel architecture, a style that makes it easier to design and add capabilities using plugins.

Two parts: a core and some plugins

The name comes from operating system design. The kernel of an operating system is very small and offers only the most basic functions. Everything else plugs in around it. A microkernel architecture has the same shape, with two primary parts:

  • The core, which holds the basic capabilities and the stable logic of the system.
  • Plugins, any number of them, which add or customize behaviors on top of the core.

The device assessment core

The core knows the general criteria for assessing any device, such as its age, its condition, and its model number. For each device type, the core hands the work to a device-specific plugin. The plugin runs the rules for that device and returns its estimated resale value. Adding a new device means writing a new plugin, not changing the core.

Partitioning and deployment

Most microkernel systems are technically partitioned, because the core can be split into layers such as presentation and business logic. But the style can also be domain partitioned, depending on how you design your plugins. Usually the deployment is monolithic, but in some situations a distributed model makes more sense. The style lives in a gray zone between monolithic and distributed, and it often shows up in hybrid architectures where customization matters. Inside the core, you are free to organize things your way: layers, or domain-style components.

Not every plugin system is a microkernel

Lots of software supports plugins, including IDEs, web browsers, and build tools. But supporting plugins does not automatically make a system a microkernel. The deciding factor is the core. There is no official dividing line, so the book suggests judging how "microkernel-y" a system is, often called "microkern-ality." Two questions help:

  • How useful is the core on its own, without any plugins?
  • How volatile is the core, meaning how often does it need to change?

In extreme microkernels, the core can do very little useful work until plugins are installed. A core that is small, stable, and only really useful with plugins is the classic case.

Microkernel and microservices are not the same thing. The similar names are a coincidence. Microkernel comes from operating system design. Microservices are named for their small, separate deployment units in a distributed system.

Plugin contracts: how plugins plug in

The core is where plugins connect. The link is usually an interface, which is a contract that says what methods a plugin must provide. The plugin implements the interface, and the core calls the plugin through it. The core does not need to know the plugin's inner details.

In a typical microkernel, the communication runs between the core and each plugin, not between plugins. A plugin like PluginA neither knows nor cares about the others. If plugins ever do need to talk, the core has to sit in the middle.

A/B testing (a common plugin use)

Plugins show up a lot in A/B testing. The architects keep the old behavior in PluginA, add the new behavior in PluginB, and decide which one to call. Neither plugin needs to know the other exists.

Encapsulated versus distributed plugins

There are two broad ways to run the plugins, and they match the two deployment models from Chapter 5.

  • Encapsulated plugins live inside a monolith. You implement each one as a native component of the platform, such as a JAR for Java, a DLL for .NET, or a gem for Ruby. They run in the same process as the core and use the same language.
  • Distributed plugins run as separate services. The core reaches them through web endpoints, event queues, and similar mechanisms. Because they are separate, they can be written in different languages, as long as the core can call them over a network.

Calls to distributed plugins can be synchronous, where the core waits for an answer, or asynchronous, where the core sends a message and keeps going. Chapter 11 covers this choice in more depth. For monoliths, asynchronous plugin calls often use threads, and for distributed plugins they often use messaging. Either way, distributed plugins can be built on any stack you can reach over a network.

Going Green's choice

The team decides to define an interface, called DeviceInterface, that each device plugin must implement. From then on, supporting a new device means implementing that interface and customizing the valuation logic for that device.

They weigh the two physical options. A monolith would load plugins into the same process, which is fast, but adding a new device means restarting the core to load the new plugin. A distributed setup avoids the restart, which gives better availability, and lets each plugin be deployed on its own, which is simpler than redeploying the whole service. It also scales better and lets future plugins use other languages. The cost is network calls, which are slower than calls inside one process.

The team chose distributed plugins for scalability, with synchronous calls, since the service was responsive enough that asynchronous messaging would only add complexity.

A new device at 2 p.m.

A new model of tablet appears on the market. In the monolithic version, the team builds the plugin and then restarts the whole assessment service to load it, which means a short outage. In the distributed version, the team deploys just the tablet plugin. The core keeps running, and the other devices never notice.

Should plugins talk to each other?

Plugins can communicate through the core, but you should be careful about it. Plugins typically talk only to the core. If one plugin needs another, the core has to act as a middleman, essentially an integration layer. That is possible, and the Eclipse IDE does it so that language tools like compilers and debuggers can work together. But inter-plugin communication has real costs.

  • It requires consistent contracts between the core and every plugin, and those contracts eventually need versioning. Eclipse's transitive dependencies between components are one thing that makes it complex.
  • It creates dependencies between plugins, so every plugin you depend on has to be present at runtime, which hurts availability.

PluginX leans on PluginY (illustrative)

Suppose PluginX calls PluginY through the core. Now you update PluginY in a way that changes the contract between them. PluginX may break even though nobody touched it, unless the core manages versions and dependencies. That is extra work you would not have if the plugins stayed independent.

Where does the UI fit?

It depends. In a monolithic microkernel, the UI is often part of the core. In a distributed microkernel service, other parts of the system usually handle the UI. The microkernel can also support the UI itself, which is useful when you need custom screens for iOS, Android, and the web, each as its own plugin-like endpoint.

Superpowers and kryptonite

Superpowers:

  • Custom behavior. It is shaped for customization, one plugin at a time.
  • Evolvability. You can gradually move the system away from old behaviors. Unlike adaptable designs, evolvable ones keep the old behavior only for a short time. Plugins make that transition clean.
  • Partitioning. Customization is handled by architecture, not just by careful design. If the system is built around plugins, the boundary is clear.
  • Adaptability. You can keep the old plugins while you add new ones.
  • Simple structure. There are just two basic moving parts, so developers can understand it quickly.

Kryptonite:

  • Misaligned volatility. The core should change very little once it is built. If it changes all the time, you probably picked the wrong style, or you put the wrong things in plugins. A too-volatile core is one of the most common mistakes.
  • Sharing between plugins. Sharing libraries between plugins is tempting, but it creates coupling and deployment headaches. Keep an eye on it.
  • Chatty plugins. When plugins talk to each other a lot, the trade-offs pile up. Successful systems that do this, such as Eclipse, pay for it with high complexity.
  • Performance. With distributed plugins, network calls can slow things down, depending on the protocol and how much data moves between core and plugins.

Where you will find it

The microkernel shows up in many places: IDEs, text processing tools, build and deployment tools, integration platforms, translation layers, insurance applications, and electronics recycling systems. It is not the only way to handle customization. It fits best when the system has a stable core and distinct, isolated customizations, meaning distinct categories of volatility.   • Plugins usually talk only to the core through a shared interface, and the core is the middleman if plugins need each other.
  • Plugins can be encapsulated in a monolith, which is fast and same-language, or distributed as services, which scale better and can use other languages.
  • Going Green used a distributed design with a DeviceInterface, so new devices arrive as plugins with no core restarts.

  • The microkernel is a common, practical style that shows up in tools you already use every day.

Microservices: Small Pieces, Big Changes

Business changes faster than ever, and your architecture has to keep up without falling over. Microservices is a style built for exactly that: many small, separately shipped pieces that can change, grow, and recover on their own. It is also one of the trickiest styles to get right, so let's take it one bit at a time.

Meet MonitorMe: a hospital case study

Picture StayHealthy, a company that makes patient-monitoring systems. Doctors and nurses use its software to watch heart rate, oxygen level, temperature, blood sugar, and whether a patient is asleep or awake. When something crosses a preset line, a person gets alerted right away. The new version, MonitorMe, has to read from eight equipment sources, keep five minutes of history for every vital sign, and run one full copy per hospital, with up to 500 patients in each building.

You are the architect on this job. Before you pick a style, you need a list of what matters most. The chapter asks you to rank the top characteristics for MonitorMe. The book's own top five for MonitorMe are testability (how easy and complete testing is), responsiveness (how fast a medical professional hears about a problem), fault tolerance (keeping going when a part breaks), data integrity (health data must be accurate), and concurrency (watching many vital signs at the same instant). It marks deployability, abstraction, scalability, and workflow as not critical, because the requirements don't ask for many changes, and the system covers a single hospital at a time. Keep that in mind; you will see why microservices looks so good for these needs.

Someone on your team says, "Microservices is obviously the perfect fit!" Before you agree, ask why. Assumptions like that are a classic trap. A good architect asks what the style actually does, what it costs, and whether those costs are worth paying here.

What a microservice really is

A service is a separately deployed unit of software that does some business or infrastructure job. A single service can still be big. A "Monitor All Vital Signs" service might handle heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, and more, and it is still just a service.

A microservice is different. The micro part describes what the service does, not how many lines of code it has. A microservice is a single-purpose, separately deployed unit of software that does one thing really well. A "Monitor Heart Rate" service that only watches heart rate is a microservice. A service that does every vital sign is not.

The single-purpose test

The book runs through a short list. "Add a movie to my to-watch list," "pay for an order with a credit card," and "work out the shipping cost for an order" are all single-purpose, so they are fine microservice candidates. "Generate sales forecasts and financial reports" and "submit and process a loan application" are complex processes with lots of different functions, so they are not. (A gut check of our own, not the book's: if you can't describe the job without piling on "and"s, it probably isn't single-purpose.)

Your data belongs to the service

The second defining trait is data ownership. Each microservice is the only one allowed to read and write its own data. Other services that need that data must ask the owner for it instead of reaching into its tables.

Why such a strict rule? Change control. Imagine fifty microservices all reading the same table. If one team changes the table's layout, forty-nine other services may break on the same day. Nobody wants that. Tying a service to its own data, so the two form one unit, is called a physical bounded context. The bounded context is the fence around the service and its data. Inside the fence, the owner can change things freely. Outside it, everyone has to ask politely. Remember that the context includes the service and all of its data, not just the data.

The Sleep Status data

The Monitor Heart Rate service needs to know whether a patient is asleep. It does not touch the Sleep Status tables. Instead, it asks the Sleep Status service for the answer. Now the Sleep Status team can reshape its data structures whenever it likes, and the heart rate service doesn't need to change at all.

Whenever you see a service reaching into another service's data, treat it as a red flag. The fence is what buys you independent change.

How micro is "micro"? Granularity

Granularity means how big or small a service's scope is. It has nothing to do with physical size. You can have a huge codebase that is coarse-grained, or a tiny one that is fine-grained.

Take blood pressure monitoring in MonitorMe. You could model it three ways:

  • Option 1: one service that does all monitoring and alerting for blood pressure.
  • Option 2: two services, one that captures and records readings, and one that analyzes them and raises alarms.
  • Option 3: four services, each doing one specific monitoring function (capture, record, analyze, alert).

None of these is automatically right. Option 1 is coarse. Option 3 is fine. The chapter's job is to teach you how to choose.

Going too fine has a name: the Grains of Sand antipattern (a bad design habit). Services get so tiny they look like sand on a beach. They end up chatting constantly to finish one business task, which means lots of coupling, slow responses, and reliability problems. Going too coarse is the opposite mistake. Big services are harder and more expensive to maintain, test, and scale, which defeats the whole point.

To find the right size, you apply two kinds of forces. Granularity disintegrators push you to split a service into smaller ones. Granularity integrators push you to merge services back together. Let's use the three basic vital signs (blood pressure, temperature, and heart rate) to see both forces at work.

Forces that push you smaller (disintegrators)

  • Cohesion. A good microservice does things that belong together. If a service's functions have little in common, split it.
  • Fault tolerance and availability. In a big service, one crashing function can take every function down with it. Put the fragile function in its own service, and the rest keep working.
  • Access control. The bigger the service, the harder it is to lock down sensitive parts. Pulling medical history into its own service makes it easier to restrict who can reach it.
  • Code volatility (also called volatility-based decomposition). If one part changes every week and the rest rarely changes, every release forces you to retest everything. Separating the fast-moving part keeps testing focused.
  • Scalability and throughput. Different parts may need different capacity. Heart rate sensors send a reading every second, while temperature may send one every five minutes. Separate services let each one scale to its own pace.

Heart rate versus temperature

Say heart rate arrives once a second and temperature arrives once every five minutes. If both live in one service, that service must handle the busiest traffic for both. Split them, and the heart rate service can be sized for its firehose while the temperature service stays small and cheap.

The book's verdict on basic vital signs

Heart rate and blood pressure are the critical signs, and they must keep working if temperature monitoring breaks. Heart rate readings come in every second, while the others come every five minutes. Each sign's data is stored separately. The book picks three separate services: better fault tolerance, data that already fits separate bounded contexts, and independent scaling for the different input rates.

Smaller services also start up faster. When a small service restarts, the functionality comes back sooner for the people who need it.

Forces that push you bigger (integrators)

  • Database transactions. A transaction is a group of database changes that either all succeed or all get undone. When one request updates several microservices, you cannot wrap all those updates in one transaction. Each service commits or rolls back on its own. If keeping data perfectly consistent matters more than anything else, merge the services so the work happens in one transaction.
  • Data dependencies. Splitting a service means splitting its data too. If the data is tangled together, such as a table that points to another table with a key, or a customer's details spread across many tables, separating it is painful. Tangled data is one of the most common reasons to keep services together.
  • Workflow and choreography. If a single business request needs several services talking back and forth, that is coupling. Coupling hurts performance (every hop adds network, security, and database delay), scalability (every service in the chain must grow together), and fault tolerance (one dead link breaks the whole request). If those qualities matter a lot, merge the chatty services.

Here is a memory aid from the chapter: start coarse-grained, then move to fine-grained once you know the system well. It is far easier to split a service you understand than to untangle a pile of tiny ones you don't.

Finding the sweet spot is always a balancing act. You weigh each force against the others and decide which trade-offs matter most. When the trade-offs are big, bring in your product owner or business stakeholder. They know which risks the business can live with.

Sharing common code

Some code shows up everywhere. Logging, metrics streaming, user authorization, and date formatting are common examples. For MonitorMe, the book also lists observability, logging, auditing (who got which alert, and when), and security (only authorized staff may reach a monitoring service). In a monolith (one big deployable application), you write that code once and everything uses it. In microservices, each service deploys on its own, so sharing takes more thought.

Imagine MonitorMe's three vital-sign services all need the same "alert a medical professional" logic. Copying the code into each service is called replication. It works only for code that never changes. The moment someone fixes a bug in one copy, the others go stale. So you pick one of two shared approaches.

Option A: a shared service

A shared service is its own microservice that other services call remotely. The alert logic lives in one place, and each monitoring service calls it when something goes wrong.

Option B: a shared library

A shared library is a packaged bundle of code (a JAR file in Java or a DLL in C#, for example). The book calls this the more common approach. It gets bundled into each microservice at compile time. Once deployed, every service carries its own copy of the shared code and calls it directly, with no network hop.

A shared library is like a first-aid kit in every room. Each room is self-sufficient and fast, but when the kit's contents change, you have to restock every single room.

For MonitorMe's alerting, you weigh the problem domain too. The book picks the shared library: the nurse hears faster, alerting still works if a shared alert service would have gone down, and several problems at once are handled better. Whichever you pick, justify it.

When one request needs many services: workflows

A workflow happens when one business request needs more than one microservice to finish. The request usually starts at a user interface. Think of a nurse named Juan asking, "How is the patient doing today?" The system must pull temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure from three different services. Juan wants one answer, not three requests, so something has to coordinate the three services. You have two ways to do it: orchestration (centralized) and choreography (decentralized).

Why split services apart if it means workflows? Because disintegrators like scalability, fault tolerance, code volatility, and access control can be worth the extra complexity of tying the services together. Other MonitorMe workflows the book lists: finding which vital signs are monitored for a patient, and registering a new patient (name, identity, demographics, medical history, each possibly its own service).

Orchestration: a conductor leads the band

In orchestration, one central microservice, the orchestrator, calls every service in the right order, handles errors, and combines the results into one answer for the caller. It is like a symphony conductor who tells each musician when to play.

The orchestrator also tracks the workflow's current state, so it always knows what happens next. That is a lot to manage, which is why the chapter jokes that the job posting probably pays well.

  • The good: the workflow lives in one place, so it's easy to understand and easy to change. The orchestrator knows where each request is, so restarting a failed request from the right step is simple. Error handling is in one spot, so individual services don't need to know how to recover.
  • The bad: it is slower, since every step talks to the orchestrator and the orchestrator often saves state to a database. It can become a bottleneck because every request passes through it. It is tightly coupled to the services it calls. And it is a single point of failure: if the conductor is gone, the concert stops. The usual fix is to run several copies of the orchestrator, like an understudy waiting in the wings.

Choreography: dancers who know their steps

In choreography, there is no conductor. Each service does its part, then calls the next service in the chain, until the workflow is done. The services communicate with each other like dancers who have learned their parts.

Blood pressure as a dance

Suppose the blood pressure monitoring is split into four services. The first captures a reading and passes it along. The second records it and passes it along. The third analyzes it and, if needed, hands the result to the fourth, which sends the alert. No one is in charge; each service just knows who comes next.

Do not quietly turn one dancer into the conductor. When one service starts directing all the others, you have slipped into the Front Controller pattern (one service acting as the central boss). That pattern suits orchestration well, but it defeats the purpose of choreography. The same trap appears when you give one service the job of tracking workflow state.

  • The good: there is no central bottleneck, so responsiveness and performance tend to be better. Services are loosely coupled, since none depends on a central boss. Each service scales to its own demand, separate from the others in the chain. Fast, scalable systems often use choreography.
  • The bad: error handling is spread out, and each service must manage its own failures, which can create a lot of extra messaging. Recovery is hard: when a user retries a request that failed halfway, nobody knows where to restart, because no single service directs traffic. Tracking state is hard too. Architects often pick one service, usually the first in the chain, to be the state owner, and have the others report their progress to it.

Quick way to choose: if you need clear control, easy recovery, and simple change management, lean toward orchestration. If you need raw speed and independent scaling and can live with harder recovery, lean toward choreography.

The trouble ticket workflow

A medical facility files a trouble ticket, and a field technician gets it on a phone, fixes the problem, and marks it fixed. The book picks choreography: it is a simple workflow with few error conditions, so orchestration would be overkill and choreography needs less service chatter.

Microservices superpowers

Every style has strengths. Microservices shines in these areas:

  • Maintainability. Each service is small and focused, so you can find the code you need to change quickly.
  • Testability. A service's scope is small, so you can test it thoroughly without testing the whole universe.
  • Deployability. Each service ships on its own. Releases are less risky than with big monoliths, and you can deploy more often, sometimes daily.
  • Evolvability. Adding a feature can mean creating a new service, testing it, and deploying it beside the others.
  • Scalability. You scale only the functions under load, not the whole system, which saves money.
  • Fault tolerance. When one service fails, only its function is down. Users can keep using everything else.

Microservices kryptonite

Just as kryptonite weakens Superman, some traits weaken the case for microservices. Watch for these:

  • Complexity. Microservices is one of the most complicated styles. You have to make hard calls about granularity, transactions, workflows, contracts, shared code, communication protocols, team layout, and deployment. That is a long list.
  • Performance. Every hop costs time. There is network latency (time to reach the service), security latency (time to re-check who the user is), and data latency (each service making its own database calls). Chatty services slow down fast.
  • Complex workflows. If your functions are tightly coupled, splitting them into separate services just creates a "big ball of distributed mud."
  • Monolithic databases. Microservices needs each service to own its data. If your data cannot be split apart, avoid this style.
  • Technically partitioned teams. If your organization has separate user interface, backend, and database teams, microservices will struggle. This is an example of Conway's Law, the idea that a system tends to mirror the communication structure of the organization that builds it. Microservices works best with cross-functional teams, where each team owns its services from screen to database.

The book's verdicts: an online auction system is well suited (high scalability, elasticity, concurrency, independent functions). A trouble ticket system for field technicians is well suited too. Quick recap

  • A microservice is a single-purpose, separately deployed unit of software that does one thing really well.
  • Each microservice owns its data. That fence, called a physical bounded context, lets it change without breaking others.
  • Granularity (scope, not physical size) is set by balancing disintegrators, which push services smaller, against integrators, which push them bigger.
  • Shared code lives in either a shared service (called remotely, easier to change, slower) or a shared library (bundled in, faster, harder to version).
  • Orchestration uses a central conductor for clear control. Choreography uses peer services for speed and scale. Avoid letting one service become a hidden conductor.

Event-Driven Architecture: Asynchronous Adventures

What if your system could do many things at once and never make a customer wait on a slow step? Event-driven architecture, or EDA, is a popular style built around things that happen and services that react to them. It is fast, scalable, and easy to extend, but it brings its own complexity. Let's see how it works, step by step.

Why speed comes from doing things at once

Picture a diner where one cook makes the grilled cheese, then the fries, then the milkshake, in strict order. Every order waits for every step, and the diner can serve only so many customers an hour. Now add three workers, one for each dish, all cooking at the same time. The order finishes in about half the time, and the diner can serve roughly twice as many people during the lunch rush.

That is the core idea of EDA in one picture. You split the work into separate services, and each service does its job as soon as something relevant happens. The services pass information through an event channel, and they don't wait for answers from each other. Faster completion means happier customers, which the chapter calls responsiveness. More orders per hour means the system can handle more load, which it calls scalability.

Think of a kitchen during a busy dinner service. The grill cook does not wait for the fry cook to finish before starting. Each station reacts to the ticket that lands on the rail. EDA works the same way: the services hear about the order and go to work at once.

Meet Der Nile

Der Nile is a large German online retailer selling everything from diapers to hair-growth supplements. Business is booming, and the ordering system is buckling. Orders take too long, and delivery times keep creeping up. Der Nile wants a new ordering system built for growth, and you are the architect. The legacy system's workflow runs step by step, which is exactly the slow diner pattern. Your first job is to ask: what can happen at the same time?

What is an event?

An event is something that happens, especially something important. A concert, a wedding, or a big promotion at work are all events in real life. In software, a user action often creates an event: placing a bid in an auction, filing an insurance claim, or making a purchase.

In EDA, an event is how one service tells the rest of the system that something just happened. Events are the main way information moves between services. An event usually carries data, such as every detail of an online order. This data is called its payload. Sometimes an event carries only a key, such as an order ID. Then any service that needs more detail must go to a data store to look it up.

The order that was placed

When a customer clicks "buy," the ordering service could publish an event that says an order was placed. The payload might include the order ID, the items, the price, and the shipping address. A payment service, an inventory service, and an email service can each react to that one event. None of them needs to know about the others.

Events are not messages

Events and messages both carry information, but they play different roles. An event broadcasts an action a service just performed. A service that sends an event tells the world, "A customer just placed an order," and does not care who listens. A message is a command or a request, like "Apply the payment for this order" or "Give me shipping options for this customer." Messages are aimed at one receiver.

Two rules make the difference clear:

  1. Events are broadcast through topics (a public channel anyone can subscribe to). Messages go to one service through a queue (a private line for a single receiver).
  2. Events always describe something that has already happened. Messages ask for something that still needs to be done.

A quick test: if the sentence is in the past tense, it's probably an event ("Order Placed"). If it's an instruction in the imperative ("Ship this order"), it's probably a message.

Initiating and derived events

An initiating event starts with a customer or end user and kicks off a business process. Clicking "buy" is a typical example.

Once a service handles an initiating event, it often broadcasts what it did. These follow-up events are called derived events, because they grow out of the first one. A single service can produce several derived events from one input. Anything a service causes or generates can become a derived event.

One payment, many outcomes

In the book's exercise, a customer's credit card purchase creates a "Credit Card Charged" initiating event. A Fraud Detection service and a Credit Limit service both react to it. Each can publish several derived events, one per outcome, such as "fraud suspected" or "limit exceeded" (these names are just our illustration). The book also notes that in Der Nile's flow, the Payment service publishes two different derived events, which is typical. Each derived event lets other services react without anyone waiting in line.

Why publish events nobody reads?

Here is a puzzle. If no one listens to an event, why publish it? Because you never know who will care next month. Publishing every meaningful action gives you extensibility, the ability to add new features without rewiring the system. Extensibility is one of the architectural characteristics you met in Chapter 2.

Adding analytics for free

Der Nile's Email Notification service sends a customer an email when an order ships or is delivered. After each email, it publishes a "Customer Notified" derived event. Months later, the business wants to know which hours of the day produce the most notification emails. You build a new Notification Analytics service that subscribes to that existing event. You don't touch the email service, the order service, or anything else.

Every meaningful action deserves a derived event. The cost is small, and the payoff is a system that can grow new features by listening instead of by surgery.

Asynchronous versus synchronous communication

EDA is fast mainly because it uses asynchronous communication, often shortened to async. Let's compare it with the more familiar style.

Synchronous (sync) communication means the sender waits for a reply before doing anything else, even just to confirm the message arrived. That is how a typical REST or HTTP API call works. Calling a friend is a good comparison: you dial, wait for the ring, and wait for an answer before you can talk.

Asynchronous communication means the sender doesn't wait. It sends the information and moves on. That is like texting a friend: you send the message, then get on with your day, and the reply comes whenever it comes.

Async is also called fire-and-forget. The event is sent (the fire part), and the sender moves on (the forget part). Sender and receiver are less tangled together, which makes the whole system fast and loosely coupled. Architects draw async links as dotted lines and sync links as solid lines. EDA relies on async when it sends and receives events.

A checkout in milliseconds

Suppose Order Placement takes 600 milliseconds to validate and save an order, and Payment takes 1,200 milliseconds to charge the card. With async, the customer waits about 600 milliseconds, since the rest happens in the background. With sync, the customer waits about 1,800 milliseconds, nearly two full seconds. The gap grows as more steps are added to the chain.

Async also helps availability. If the Payment service is down, the async Order Placement service doesn't care. The customer still gets "order placed." With sync, Order Placement can't work properly while Payment is down, and the customer sees an error and has to try again.

The trade-off: error handling gets harder

Remember the First Law of software architecture: everything is a trade-off. Async's big downside is error handling. With sync, a bad card gets reported straight back to the customer, who can fix it and resubmit. With async, the customer thinks everything is fine, because the system hasn't told them otherwise. Yet the order can't be processed until they fix the payment problem. In the book's example, the Payment service has to raise the error, and an Email Notification service has to tell the customer to update their card. That is a lot more moving parts, which makes error handling much more complex.

Where does the data live? Database topologies

Data is a tricky topic in EDA. If every service shares one database, the services are coupled to that database. If every service owns its own data, as in microservices, the services often need to ask each other for data synchronously, which couples them in a different way. Either way, data becomes a coupling point, which EDA tries hard to avoid. The chapter describes three database topologies, meaning three ways to arrange data.

Monolithic database

All services share one database. The big advantage: when a service needs data it doesn't own, it simply queries the database. For example, Order Placement can read current inventory and shipping options directly, with no extra call.

The price is steep. Change one table's structure and it's hard to know which services will break. Testing and releasing become a risky guessing game, and the system grows brittle. The shared database is also a single point of failure, and it may not scale as the system grows.

Domain-partitioned databases

Here, each business domain (a group of related functions) gets its own database, shared by all services in that domain. The Order Placement, Payment, and Inventory services all belong to the Order Placement domain, so they share one database. Services in other domains can't touch that database directly. They must make a synchronous call instead, which couples them.

The book calls this a well-balanced, middle-of-the-road solution within EDA.

Database-per-service

Every service gets its own database. This forms an even tighter physical bounded context than the domain approach. Changing the database is easy, because only the owning service is affected. Fault tolerance and scalability improve too.

The catch is cost, in money and in speed. Splitting data into tiny bounded contexts is also hard, because tables are tied together by things like foreign keys, triggers, views, and stored procedures. When a service needs data it doesn't own, it must make a synchronous call to the owner. That creates a lot of coupling and chatter, which slows the system down.

Picking a topology

Suppose the system must be as fast as possible, and you expect anywhere from 20 to 300,000 users at once. The monolithic database can get in the way of scale. Domain-partitioned databases are a middle ground. Database-per-service scales and survives failures best, but its synchronous data calls make it the slowest, and it costs the most. For a medical monitoring system that must never fully fail, you would weigh fault tolerance heavily. The "right" answer depends on which trade-offs your business can live with. (The book leaves this as an exercise, so treat these as our reading of the scorecards.)

EDA versus microservices: six differences

EDA and microservices look alike at first glance. Both are distributed, and both are good for scalability, agility, elasticity, and fault tolerance. Here are six differences, counted down from least to most fundamental.

  • 6. Performance. EDA combines async processing with doing many things at once, so it is very fast. Microservices, with bounded contexts and fine-grained services, often need synchronous calls and pay a lot of latency. The book's star ratings give EDA five stars for performance and microservices two.
  • 5. Physical bounded contexts. Microservices can't work without them. EDA can use them, but doesn't require them, because data sharing is common and ownership is less strict.
  • 4. Data granularity. Microservices requires each service to own its own fine-grained data. EDA lets you choose a monolithic database, domain-partitioned databases, or database-per-service.
  • 3. Service granularity. Microservices are single-purpose and fine-grained. EDA services (called event processors) can be any size.
  • 2. Event versus request processing. EDA is built on reacting to things that already happened, and it triggers more events in turn. Microservices is built on handling requests and commands about things that still need to happen.
  • 1. Communication style. This is the biggest difference. EDA mostly uses async communication. Microservices typically relies on synchronous calls, often REST. EDA can occasionally call synchronously, for example to fetch data it can't reach, and microservices can go async when a command needs no reply. Those are exceptions, not the rule.

Mixing the two: event-driven microservices

Nothing stops you from combining the styles. The result is a hybrid called event-driven microservices. Just using database-per-service doesn't make a system event-driven microservices, though. You need two principles from microservices in the design: single-purpose services and physical bounded contexts.

Fixing a half-converted design

Imagine an EDA where Order Submission accepts an order, validates it, charges the payment, and adjusts inventory. That is fine for EDA, but not for microservices, because the service is doing four jobs. Split it into single-purpose services, each publishing its own events. Also, if the Email service reads the Order Submission database directly, that breaks the fence. Instead, Email should call the owning Order Placement service (the renamed Order Submission service, now that it has been split up) for order details. Now each service stays in its own bounded context.

EDA superpowers

  • Maintainability. Services are highly decoupled, so each one is fairly independent and easier to maintain.
  • Performance. Mostly async, with work happening in parallel, makes it very fast.
  • Scalability. Each service scales on its own, and the event channel acts like a pressure release valve when a bottleneck builds up.
  • Evolvability. Every action can publish a derived event, giving you easy hooks for new features.
  • Fault tolerance. If one service goes down, the others in the workflow keep running.

EDA kryptonite

  • Complexity. Asynchronous work, parallel processing, and several database topologies, each with trade-offs, make EDA a complicated style to design and run.
  • Testability. Asynchronous and parallel behavior is hard to test. It is also hard to prove that a change in one service or event hasn't broken something elsewhere.
  • Synchronous calls. If your system relies on lots of synchronous calls, or on workflows where services must wait on each other, EDA is a poor fit.
  • Databases. Whatever topology you pick, services end up coupled, either to the database or to each other. There are no perfect options here.

In short, it's the sports car of architectures (our analogy): quick and fun to extend, but harder to keep running smoothly and to test thoroughly.

Putting it together

The book's finished Der Nile system shows only the happy-path events, to keep the picture readable. A customer's order starts at Order Placement, which publishes "Order Placed." Payment, Inventory, and Email react. Payment publishes "Payment Applied," and Fulfillment publishes "Order Fulfilled." Shipping publishes "Order Shipped," and Email publishes "Customer Notified." Inventory also publishes "Inventory Adjusted," which a Warehouse service uses to publish "Stock Replenished." Real life would add error events too.

Quick recap

  • An event is something that just happened. Services broadcast events to share information, and events carry a payload of data.
  • Events go out through topics to anyone listening. Messages are commands or requests sent to one service through a queue.
  • Initiating events start a business process. Derived events are the follow-ups a service publishes in response. Publish them freely to keep the system extensible.
  • Async, fire-and-forget communication makes EDA fast and loosely coupled, at the cost of harder error handling.
  • Choose one of three database topologies: monolithic (simple but brittle), domain-partitioned (a balanced middle), or database-per-service (most independent but most costly).
  • EDA and microservices differ mainly in communication style and in what they process, events versus requests. Combine them for event-driven microservices when you need both.
  • EDA is fast, scalable, and evolvable, but complex and hard to test.

Six Bonus Lessons for the Road Ahead

The book is over, but your architect journey is just starting. This appendix covers six topics the chapters had no room for: staying hands-on with code, what the job expects, the people skills that make designs stick, drawing readable diagrams, balancing deep and wide knowledge, and practicing with katas.

1. Keep your hands on the code

Good architects still write source code. It keeps your skills sharp and shows you how your decisions behave with real users. The catch is that architecture work can eat every hour you have, so code in the right places.

Stay off the critical path

The critical path is the chain of work that decides when the product ships. Don't become the only person who owns it. Leave the framework code and the most crucial parts to your team, so if architecture work pulls you away, nobody stalls.

Write proof-of-concept code

A proof of concept (PoC) is a small program that tests whether an idea works. Stuck between two options? Build a tiny version of each and see which one hurts less.

Unless you are sure the code will be thrown away, write it as if it will ship. Prototypes have a funny habit of ending up in production.

Other ways to stay useful

  • Pay down technical debt. That is the pile of needed fixes a team has pushed off for later. Chip away at it. Your team will appreciate it, and if you get called away, it won't hold anyone up.
  • Help during production outages. An outage is when a live system breaks. Help find the root cause, the real underlying problem rather than the symptom, and make the code changes that get things running again. You also get to see how your architecture is really implemented.
  • Do lots of code reviews. Someone else reads your code before it merges. Nobody loves them, but they keep the code aligned with your decisions.

An architect who never codes is like a building inspector who has never held a hammer. They can talk about wiring all day, but they will miss the cable stapled the wrong way.

2. What architects are expected to do

Companies word the role differently, but these expectations show up almost everywhere.

  • Make decisions. Define decisions and design principles (the rules of thumb your system follows), and use them to guide technology choices.
  • Keep current. Trends move fast, and you need to see what is coming.
  • Analyze continuously. Ask whether the design is still viable as business and technology change. That is architectural vitality.
  • Enforce compliance. Check that teams follow approved decisions. This is architectural governance. A great design does nothing if nobody follows it.
  • Know a little about a lot. You don't need to be an expert in every platform, but you should know what exists.
  • Lead people. Leadership skills are at least half of the job. Developers and architects usually prefer technical problems to people problems, but the architect is expected to guide the team through the build. The book quotes Gerald Weinberg: it's always a people problem, however it looks at first.
  • Know the business domain. That is the area of the business your software serves. Without it, you can't understand the real problem.
  • Navigate office politics. Product owners may call your plan too expensive, and other developers may prefer their own approach. You have to make your case.

The contested database

A product owner wants the cheapest database. You know it will buckle during the holiday rush. Instead of saying "no," bring a short test showing what breaks and what it would cost in lost sales. Now the talk is about money, not opinions.

3. The soft skills that make designs stick

Soft skills are people skills. They sound easy, but they take years of trial and error.

Demonstrate, don't debate

Every environment is different, so searching the internet won't settle an argument about your system. Run the options in something close to production and show the results.

Demonstration beats discussion. Show, then talk.

Pick your battles

Fight hard for what the architecture cannot survive without, and let the rest go. People who choose their fights wisely earn respect.

Speak business value

Stakeholders, the people with a stake in the outcome, rarely care about fault tolerance. They care about time to market, regulatory compliance, and mergers and acquisitions. Translate your concerns into their terms.

Translating a message queue

Instead of "we need a message queue for fault tolerance," try "if payments go down for an hour, we still accept orders, so no sales are lost." Same idea, and now the business is on your side.

Bring developers into the decision

The book gives two leadership rules. Rule 1: if developers don't know why you decided something, they are less likely to agree. Rule 2: if they weren't involved, they are less likely to follow it. Always justify your decisions, and make sure the whole team understands the reasoning. Ask for their opinions, invite them to a risk-storming session (a group exercise for finding risks in a design), or use the Request for Comment status in an ADR (Architecture Decision Record, a short document that records a decision and why).

Divide and conquer

When someone demands all-or-nothing, split the problem. Does every part of the system really need a 400-millisecond response time? Or 99.999% availability? Some parts will, and some won't. The book borrows from Sun Tzu here: if the enemy is united, separate them. Once you see which piece is hard, negotiating gets easier.

Keep it clear and concise

The book's "four Cs": be Clear, be Concise, Communicate, and Collaborate. You need all four to earn trust.

Be available

Protect a block of time early morning or late afternoon for your team's questions, so you aren't stuck in meetings all day.

4. Diagrams people actually read

Chapter 3 introduced ADRs as the best way to record the analysis behind a decision. A diagram is the other common tool. It shows what the thing looks like: its structure, topology (how the parts are arranged), communication, dependencies, and integration points. Diagramming fills whole books, so these are quick tips.

  • Keep it simple. Too many lines create the hairball effect, a tangled mess nobody can read.
  • Always include a title. Most diagrams show one view or perspective, and the title should say which.
  • Use one-way arrows. A double-headed arrow leaves readers guessing whether it means two-way talk or is just the default.
  • Use real labels, not acronyms. Only insiders know the acronyms. Spelling things out also saves extra documentation.
  • Solid lines for synchronous, dotted for asynchronous. Synchronous (blocking) means the caller waits for an answer. Asynchronous (nonblocking) means it moves on.
  • Use consistent shapes and colors. Random shapes add noise.
  • Always include a key explaining each shape and line.
KEY
  ====>  synchronous call (the caller waits)
  - - ->  asynchronous call (the caller moves on)

[Checkout Web App] ====> [Order Service]
[Order Service]    ====> [Payment Service]
[Order Service]    - - -> [Email Service]
[Order Service]    ====> [Orders Database]

The database cylinder is about the only shape everyone agrees on. For everything else, add a key.

5. Depth versus breadth

Technical depth is every topic where you have real expertise. Staying expert in anything takes time, because technology keeps changing. Technical breadth covers your expertise plus what you know about other solutions existing, and some of their trade-offs. As an architect, knowing five ways to solve a problem beats knowing one way deeply.

Picture a pyramid. Early in your career, the pyramid shows everything you know of the world's information. As you move from junior developer to tech lead, the "stuff you know" grows: languages, frameworks, tools, and platforms. You can also help and mentor other developers. When you become the go-to expert, a middle layer forms: the "stuff you know you don't know." You know where your knowledge ends. Getting there takes a lot of effort, since you must also keep your hands-on skills sharp. New architects start with a tech lead's pyramid, so make an effort to broaden it on purpose.

Broadening on purpose

Say you are a .NET expert. Volunteer for a Java project, a JavaScript-heavy user interface, or a hard data problem. Each one adds breadth, and you start to notice patterns that repeat across tools.

Your expertise will fade a little over time. That hurts, but it trades depth for breadth, and breadth is what lets you see trade-offs.

6. Practice architecture with katas

Bruce Lee's point was that drilling one kick ten thousand times beats throwing ten thousand kicks once. A kata is a practice form from martial arts. An architectural kata simulates designing a real system, so you can drill the process.

How a kata runs

  1. Form teams. Use groups of three to five people. Odd numbers help, because a majority can settle disputes.
  2. Get a problem. A moderator keeps time and facilitates. Each team is assigned a project and asks the moderator, playing the "customer," clarifying questions. Each team works on a different kata, though the authors sometimes give the same kata to two teams to compare results.
  3. Design. Follow the book's process: analyze architectural characteristics, determine logical components, choose an architectural style, and document decisions. Any technology is fair game, but honor real limits like budget and hiring. The focus is architecture and trade-off analysis. The team also sketches a rough vision of its solution.
  4. Present and get challenged. Teams defend their design against hard but fair questions. While listening to another team, your job is to ask questions. Picking an overall winner is optional.

A parking app kata

The moderator says, "Design a parking app for three city garages." Your team asks how many cars arrive at peak hour, sketches two options, and presents both. The other teams push on the weakest part. You leave with a sharper sense of trade-offs.

Rules that make katas work

  • Mix the teams. Colleagues who work together daily should generally be split up. The goal is learning to work with architects you don't know. Treat the rules as adaptable, and let the moderator decide anything they don't cover.
  • Keep supplies low-tech. Poster paper or a whiteboard is enough.
  • Size the time. A kata can take as little as 45 minutes or as long as several weeks. The authors have run katas for real companies that gave teams eight weeks.
  • Give balanced feedback. Ask constructive questions, and don't focus only on strengths or only on flaws.

Where to go next

You made it to the end. The book's website, headfirstsoftwarearchitecture.com, has what comes next, and the book also suggests some further reading.

Quick recap

  • Stay hands-on with code, but keep off the critical path.
  • Architects make decisions, watch for architectural vitality, enforce governance, and navigate office politics.
  • Soft skills decide whether a design survives: demonstrate instead of debate, pick your battles, and speak business value.
  • Diagrams need a title, a key, one-way arrows, real labels, and solid versus dotted lines for sync versus async.
  • Build breadth as well as depth, so you know the trade-offs between options.
  • Practice with katas in small teams, where you present, get challenged, and give balanced feedback.

That's the whole book, from the first trade-off to the last microservice. If you only remember one thing: architecture is the art of choosing which trade-offs you can live with, and writing down why.
