Before You Start

The big idea: this is a short, pocket-sized guide to succeeding with testing and building a quality culture on agile teams, drawn from two much larger earlier books.

Why this book?

The authors, Janet Gregory and Lisa Crispin, wanted a small, concise, easy-to-read book. Anyone should be able to pick it up and get a basic understanding of how to succeed with testing and build a quality culture in an agile setting. (Agile means building software in small steps, with frequent delivery and constant feedback.)

Their first two books are much larger. They go into more depth and include real-life stories from practitioners. This book refers to them by short names:

  • Agile Testing is Agile Testing: A Practical Guide for Testers and Agile Teams, published in 2009.
  • More Agile Testing is More Agile Testing: Learning Journeys for the Whole Team, published in 2014.

You will see these two names again and again in the chapters that follow. When the text says "see More Agile Testing", it is pointing you to the longer book for details.

Who this book is for

This is not an introductory testing book. Many good resources already teach the basics of testing, test automation, DevOps (teams that build and run software together) and similar topics, and the book's bibliography links to some. It is also not a basic introduction to agile development.

It is for readers on teams that are adopting agile. It is also for people who want to know how testing and quality fit into agile development and are looking for guidance, such as managers or executives.

Example (added here)

A team lead at a small online shop has just moved her group from long release cycles to two-week sprints. She does not need a lesson on what a test case is. She needs to know who should test what, and when. This book is aimed at that kind of question.

How to read this book

  • Start in any section, or read single chapters based on what you want to learn. Each chapter stands on its own, although it may point to other chapters.
  • Use it as a pocket guide and keep it handy while you work. When you are stuck and need inspiration, or your team is wondering about a testing challenge, look through it for ideas.
  • When you want more depth, go to the authors' other two books.
  • Throughout the book you will find hints, which give ideas on how to approach a specific problem.

Section 1

Foundations

This first section gives the authors' definition of agile testing. The heart of it is that the whole team takes part in testing and in building quality into the product. That needs a big mindset shift: the team learns to prevent defects instead of relying on testers as a safety net to catch them. New agile teams also learn to slice big features into small, incremental chunks and deliver small changes often, while keeping the big picture in mind so customers stay happy. The section has three chapters: what we mean by agile testing, the whole-team approach and mindset, and test planning in agile contexts.

Section 1 · Chapter 1

What Do We Mean by Agile Testing?

The big idea: agile testing is collaborative, continuous testing from the first idea to delivery and beyond, aimed at building quality in and supported by the whole team.

People have asked the authors many times to define "agile testing". They give their definition at the end of this chapter, but many factors feed into it. Teams that succeed with agile testing tend to share a handful of practices:

  • Building quality in. The team works to prevent misunderstandings about how a feature should behave, as well as defects in the code.
  • Guiding development with concrete examples. The team uses practices such as acceptance test-driven development (ATDD, agreeing on acceptance tests before coding), behavior-driven development (BDD, describing behavior in shared, readable examples) or specification by example (SBE, using examples as the specification).
  • Including testing activities. These are having conversations to build shared understanding, asking questions to test ideas and assumptions, automating tests, performing exploratory testing (learning the product by testing it without a fixed script), testing quality attributes such as performance, reliability and security, and learning from production usage.
  • Using whole-team retrospectives and small experiments. The team keeps improving testing and quality, and finds what works in its own context.

The rest of the book expands on each of these. The authors do not call them "best practices", because the practices keep evolving.

Continuous testing models

Testing is an integral part of software development, next to coding, operations, understanding customer needs and more. The authors like models that show a continuous, or holistic, approach to testing. One favorite comes from Ellen Gottesdiener and Mary Gorman, in their 2012 book Discover to Deliver. It is shown in Figure 1.1.

In that model, development is an infinite loop that confirms things continuously, which is how software really gets made. You learn about a feature your customers want. You build and deliver it. Then you learn how customers actually use it, and you use that feedback to decide what to build next, or what to remove.

Dan Ashby took a similar approach in his blog post on testing in DevOps (a way of working where development and operations share responsibility for building and running the software). His diagram, Figure 1.2, shows that testing is an integral part of DevOps. Chapter 8 goes into more detail.

The authors like terms such as continuous testing and holistic testing, and they accept that each team has to adapt them to its own context.

Example (added here)

A team building a ticket-booking site tests during planning by asking "what happens if two people book the last seat?". They test in the code with unit tests, in the build with automated checks, after release by watching error rates and booking failures, and they feed what they see back into the next plan. No single stage is "the testing phase".

Ten principles for agile testing

In Agile Testing the authors introduced ten principles for agile testers. They now see that the principles are not only for testers but for anyone on the team. They wrote them when most testers still sat in a separate testing team on a phased and gated project (work moves through fixed phases, each with a gate to pass), with testing at the end, after the coding was "finished". The principles still apply to anyone on an agile team who wants to deliver the highest-quality product possible:

  1. Provide continuous feedback.
  2. Deliver value to the customer.
  3. Enable face-to-face communication.
  4. Have courage.
  5. Keep it simple.
  6. Practice continuous improvement.
  7. Respond to change.
  8. Self-organize.
  9. Focus on people.
  10. Enjoy!

Testing manifesto

The chapter ends with a "testing manifesto" by Karen Greaves and Samantha Laing, shown in Figure 1.3. It reflects the mindset shift that agile testing needs: we test throughout development, we focus on preventing bugs, we test much more than functionality, and the whole team takes responsibility for quality. These ideas run through all the authors' books. When your team is stuck on a quality or testing problem, the authors suggest reflecting on them to find a way forward.

Agile testing definition

The simplest definition the authors have found is this:

Key idea

Agile testing is collaborative testing practices that occur continuously, from inception to delivery and beyond, supporting frequent delivery of value to customers. Testing activities focus on building quality into the product, using fast feedback loops to validate our understanding. The practices strengthen the idea of whole-team responsibility for quality.

It takes a while to digest. The authors have a blog post that gives more detail on this definition.

Read the definition against a login page story. "Collaborative": a product owner, programmer and tester agree on what a valid login is before anyone codes. "Continuously": unit tests run on each commit, and failed logins are watched in production. "Fast feedback loops": the team learns within minutes if a change breaks sign-in, not weeks later.

Section 1 · Chapter 2

Whole-Team Approach and Agile Testing Mindset

The big idea: quality is the job of the whole delivery team, so the team prevents defects together instead of leaving them for a test team at the end.

Many software teams still use a phased-and-gated, linear approach. People in one role sit in a silo (a separate team that hands work to the next one). The test or QA team is seen as the group responsible for quality, usually at the very end, right before production, when it is too late to do much to improve quality.

Agile development breaks down the silos and turns development into a continual, iterative process. The whole delivery team works together to build quality in throughout. By "whole team" the authors usually mean the delivery team: the people responsible for understanding what to build, building it, and delivering the final product to the customer.

In larger organizations, even ones that have adopted agile, more than one team may work on a product. Examples are an independent database team or a user experience team. In that case the whole team means whoever you need to deliver the product. The DevOps movement has made the inclusion of operations in delivery more visible. Janet calls the people outside the delivery team an extended family.

Focus on quality

The whole-team approach means every team member is responsible for the quality of the product. Part of that is making sure testing tasks are finished alongside the other development tasks. When the aim is the highest quality possible, rather than speed, the team builds a solid foundation of practices. To get there, teams manage their workload so they have time to learn core practices such as test-driven development (TDD, writing a small test before the code that passes it) and exploratory testing. They also take time to learn the business domain and build relationships with business experts. That helps them find the features with the most business value and implement them as simply as possible. Over time, by focusing on quality, teams do begin to work faster.

Several areas call for a change in how team members approach development. When the whole team owns quality of the product and of the process, each member has to be proactive in solving problems. For example, everyone can help work out what is most valuable to customers. The team delivers just enough of that value in small increments, to learn how customers use it. These quick feedback loops let the team focus testing on the features that matter most to the customer.

Definition of Done

Each team needs to discuss and agree on a "valuable Definition of Done" (DoD, the team's shared checklist for when work counts as finished). It should include how the team plans to deal with defects found in the code. The DoD must include testing, and the question to ask is "What types of testing do you mean?" Chapter 9 covers the agile testing quadrants (a way to sort kinds of tests) and answers that question. Every team member must understand the DoD in the same way.

Example (added here)

A team's DoD for a story might say: code reviewed, unit tests passing, acceptance examples automated, exploratory session done, no known defects open. One member who reads "tested" as "I ran it once on my laptop" breaks the shared meaning, which is why the team must talk the list through.

Key idea

Every team member shares responsibility for quality, and the team agrees together what "done" means, including which types of testing are done.

How teams deal with defects

A big mindset shift for testers is taking a team approach to fixing defects. More Agile Testing talks about focusing on preventing defects rather than finding them after coding is done. When the whole team concentrates on building quality in, using practices like acceptance test-driven development and thinking about constraints around quality attributes, the number of defects found in code can fall a long way.

Many teams practice zero defect tolerance. This means zero known defects escape an iteration or story completion. For this to work, teams need fast feedback from testing, so defects are fixed right away. Once a defect is found, the programmers write one or more executable tests, fix the code so the tests pass, and do exploratory testing if needed. The team can then forget about it, knowing the issue is fixed. Quite often this change in approach turns an adversarial environment into a cooperative one.

Example (added here)

A tester finds that a discount code can be applied twice. Instead of filing a ticket for later, she tells the programmer, who writes a failing test for the double discount, fixes the code until it passes, and the pair spends ten minutes exploring nearby cases. The bug is closed before the story is called done.

Try it

Look at how your team handled the last defect found late in an iteration. Which steps of the "write a test, fix the code, explore if needed" routine were skipped, and what would have been needed to do them immediately?

Multiple perspectives

Team members have different viewpoints, skills and perspectives. Using all of them gives a better understanding of the risks of delivering a feature. For example, designing for testability helps turn examples of desired and undesired behavior into executable tests. Team members become generalized specialists: they may be expert in one or two areas but can contribute to the team's shared goals in many ways. Some examples:

  • Testers may be experts at testing the product, but they can also help the team understand features and stories by asking questions that uncover hidden assumptions.
  • Programmers can do exploratory testing on their own code before they check it in.
  • Product owners run acceptance tests on every story.

Chapter 11 says more about the tester's role and how it may change on agile teams.

Summary
  • Whole team. The delivery team, plus an extended family of specialists when several teams work on one product, shares responsibility for quality.
  • Focus on quality. Time for TDD, exploratory testing and learning the business domain pays off in speed later.
  • Definition of Done. Agreed by the team, covers defects and testing, and means the same to everyone.
  • Defects. Prevent them, and with zero defect tolerance fix any found at once, with an executable test.
  • Perspectives. Generalized specialists use their strengths to help each other.

Section 1 · Chapter 3

Test Planning in Agile Contexts

The big idea: plan testing for your own context, meaning your team, your product and the level of detail you are working at, and never lose the big picture while delivering small slices.

One of the top seven success factors in Agile Testing is "Don't Forget the Big Picture." Teams get caught up in building, testing and delivering small increments, which the authors encourage. But they can forget how a small slice fits into the whole system or helps solve the business problem.

To plan testing well, a team has to consider its context. Think about three aspects: the team, the product, and the levels of detail of your system.

The team

Not all teams are the same. A small, co-located team (everyone in the same place) has easy communication. It has a good chance of learning and sharing each other's values. This is a sweet spot for delivering a great product, and planning is much easier. The team can readily understand the next feature and dig into story and task level planning.

Many people, however, work in large, globally distributed organizations, which bring other challenges. Large organizations have multiple projects and many teams. When they adopt agile, they often replace silos based on role, such as developers and QA, with Scrum or feature team silos.

When many large teams work in the same code base, integration (making everyone's changes work together) can become a huge challenge. Teams may need specialists, such as performance, security and reliability testers, but there may not be enough of them for every cross-functional team. It is even hard to make these issues visible. Release-level planning is especially hard here, yet it is critical to delivering new capabilities to customers.

Whatever the context, the delivery team must take responsibility for planning and completing all testing activities, even if that means bringing in specialists. If the team has dependencies, it should work with other teams to manage or remove them, preferably before coding starts. Adjustments are needed to suit each context.

Example (added here)

Four teams share one banking app. Only two security testers exist. Each team is still responsible for its security testing, so the teams book the testers early for a shared review in the release plan instead of hoping a free tester appears on release day.

The product

The level of quality stakeholders want depends on the product, and so do the type and amount of testing. A content management system used only by internal staff has different priorities than medical device software, because each lives in a different environment and carries different risks.

Consider the size of the product, how many people use it, and whether it connects to external applications. Think about how the product is delivered and the risk in that delivery mechanism. If the organization hosts its own web application, it has much more control over when and how often the product is updated. If the product must work on many devices such as phones, how do updates happen without interrupting regular use?

One of the main purposes of testing is to identify and reduce risks, to the user and the business. That shapes how you plan testing, and it is why delivery teams need to learn the business domain and work closely with business experts. Domain expertise helps in planning what to test. Does your team have a good idea of how the product is used? Do all members have domain knowledge? Working with product and business experts helps the team find the best ways to build capabilities customers value.

There is a lot to consider around your product domain. It is not only the software you test, but the product your end users depend on.

Try it

List three facts about your product that change how much and what kind of testing it needs, such as who uses it, how updates reach them and what the worst failure would cost.

Planning across levels of detail

Testing across multiple levels (Figure 3.1) needs extra planning. Release cycles usually start by deciding what might be delivered in the first "learning release". Perhaps only part of a feature is delivered. Features are broken into stories and prioritized so the team knows what to deliver first. The team should understand the big picture before it brings stories into an iteration. Developers working on a story focus on finishing individual tasks. Testers can fall into the trap of thinking only about the story they are testing, so reminders of the big picture matter.

Release/feature level

Teams need to see how each delivered story may affect the big picture, especially in larger or global organizations. A release may hold many features, and each feature many stories and tasks, all of which affect the system as a whole.

When several teams work on the same product, a common problem is that teams become "siloed". They forget to talk to other teams to resolve possible dependencies.

Figure 3.2 shows why a test approach that includes all teams working toward one product release matters. To give a big picture of test coverage, consider bringing people from different teams together to build a testing mind map or a feature test matrix (details are in More Agile Testing) covering the product.

One size does not fit all, so make allowances for the size and number of your teams, where they are, how work is coordinated, and whether every team has the skills testing needs.

Ideally, activities are coordinated with other teams as features are built. But a more finished product may be needed for some things, such as final screenshots in user or training documentation. And because deploying a patch to a mobile application is not usually quick, the whole team may need to explore the newest version one more time.

Key idea

Hint from the book: do not have testers and perhaps operations staff wrap up the pre-deploy endgame while developers start new stories. Preparing for delivery is a whole-team effort, just like development.

Story level

At this level it does not matter whether the team uses time-boxed iterations or a flow-based method such as Kanban (a board where work flows continuously). Start with high-level acceptance tests (Chapter 4 covers this). Collect examples to build shared understanding of the story, and turn them into tests. If tests are written before coding, they guide development and prevent defects.

The book adds a hint here: consider what exploratory test charters (short missions that guide an exploratory session) might be needed (Chapter 6), and think about the product's constraints and what they mean for testing quality attributes (Chapter 7).

As the team plans testing and discusses implementation for each story, details about testing emerge. Create new examples and tests that reflect what you have learned about the story.

Example (added here)

For a "reset password" story, the team starts with examples: a valid email gets a link, an unknown email gets the same message, an expired link is refused. During the discussion someone asks about a link used twice, so a new example and test are added.

Task level

Programmers use test-driven development (TDD) to write low-level, or unit, tests before each small piece of code. Some call it Design-Driven Development, since it helps them design their code for testability. These tests are fairly easy to write, run fast and give the team quick feedback. They make up much of the base of the test automation pyramid model discussed in Chapter 10, Visualizing a Test Automation Strategy.

Planning for regression testing

Regression testing is making sure the system still does what it did yesterday. Modern practice delivers small changes to production often, leaving no time for full manual regression checking, so the team builds test automation while it builds the product. Automation should be part of each story, especially at the service layer (Chapter 10). If a story changes functionality, include tasks to change the existing tests.

Automated regression tests give confidence in the product with fast feedback. Many, if not all, run as part of continuous integration (CI, where the build and tests run on every change). Some teams run slower tests less often, for example several times a day instead of on every build. Release feature toggles (switches that hide a change from production users) let teams do some testing asynchronously while they keep deploying to production. The feature is turned on when all testing is complete (see Chapter 8, Testing in DevOps). More is in Chapter 23, Testing and DevOps, of More Agile Testing.

Example (added here)

A team adds a new "gift wrap" option to checkout. Behind a feature toggle, it is deployed on Tuesday and tested with real production data by staff only. The slow browser tests run at lunch and at night. On Thursday, when everything passes, the toggle is turned on for customers.

Summary
  • Context. Consider the team, the product and the levels of detail.
  • Team. The delivery team plans and completes all testing, bringing in specialists and managing dependencies early.
  • Product. Risk, domain knowledge and delivery method shape how much and what kind of testing is needed.
  • Release/feature level. Bring teams together with a testing mind map or feature test matrix, and treat release prep as a whole-team job.
  • Story level. Turn examples into tests before coding and keep adding as you learn.
  • Task level. TDD unit tests give fast feedback and form the base of the automation pyramid.
  • Regression. Automate with each story, run in CI and use feature toggles.

Section 2 · Chapter 4

Guiding Development with Examples

The big idea: concrete examples of wanted and unwanted behaviour give the whole team the same picture of a story before coding starts, and the best of those examples become automated tests that tell you when you are done.

Using examples to guide the building of features and stories is not new. Many teams have done it for years, and the authors see it as a tried-and-true, valuable approach. Good practitioners keep finding new ways to make it work, such as Matt Wynne's example mapping, which you will meet in Chapter 5.

A concrete example shows what the system should do, or should not do, in a specific situation. When the team looks at such examples together, they build a shared understanding of each feature and story. The payoff is building the right thing the first time. There are fewer story rejections (stories sent back because they were not what the customer wanted) and a shorter cycle time (the time from starting work to deploying to production).

Testers have a clear part to play. They ask for concrete examples, and they use them to create executable tests (tests a computer can run) that guide development. They can be the voice of experience when the team has these conversations.

Example-based methods

There are a few variations on building features and stories from examples. Here are the three the book names.

Behavior-driven development (BDD)

Janet hears more teams say they use BDD than any other variation. Daniel Terhorst-North first introduced it. BDD captures example scenarios in a natural, domain-specific language, meaning plain words from the business world rather than code. Its Given/When/Then syntax has three parts: the preconditions (the situation at the start), a trigger action, and the resulting post-condition (what should be true afterwards). While developers write the production code, they are likely to automate some or all of the scenarios. Passing scenarios tell them they have delivered what the customer wants.

Writing scenarios sounds easy, but it takes practice to simplify each test so that only one thing is being tested. The book points to Figure 4.1 at this spot, and that figure shows the closely related ATDD flow described next.

Example (added here)

A booking site has the story "As a guest, I can cancel a booking for a full refund up to 48 hours before check-in." One scenario might read: Given I have a booking that starts in 3 days, When I cancel it, Then I see a full refund confirmation. It tests one thing only. A second scenario covers a booking that starts in 1 day, and the Then part is a refund of zero. Keeping them separate means that when one fails, you know which rule broke.

Acceptance test-driven development (ATDD)

ATDD is similar but looser. It is a more generic way of guiding development with examples, without strict language or rules. Most people use it for functional tests (does the feature do what it should), although requirements for other quality attributes such as security or accessibility can be included.

One ATDD approach the authors have used: while the team plans the story, capture at least one high-level example of desired behaviour, the "happy path", and at least one example for each type of misbehaviour. As coding goes on, testers and other team members draw out more detailed examples. At least some of the examples become executable tests that help the team decide when they are done.

The figure starts by slicing the feature into stories. The highlighted bubbles are done as part of story readiness workshops, backlog refinement, or "three amigos" discussions (short talks between a tester, a programmer and a business expert, covered in Chapter 5).

Example (added here)

Take a "forgot password" story. At planning the team writes one happy-path example: a user enters a registered email and gets a reset link. They add one example per kind of misbehaviour: an unknown email, an expired link, a reused link. During coding, the tester adds details such as "the link works for 30 minutes". The happy path and the three misbehaviours become automated tests. When all four pass, and a short exploratory session finds nothing alarming, the team calls the story done.

Specification by Example (SBE)

Teams practising Specification by Example start by naming the goals around the story, using an approach like impact mapping (see Chapter 5). In a specification workshop the team then draws out key examples, and these become the specifications. As development goes on, the examples are refined and turned into executable specifications that check the product often. These executable examples, like those from BDD and ATDD, become living documentation of the application: documentation that cannot go stale, because it fails when the product stops matching it. When Gojko Adzic coined the term, he deliberately avoided the word "test" for any of the activities.

Why examples help

Looking at a new capability from several perspectives helps the team pinpoint the value customers get from it. That variety helps each team member get past unconscious biases and think outside the box. When each stakeholder is asked for concrete examples, the team can compare them, and discrepancies are easy to see. It is also easier to dig down to the minimum specific value customers need, which lets the team deliver "just enough" of the right thing.

Key idea

Abstract requirements let people nod along while picturing different things. A concrete example forces everyone to picture the same thing, so misunderstandings show up before any code is written.

A worked scenario from the book

Example

Story: As a Canadian shopper, I want to give the cashier cash and I expect the correct change so that I only pay the right amount for my groceries. The cash register reports the correct amount of change to give.

Scenario: the happy path, where the shopper gives more than the cost of the groceries and receives the correct change.

  • Given I am a Canadian shopper and have purchased groceries worth $4.97,
  • When I give the cashier $5.00,
  • Then I expect to get $0.05 change.

Writing this example surfaced a business rule the team may not have thought of. Canada has no pennies in use. The amount is rounded up or down, and change is given to the nearest nickel ($0.05).

Tabular format

One of Janet's favourite ways to show examples is a table. It quickly shows what is missing and what people are thinking during the discussion. Each line can become a test. Figure 4.2 shows the grocery scenario this way.

Add two more rows to the table: one where the shopper pays with exact change, and one where the groceries cost $4.98. What change should the register give in each case, and which rule from the story decides it?

Other ways to structure the conversation

There are many good ways to bring teams together to draw out examples:

  • Jeff Patton's story mapping helps the whole team walk through the user's journey through their product.
  • The authors like to capture specific business rules, plus examples that illustrate them, with example mapping (Chapter 5).
  • Ellen Gottesdiener and Mary Gorman's 7 product dimensions make sure teams collect examples of many different aspects of value.

Any technique that promotes face-to-face collaboration in small cross-functional groups is worth trying. The authors point to Chapter 7 of their book More Agile Testing for more details and stories.

This is your foundation

Getting delivery and business team members together to gather examples is key to delivering value to customers at a frequent and sustainable pace. It lets the team build shared understanding before they start coding, and it keeps everyone grounded in reality. The concrete examples become executable tests. These make sure the team builds the right thing, and that it keeps working correctly until customers want to change it.

Key idea

When a discussion about requirements turns hand-wavy, or turns into an argument about how something should behave, stop and ask for an example. Even better, get people drawing examples on a real or virtual whiteboard.

Summary
  • Examples build shared understanding. They help the team build the right thing with fewer rejected stories and a shorter cycle time.
  • BDD. Scenarios in natural, domain-specific language using Given/When/Then, often automated. Keep each to one thing.
  • ATDD. A looser approach: at least one happy-path example and one per type of misbehaviour at planning, more detail during coding, some examples automated.
  • SBE. Start from goals, such as with impact mapping, hold a specification workshop, and refine examples into executable specifications that act as living documentation.
  • Tables. A table shows gaps quickly, and each row can be a test.
  • Stop and ask. In a vague discussion, ask for an example.

Section 2 · Chapter 5

Enabling Collaboration

The big idea: a few simple practices, such as asking why, talking in small mixed groups, mapping impacts and rules, and drawing things where everyone can see them, help teams and customers solve the right problem together.

Collaboration within a team, and between teams, is one of the cornerstones of successful agile teams. Yet many teams have no idea how to start building those relationships. This chapter covers a few very simple practices that help you get traction.

Collaborate with customers

Start with the customer, who is usually represented by a product owner. If a team does not understand the problem the customer is trying to solve, it may solve the wrong one. Teams must work with the customer to understand their true needs.

First, everyone on the team should understand the domain, the business area the product works in. You can do this by working closely with end users, asking for examples or scenarios, or drawing pictures on whiteboards to show differences and clarify meanings. Questions like these make the customer think about usage and the risk that goes with it:

  • "How will you use this?"
  • "What's the worst that can happen?"

Testers can help developers and customers talk to each other, but they should take care not to get in the way.

The Power of Three

Getting a tester, a programmer and a business expert (a product owner, product manager or business analyst) together to talk about a user story is called the Power of Three. George Dinwiddie calls it the Three Amigos. It builds shared understanding of stories, of features, and of how they fit into the product.

Key idea

Three viewpoints see more than one. Whenever a question comes up, bring the tester, programmer and business expert together, plus one or two other roles if useful.

Example

A developer pair is working on a new story, and one of the business-facing tests fails. They go to a tester and say they think the test expects the wrong behaviour. That is the moment to grab a product owner for a three-way talk. The quick conversation saves much time later, compared with fixing a defect that made it into the code.

Depending on the product and the kind of feature, you may need more perspectives, such as a UX designer, a data expert or an operations expert.

Impact mapping

Frameworks such as impact mapping help you decide which features to build, and maybe even their priority. The steps are:

  1. Start with the goal of a feature. This is the "why".
  2. Identify who might help you reach the goal and who might get in your way. These are the actors, the "who".
  3. For each "who", ask how they might help or hinder you in reaching the goal. These are the impacts, the "how".
  4. Think about what deliverables might result from each impact. This is the "what".

The exercise helps the team see the big picture and the reasons behind what they are building. It answers the question "How will we know if this feature achieves the goal after we release it?"

Example (added here)

A small online shop wants to cut the number of abandoned carts. Here is its map, built left to right.

  1. Why (goal): Lift completed checkouts by 10% within three months.
  2. Who (actors): first-time shoppers, who might help or hinder by finishing or abandoning checkout; and the support team, who hear the complaints.
  3. How (impacts): first-time shoppers abandon when forced to create an account, so a guest checkout would help. They also give up if shipping costs appear late, so showing costs early would help. Support staff could help by flagging the most common checkout complaints.
  4. What (deliverables): a guest checkout option; a shipping cost estimate on the cart page; a weekly report of checkout complaints.

Because the goal is a number, the team knows how to check after release whether the work paid off. A deliverable that cannot be traced to any impact is a candidate for dropping.

Try it

Pick a feature your team is about to build. Write its goal in one sentence, then list two actors who could help and one who could get in the way. What is one impact for each?

Ask questions

A feature planning meeting often starts with a discussion of how to implement the feature. Sometimes the product owner arrives with her own solution. Lisa once heard: "Take the same code as we use for discount codes and make them negative amounts so we can add surcharges." Do not let that happen. Start with the why.

When you meet a business stakeholder such as a product owner to talk about upcoming features, the first question is "Why are we doing this feature?" Another good one is "What problem will this solve for the business, the customer, or the end user?"

QA can stand for "Question Asker", an idea from Pete Walen. Testers routinely ask questions nobody else thinks of. If your team has no tester, designate a question-asker role.

Experienced teams often build quality criteria into how they work. If they want to prevent security issues such as cross-site scripting (XSS, where an attacker gets a page to run harmful script) and SQL injection (sneaking database commands into an input field), the protection is probably built into the system's architecture.

Business stakeholders, however, often wrongly assume the technical team already knows which quality attributes matter. Examples are how many concurrent users there will be, which devices must be supported, and how fast the perceived response time must be. Chapter 7 covers quality attributes.

Specific and open-ended questions both help expose hidden assumptions:

  • "Is it possible we could implement this feature and not solve the problem?"
  • "What will users do before using this feature?"
  • "What will they do afterwards?"

Example mapping

Matt Wynne introduced the authors to example mapping, and they find it a great way to explore a new feature and the value it should deliver. You work with the product owner or other stakeholders on the business rules, in a Power of Three kind of discussion. Business rules are a good place to start because they help you slice a feature into stories and build shared understanding of how it should behave.

The technique works well because concrete examples are used to clarify the rules. As the talk continues, keep the main goal in mind and focus on the value the feature gives customers and end users. Teams often find that real examples expose more business rules.

Example mapping uses four kinds of index cards, one colour each:

  • A story card (yellow) at the top names the story being discussed.
  • Rule cards (blue) go in a row beneath it, one per business rule.
  • Example cards (green) go under the rule they illustrate. Each often starts "The one where...".
  • Question cards (pink) go off to the side for anything nobody can answer yet.

How a session runs, step by step

  1. Put the story card at the top.
  2. Ask the product owner what rules apply. Write each on a rule card.
  3. For each rule, ask for a real example and write it on a green card under that rule.
  4. When someone says "I'm not sure" or two people disagree, write a question card instead of arguing. Someone follows up later.
  5. New rules often appear while examples are discussed. Add them to the row.
  6. Stop when the team feels they understand the story. A rule with many examples may be a story on its own, and rules can be used to slice the feature into stories.
Example (added here)

The story is "As a shopper, I can apply a discount code at checkout." Three rule cards go up: a code can be used once per customer, a code expires on its end date, a code cannot be combined with another code. Under the first rule go examples: "The one where Sam uses SAVE10 for the first time", and "The one where Sam tries SAVE10 a second time". Under the expiry rule: "The one where the code expires at midnight and Sam checks out at 00:01". While writing examples, the tester asks whether a code works on items already on sale. Nobody knows, so it becomes a pink question card for the product owner. The team leaves with three rules, several ready-made test cases and one open question, instead of a vague story.

Using example mapping to draw out business rules, examples and open questions is an effective way to make sure the larger team starts on the same page when they plan the iteration.

Build trust using visibility

Testing lets teams identify risks so customers can make the best decisions, and that builds trust. When the team asks for their input, customers gain confidence that they will get working software.

Teams can use mind maps, flow diagrams, context diagrams, state diagrams or other tools to look at dependencies and the ripple effects of each new feature. If features are not easily understood and used by everyone, they cannot give the value intended. Keeping an eye on the big picture is one strength testers bring to agile teams.

Drawing on a whiteboard while discussing a story is a proven way to improve communication, and getting up and moving helps people think and learn.

Try it

At your next story discussion, grab a marker and some cards, sticky notes or a whiteboard. Ask everyone to stand up and draw, or to move cards around. What do people notice that they had not noticed while seated?

If some participants are remote, use online collaborative tools. The authors' teams have found mind maps very effective, either on a physical whiteboard or in a real-time tool such as Mindmup, for identifying unknowns and finding creative solutions.

Visual aids help teams ask better, more focused questions. Whenever the team hits a problem, they find a way to make it visible, so they can start thinking up experiments to make the problem smaller.

Summary
  • Understand the customer's real need. Work with the product owner, learn the domain, and ask "How will you use this?" and "What's the worst that can happen?".
  • Power of Three. Tester, programmer and business expert (the Three Amigos) talk about a story, and bring in others as needed.
  • Impact mapping. Goal (why), actors (who), impacts (how), deliverables (what). It shows how you will know the feature met its goal.
  • Ask questions. Start with why. QA means Question Asker. Do not assume stakeholders have thought of quality attributes.
  • Example mapping. Story, rules, examples and questions on cards, used to expose rules and slice stories.
  • Visibility builds trust. Draw, move cards, use mind maps, and make problems visible so you can experiment.

  1. Who takes part in the Power of Three, and what is another name for it?

    Show answer

    A tester, a programmer and a business expert such as a product owner, product manager or business analyst. George Dinwiddie calls it the Three Amigos.

  2. What are the four levels of an impact map, in order?

    Show answer

    Goal (why), actors (who), impacts (how), and deliverables (what).

  3. What is the first question to ask a product owner about an upcoming feature?

    Show answer

    "Why are we doing this feature?"

  4. What kinds of things go on the cards in example mapping?

    Show answer

Section 2 · Chapter 6

Explore Continuously

The big idea: exploratory testing is a person learning about the software by trying small experiments, and it works best when it has a purpose, a time limit, and a fresh point of view.

More agile teams are finding value in exploratory testing, but many teams still find it new or unfamiliar. So this chapter starts with what it is and what it involves. In Explore It!, Elisabeth Hendrickson defines it as "simultaneously designing and executing tests to learn about the system, using your insights from the last experiment to inform the next."

In practice, a person uses the system and watches what it really does. They design a small experiment, see the result, and change the next experiment based on what they learned. Along the way they may make surprising discoveries, including effects of interactions that nobody had thought about. Exploratory testing also exposes misunderstandings about what the software is supposed to do.

Testers, programmers, or any other team members who explore need to be open to what they see. They observe, learn, use critical thinking, and challenge expectations. The goal is to reduce risk and gain confidence in the product. There are no scripts and no lists of expected outputs. Instead, the session has an identified goal, some resources (or variations), and a mission. People take notes as they go, and later they debrief (talk through what happened) with teammates and business stakeholders. After a session, the tester may show bugs to the team, or propose new features or stories that seem to be needed.

Exploratory testing is a disciplined approach. Do not mix it up with two other things:

  • Ad hoc testing is testing done with no planning or documentation.
  • Monkey testing means entering random inputs and taking random actions to see what breaks.

Think of the difference between wandering around lost and exploring on purpose to gain insight. The rest of the chapter gives a few techniques for exploring with purpose.

Personas, jobs, and roles

Testing a new product with fresh eyes is a gift. A newcomer has few ideas about how the product "should" behave, and their confirmation bias (the habit of noticing only what matches what you already believe) is weaker. Everyone still has unconscious cognitive biases, but a new pair of eyes sees the product more objectively. Lisa has seen this when she pairs with new testers on her team: they quickly spot bugs that have been there all along but that nobody else could "see".

If you know the product inside out, you can get some of that fresh view by taking on a persona or a role. This helps you beat biases such as inattentional blindness (not noticing something obvious because your attention is elsewhere) and confirmation bias.

A persona is a made-up user that the team creates, with traits such as age, education, experience, personality quirks, and profession. Some teams keep a standard set of personas for their target customers and use them when they design new features. Figure 6.1 shows a hacker persona that you could use in testing.

Personas work even better when you combine them with a job or role. The book gives this example:

Example

Jill, an executive assistant, is 30 years old, always in a hurry with too much to do, and looks for shortcuts as she uses the product. Test your team's hotel reservation application as Jill, who is booking a hotel at the last minute for her boss.

When a tester takes on Jill's persona, she is likely to use the feature in ways she normally would not. For example, she might find that clicking the submit button several times out of impatience creates duplicate reservations.

Workflows and tours

A common way to explore is to walk through the expected workflows, also called user journeys. Start with one journey, then explore variations of it. In the hotel-booking application, an obvious journey is: search for a place and a date range for a number of guests, choose a room, and enter address details to confirm. A variation is to enter an address from a different country. Does the form accept several formats of postal code? Does it check the postal code against the street address?

Another popular approach is to use tours. Think of a trip to Paris. You might visit the Eiffel Tower, the Louvre, the Arc de Triomphe, and maybe Notre Dame cathedral. When you have seen them all, you do the tour again but visit the sights in a different order, and things may look different. Software behaves the same way. In the landmark tour, you use different features and capabilities in different orders, and that may cause unexpected behavior. Figure 6.2 shows the idea.

Example (added here)

On a booking site, the usual tour is search, book, pay. On a second tour you search, start a booking, go back and change the dates, then jump to the cancel page, and only then return to pay. The page now shows a price for the old dates, because the pay step never learned that the booking changed. A single fixed script would not have taken this path.

The book gives a hint: search the internet for "exploratory test tours" and you will find many ideas, but it suggests designing your own.

Risks and value to the customer

Teams often overlook business risks and what the customer values. You can design exploratory sessions around both, to bring hidden assumptions into the open. Ask "What is the worst thing that can happen?" and the answer may point to a risk worth exploring. If theft of customer data is a big risk, for example, the team will want to spend extra time exploring the security of the product.

Value is the flip side of risk. So also ask "What is the best thing that can happen?" and explore around that business value.

Explore in pairs or groups

You can explore at any time. Programmers may explore while coding to get quick feedback on visual problems. Testers may explore for browser compatibility. Even so, the authors recommend pairing with someone to get the most out of it (Figure 6.3).

At the feature level, one way to explore is with groups. One of Lisa's teams often got people together for ad hoc or exploratory sessions on major or risky features, to gain extra confidence. Testing for concurrency issues (problems when several users or processes act at the same time) is a good example. More eyes on the problem means a better chance of finding something.

Like mob programming, mob testing can be used for exploring. One person is the driver, a role that rotates every few minutes, and several others help by asking questions and making suggestions. Having many viewpoints may reveal effects on other parts of the system.

Charters

In Explore It!, Elisabeth Hendrickson describes how to use charters for effective exploratory testing. A charter organizes what you need to learn about your application into focused, time-boxed sessions (sessions with a fixed length). Charters work well together with personas, jobs, and roles. Elisabeth's template is:

Explore <target>
With <resources>
To discover <information of value to someone>

A good way to begin a charter is to think about the resources, or as Janet prefers, the types of variations you will use. Security testing, for example, may need various format exploits. You can write a charter to explore several UI pages with those exploits. The book's example:

Example

Explore the user signup page for 30 minutes. With cross-site scripting exploits. To discover any vulnerabilities.

The authors like to time-box sessions because it keeps the charter focused. A simple way is to put the time limit in the charter itself, as above.

Lisa likes to introduce people to exploratory testing by putting them in small groups to test toys and games for young children. First they create a persona, such as "Judy is a very strong, active four-year-old." Then they test a game made for ages 3 to 6 with this charter:

Example

Explore the game as Judy. Using all her energy, unexpected movements, and strength. To discover whether the game is safe for her age group.

If the group can break off a small piece that is a choking hazard, the game may not be safe. That is a different test from simply using the game as your adult self.

Example (added here)

A team has just built a "save payment card" option in checkout. They write a charter that uses Jill, the hurried assistant, as the persona: "Explore the checkout page for 45 minutes. With an expired card, a card pasted with spaces, and repeated clicks on Save. To discover whether Jill can end up with a wrong or duplicate saved card." The target, the resources, and the information sought are all clear, and the time limit stops the session from drifting.

There are other ways to write charters. Some teams use mind maps, some use mnemonics (memory aids), and some just write one sentence about what they want to explore.

Try it

Pick a screen in a product you use daily. Write a charter in the three-line template, with a time limit, and use a persona that is unlike you.

Executing, learning, steering

People often get stuck when they write or run their first charter. The authors' hint: if that happens, do not think too hard the first time. Write it, then try it. Use your observation skills, critical thinking, and intuition.

As you run charters, you learn, and what you learn lets you write new charters. This is the steering part: each session points to the next one. The authors also encourage note-taking. A benefit of pairing is that the person who is not driving can write the notes. They also believe that debriefing with other team members after exploring is key to learning and sharing information.

Additional techniques

Some techniques help you think outside the box. Mike Talks has his "Oblique Testing" cards, which can push a tester down a path they might not have considered. Beren van Daele's TestSphere cards get testers thinking and talking about their testing in different ways. Our unconscious biases can stop us from seeing important problems, and cards like these can offset those biases and help teams be more creative.

Leverage tools for effective exploring

Exploratory testing is human-centered, but automated scripts and tools can help. They can generate test data or "set the scene" so you start exploring from the right place. Other tools help too:

  • Emulators stand in for embedded or mobile devices, although real devices still need to be tested and explored.
  • Log files can reveal "silent" failures or possible data loss. One of Janet's teams started highlighting warnings to make them more visible, and that exposed a major problem: one method was being used incorrectly.
  • Recorders can track which pages you visited and what data you used, so you can replay the steps if something unexpected turns up.
Key idea

Exploring is not wandering. You design small experiments, learn from each result, and let that learning steer the next experiment, with a charter, a time limit, and notes to keep it disciplined.

Summary
  • Exploratory testing. Designing and running tests at the same time, using what you learn to shape the next test. No scripts.
  • Not ad hoc or monkey testing. Those lack planning, or use random input. Exploring has a mission and notes.
  • Personas, jobs, roles. Take on another point of view, such as Jill the hurried assistant or a hacker, to beat biases.
  • Workflows and tours. Walk a journey, then vary it. The landmark tour revisits features in different orders.
  • Risk and value. Ask for the worst and the best that can happen, and explore around both.
  • Pairs and mobs. More people give more perspectives. In a mob, one driver rotates every few minutes.
  • Charters. Explore target, with resources, to discover information. Time-box them.
  • Tools. Scripts for data, emulators, logs, and recorders support the human explorer.

Testing Quality Attributes

The big idea: quality attributes such as security, performance, and reliability are constraints on every story, so the team talks about them early instead of testing them at the end.

Quality attributes, which some people call non-functional requirements, are often forgotten when a team discusses a new feature or story. A quality attribute defines the properties under which a feature must operate. The authors prefer not to see them as an add-on. They see them as a constraint the team must consider with every feature or story.

Defining quality attributes

There are two main types to consider: development attributes and operational attributes.

  • Development attributes include code maintainability, code reuse, and testability. They describe the "how" of building the code. This is internal, technology-facing quality, and the software delivery team owns it.
  • Operational attributes are what people usually mean when they talk about quality attributes.

Ellen Gottesdiener and Mary Gorman sort some of the quality attributes into operational and development groups, as shown in Figure 7.1.

The operational attributes in the figure are availability, recoverability, installability, robustness, interoperability, safety, performance, scalability, reliability, security, and usability. The development attributes are efficiency, flexibility, modifiability, portability, reusability, and testability.

Many of these are technology-facing (Chapter 9 on the agile testing quadrants explains that term). If business stakeholders do not understand them well, the delivery team can help them set the right quality level for each attribute.

Example (added here)

A product owner says a ticket-booking site must be "fast". The team asks what that means. Together they agree: search results appear in under two seconds for up to 5,000 people searching at once. That sentence names an attribute (performance), sets a level, and gives testers something to check.

Mitigating risks by collaborating early

Every product and organization has its own needs and risks. When you consider which quality attributes matter to your customers, the team can talk about the risks for the product.

Example

Janet worked on a team where reliability was the most important quality attribute, though not the only one. The team asked, "What do we need to be able to prove reliability?" They concluded they needed a complete reliability testing environment. The build was deployed there at the end of every iteration and ran a full set of automated tests plus some exploratory tests. The team worked to get the automated tests and simulations running with every story.

Some teams think about quality attributes only after they deliver the functionality, and then write stories for the "non-functional" requirements. This is generally a bad idea, because it may mean redoing the architecture or the code design.

These attributes may in fact have a higher priority than the functional requirements. In some business areas, valuable functionality does not make up for weak security or performance. Teams that wait until late in the cycle to test them, often in the endgame just before release, hit a total roadblock. These problems are usually design problems, and they cannot be fixed that late.

Key idea

Do not wait for the product owner to raise quality attributes. The delivery team should start the conversation early, because the owner is probably thinking about features and taking quality for granted.

As a team, decide which aspects of quality matter most to customers and the business. A good first step is to draw a context diagram of the proposed feature, which shows how it interacts with other capabilities or systems. If you know the dependencies and fragile areas early, there is time to make good design decisions and to gain the technical knowledge you need. The team might plan a spike, which is an experiment or investigation story, to explore possible designs and architecture.

Release planning and feature planning are good moments to ask business stakeholders questions like these:

  • What is the worst thing that can happen after we release this capability? Does that make it high risk?
  • Is it ok if the system, or one capability, is down for some time? If not, what is the maximum time, or percentage of time, it can be down?
  • For a web app, which browsers might customers use?
  • Can we assume customers use mobile devices? Would that include phones and tablets, and both Apple and Android? What about other devices?
  • How will we know if the feature is successful once we release it?

Planning for pre-release testing

Some quality attributes need more testing right before release, when all the parts of the feature set are connected. For example, the team may do load or performance testing in a staging environment (a copy of production used for final checks) to get a last baseline. If the team uses blue/green deploys in a cloud setup (two identical production environments, with only one serving users at a time), it may run this testing on the idle production environment.

Teams can also use release feature toggles (switches that turn a feature on or off) and similar techniques to hide new features from customers while they test various quality attributes in production. When the team is confident about the quality of every aspect of the feature, they switch it on. Chapter 8 covers testing in production.

Planning for later learning

The last question, "How will we know if the feature is successful once we release it?", is a good way to talk about the purpose of the feature and how to measure whether it reaches its goals. Provisioning data for analytics tools has to be planned into the stories for each feature. At story level, think about how the team can monitor the system to measure usage and the quality of the attribute. The team may need new tools for logging and monitoring.

At task level, programmers should think about instrumenting the code (adding hooks that record what it does) so that the team can measure with automated tests or monitoring tools. Figure 7.2 shows how a task board can carry this. The solution can be as simple as running an optimizer on a new database query, or using a static analysis tool (a program that checks code without running it) to check that the code meets accessibility standards. A more complete approach, such as instrumenting every event in the code so production issues can be pinpointed and fixed fast, may be right for you.

Example (added here)

For a "download invoice" story, a team adds a task "log how long PDF creation takes and report it to the monitoring dashboard". After release the dashboard shows that big invoices take 20 seconds. The team learns this from real use and fixes it, instead of hearing about it in a complaint.

Try it

Take a story from your current backlog. Which quality attributes from Figure 7.1 matter most for it, and which task would you add to the board so you can measure one of them after release?

Regulatory compliance

Regulatory compliance is not always treated as a quality attribute. Like the attributes above, though, you need to think about it from the start. Compliance does not have to mean stacks of documents, but it usually means extra work for the team, so it is wise to plan early.

Organizations need to work with auditors and regulatory agencies to learn what information is required to show compliance. Everyone needs the same vision of what an appropriately disciplined approach looks like. For example, if automated tests run every day, their results act as living documentation. Could those results serve as evidence of the approach or coverage? Both authors have worked with teams that had to show compliance, in medical devices and in finance, and they did it with very little "extra" work. The book points to Chapter 21, "Agile Testing in Regulated Environments," in More Agile Testing for more examples.

Summary
  • Quality attributes. Properties under which a feature must operate. Treat them as a constraint on every story, not an add-on.
  • Two types. Development attributes (the "how" of the code, owned by the delivery team) and operational attributes (what most people mean).
  • Collaborate early. Late "non-functional" stories can force a redo of architecture or design. Start the conversation yourself, with a context diagram and maybe a spike.
  • Ask stakeholders. Worst case, acceptable downtime, browsers, devices, and how success will be known.
  • Pre-release. Load tests in staging or on the idle blue/green environment. Hide features behind toggles while testing in production.
  • Later learning. Plan analytics data, logging, monitoring, and code instrumentation into stories and tasks.
  • Compliance. Plan early and work with auditors. Daily automated test results can serve as living documentation.

Testing in DevOps

The big idea: getting changes to customers now happens through fast, automated pipelines, but testing skills still matter. Testing simply spreads across the pipeline and continues into production, where teams watch and observe the live system.

Software teams have always had to get new changes into production, the live system that customers use. In the past much of that was manual. Today there are new tools and practices for building software artifacts (the packaged output of a build) and deploying them into test and production environments. The basic process has not changed. Teams still run many testing activities so they feel confident about each change. Some of the testing has new names, but basic testing skills are still relevant.

The DevOps movement grew from a gap. Some organizations had embraced agile development but left their operations staff (the people who run the servers and the live system) out of the change. It also grew from the move to cloud-hosted applications, where infrastructure as code (setting up servers by writing code, not by typing commands by hand) replaced command line work. Roles have adapted as a result. Operations specialists learn to code. Programmers take responsibility for their code even after it reaches production, instead of throwing it over the wall to operations.

Testers adapt too. They help design automated test suites that give reliable and valuable information. They help tune the delivery pipeline. They also test infrastructure code to make sure it runs reliably. The book points to Chapter 23 of More Agile Testing for how operations specialists can help a delivery team improve quality. They can set up test environments, help build test automation frameworks, generate test data, and more.

Continuous delivery and deployment

A team practicing continuous delivery (CD) has a deployable release candidate every time a new change is committed to the code repository and then passes through a deployment pipeline. A release candidate is a version that could go to customers. The pipeline starts with continuous integration (automatically building and checking each change). That may include automated test suites at several levels: unit tests, API tests, and full workflow tests through the user interface. It may also include other steps, such as static code analysis (a tool reads the code without running it and flags problems) and automated deployments to various environments. Business stakeholders then decide whether to deploy the release candidate to production, and they may do so many times a day. Figure 8.1 shows an example.

Continuous deployment (also abbreviated CD) is the same process with one difference: each successful release candidate deploys to production automatically. Nobody presses a button. Figure 8.2 shows an example.

If you have not done this before, it sounds as scary as testing in production. If you release many times a day, how can you fit in all the testing you need? The answer is that human-centric testing is as much a part of a delivery pipeline as automated tests. That covers tests the team has not yet automated, and activities such as exploratory testing (learning the product by using it, with no fixed script) and accessibility testing (checking people with disabilities can use it).

Key idea

The key is recognizing the difference between deploying and releasing. Code can be deployed to production without customers seeing it, so testing can continue afterwards.

Techniques such as release feature toggles (switches that turn a feature on or off without a new deployment) let a team hide changes from customers until all the needed testing is complete. Testing can therefore happen asynchronously, meaning it does not have to finish before the code ships.

Example (added here)

A shop team adds a "gift wrap" option to the checkout. The code goes to production on Tuesday morning behind a toggle that is switched off, so customers see nothing. The tester then explores the feature in production using a test account and finds that the price does not update for express shipping. The developers fix it the same afternoon. On Wednesday the product owner switches the toggle on for everyone. The code was deployed on Tuesday and released on Wednesday.

Continuous delivery or deployment is a high standard for a team. Delivery team members and business stakeholders need a shared understanding of each feature and story. Developers must master ways of hiding features from some or all customers until release. The pipeline has to be fast enough to deploy daily or many times a day. That needs good infrastructure, and infrastructure means more code to build and test. There are many new skills to learn. When each team member brings their T-shaped skills (deep skill in one area plus working knowledge of others) and everyone collaborates, the team can solve problems more easily and keep shortening the feedback loops that the pipeline represents.

Testing in production

"Testing in production" once sounded like "throw the code into production and let customers find the bugs," or "test live and hope nobody's account is affected." Sadly, some teams did exactly that. Today the phrase has a less fearful meaning. It has become a necessity in many cases, but it does not mean releasing poor quality code and letting customers find the bugs.

It helps companies in several ways. It is usually impossible to build a test environment that looks exactly like production, so there is no easy way to know what the software will do until it runs there. The techniques include:

  • Release feature toggling turns specific functionality on for specific customers to get quick feedback. This is sometimes called a learning release or a minimal viable product (MVP, the smallest version that teaches you something).
  • A/B testing is probably the best-known technique. It shows different designs to different people and judges which one leads to more click-throughs or sales.
  • Analytics and tracing show details such as how one user moves through the application, or totals such as the percentage of customers who use a certain capability.

Removing unused features can matter as much as adding popular ones, because every line of code has a maintenance cost and adds risk. In short, testing in production is about monitoring and observing.

Monitoring and observability

Monitoring system health in production is as old as software that logs events. Teams set up alerts for certain errors or for going over an error budget (the amount of failure they agree to tolerate). One example: "Post an alert when the number of 503 errors goes up by 10% over average." When the alert fires, team members dig into the logs and analytics to find and fix the problem.

Professional testers know you cannot predict every error that might happen in production. That led to a newer practice called observability, often written o11y (the letter o, then 11 letters, then y). Teams that practice it instrument their production code and log every single event, so tools can analyze the data when needed. Using tools and experiments, they study structured log data, metrics and traces, and learn things about the product that a test environment cannot show. Testers contribute here because they notice unusual patterns and identify risks. Abby Bangser has described observability as a form of tool-assisted exploratory testing.

Observability lets a team respond fast when a user reports a problem nobody has seen before, or when metrics look unusual. Structured logs let the team trace one user's activity and find where the system first behaved wrongly. The team can then revert the change or deploy a fix, often within minutes. Recovering this quickly is what lets teams release small changes fearlessly, and it is what makes continuous delivery or deployment a safe practice.

Example (added here)

Support reports that one customer cannot pay with a saved card. Nobody has a test for that case. Because every event is logged with a user id, the team follows that customer's path and sees that the card service started returning errors right after Monday's release, but only for cards saved before 2019. They revert the change in ten minutes and write a new test for old cards.

New technology brings us new capabilities

In the past few years, "big data" storage has become affordable even for small companies. Teams can log information about every event in any test or production environment. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have sped up the analysis of these huge amounts of data. Teams can learn quickly how customers use the product, find problems before customers do, and recover quickly from failures.

Testing in production is only one part of building quality in. Teams still plan and run all the appropriate tests while they write the production code. They can additionally observe production use and run risk-free tests in production for another level of confidence.

Figure 8.3 is a graphic by Cindy Sridharan. It shows three activities working together. Teams test to simulate production as well as they can. They monitor production for failures they can predict. They use observability to catch whatever slips through those other quality efforts. The overlap in the middle is the best-effort simulation of failure modes.

  • Continuous delivery vs deployment. In delivery, people decide when a release candidate goes live. In deployment, every passing candidate goes live automatically.
  • Deploying is not releasing. Feature toggles hide code from customers, so human-centric testing can run asynchronously.
  • Testing in production. Toggles, A/B tests, analytics and tracing teach you how the real system behaves, and they do not mean customers find your bugs.
  • Observability. Logging every event lets teams trace unknown problems and recover in minutes, which makes frequent small releases safe.

Section 3 · Chapter 9

The Agile Testing Quadrants

The big idea: the quadrants are a thinking tool that sorts testing into four groups, so a team can see every kind of test it might need, who should do it, and when.

Brian Marick first wrote about the agile testing quadrants (Figure 9.1) in 2003. At that time Lisa and Janet were trying to work out how to talk about testing to the agile community, especially the testers. Most agile people were then discussing only "customer tests" and "programmer tests." Many teams focused only on functional acceptance tests, which check how features should behave. That is still a pitfall for teams moving to agile. The quadrants gave the authors a way to talk about all the types of testing a team might need, and they help the team see the big picture.

The quadrants are a taxonomy, a sorted list, of types of testing. Use them as a thinking tool. They help a team discuss which testing activities it needs and make sure it has the right people, resources and environments to do them.

How to read the model

Tests on the left-hand side guide development. They are written before coding starts, or while coding proceeds. Tests on the right-hand side critique (evaluate) the product after coding is complete. Left-side tests help prevent defects. Right-side tests find defects in the code, or perhaps find missing features.

The top half holds tests that business stakeholders can read. They answer "Are we building the right thing?" and are about external quality as the business defines it. The bottom half holds tests written by and for technical team members. Business stakeholders care about the end results but would not try to read these tests. This half is about the correctness of internal code and infrastructure.

The quadrants are numbered for easy reference:

  • Quadrant 1 (Q1): technology-facing tests that guide development.
  • Quadrant 2 (Q2): business-facing tests that guide development.
  • Quadrant 3 (Q3): business-facing tests that critique the product.
  • Quadrant 4 (Q4): technology-facing tests that critique the product.

The model helps teams think through the testing they need to be confident in what they build. It also builds a common testing language inside the team, and across teams when it is used to communicate with the wider organization. Janet's favorite thing about it is that it gives a holistic view of testing and makes the whole team's responsibility for testing activities visible.

Key idea

Each team has its own mix of business domain, product, skills, product maturity, technical stack and regulation, so the quadrants are a tool, not a rule. Fill them with the testing your context needs.

Figure 9.2 shows typical types of tests for each quadrant. Use them as a guideline. There are many gray areas, and your team's context is unique, so your quadrants should be too.

Example (added here)

A team builds a hotel booking form. In Q2 they write examples such as "a guest cannot book a checkout date before check-in" and sketch the form on paper. In Q1 the developers write unit tests for the date logic. In Q3 a tester explores the form like a nervous first-time visitor, and a few real users try a beta. In Q4 the team checks that 500 people booking at once does not slow the page, and that card details cannot be read by others.

What tests in what order?

The numbers 1, 2, 3, 4 are only for easy reference. "Business-facing tests that guide development" is a mouthful, so people say "Quadrant 2" or "Q2." The numbers do not mean the testing should happen in that order. The team decides the right time for each activity as it plans.

Example

A team decides to use a new architecture for upcoming features. They must be sure it will scale to a certain number of users and load. They do a "spike," which means writing throw-away code only to test the architecture. They run load and performance tests on it (Q4) to see whether it meets response time needs and stays stable. If satisfied, they delete the spike and start stories for a new feature, this time using their usual development practices. If not, they may rethink the architecture and repeat.

For feature development, most teams probably start in Q2. They may draw paper prototypes and show them to potential customers. The delivery and business people may hold a story-mapping or specification workshop to discuss features and slice them into stories. In backlog refinement or story-readiness workshops, they can use activities such as example mapping (a structured conversation that collects rules and examples for a story) to dig out more detail.

Once the team starts on a story, they work on Q1 tests, writing unit tests as part of test-driven development. At the same time they may work on story tests in Q2. When enough of a feature is delivered to explore, they may move into Q3. But if security is the top concern for the business and customers, security testing in Q4 may take priority over functional story testing from Q2. Each team finds its own way of working, and it may differ for different features or projects.

Using the quadrants

Janet and Lisa have seen teams use the quadrants in many ways. Some put a poster of the blank quadrants on the wall. When they plan a new feature or release, they talk through all the testing they need and write each type in the right quadrant. It works as a reminder of what to do and what they may have forgotten.

Quadrant 1

Teams can also use the quadrants to decide what to automate. Developers usually automate Q1 tests as they write the production code. Many teams use test-driven development (TDD): write a small unit test for a small piece of functionality, then write the code that makes it pass. Q1 tests are built to run fast because they cover a tiny area of code and usually do not touch other layers or databases. They give the fast feedback teams need to change code quickly and fearlessly.

Quadrant 2

Business-facing tests that guide development are an important foundation for most teams. Product owners, developers, testers and others meet often to plan features and stories. They may use example mapping to draw out the business rules of each story and the examples that illustrate them. Teams that practice behavior-driven development (BDD), acceptance test-driven development (ATDD) or specification by example (SBE) turn these into scenarios that specify behavior as executable tests. The scenarios can be automated as the code is written.

Quadrant 3

Q3 tests tend to be human-centric. They find out whether the delivered stories and features give customers the value intended. Automation may help by setting up data or state. More teams now do exploratory testing in production, using release feature toggles to hide new features until testing is done. Q3 includes forms of testing in production, such as monitoring analytics to learn what really happens and how customers use the features. What the team learns in Q3 feeds back into Q2, often creating new stories or features.

Quadrant 4

Many Q4 tests depend on automation and tools, though some need extra human testing. For example, automated tools for accessibility testing (often shortened to "a11y": the first and last letters with 11 letters between) are still less effective than manual exploratory testing. Q4 results often feed back into Q1 as the team changes the code design to improve quality attributes. Monitoring performance and errors in production, or testing recoverability (how well the system comes back after a failure), also fall into Q4. Technology now makes it feasible and safe to test in production. That does not mean customers find bugs for us. It means we learn for sure how the code behaves in a true production environment. Chapter 8 has the details.

Defining "Done"

Use the quadrants to say what "Done" means for your team. Many teams struggle to decide what testing belongs in that definition. Chapter 3 talked about levels of detail, and they apply here. Instead of one "Done," the authors suggest being more specific:

  • Story Done likely includes all of Q1, all of Q2, and perhaps some of Q3, such as exploratory testing.
  • Feature Done includes all the story testing, and perhaps tests such as user acceptance testing (UAT) and exploratory testing at the feature level. The authors recommend that every quality attribute that could not be tested at story level be tested at feature level.
  • Release Done includes every test that applies in your context, from every quadrant.

Remember that these levels do not force an order for the tests. Think instead about which tests guide development (prevent defects) and which critique the product (find defects in the code).

Find the models that fit your context

Many teams find the quadrants useful for planning their testing. Others have adapted the model, and there are many useful variations. Chapter 8 of More Agile Testing can be downloaded from the authors' site, agiletester.ca, and includes several adaptations of the quadrants. The authors' resources list has more. Whichever model works best, keep it visible and use it to start conversations about how to keep improving your testing.

Draw an empty set of quadrants on a whiteboard. Take the next story on your backlog and write every test you would want for it in the quadrant where it belongs. Which quadrant is the emptiest, and who on your team could fill it?

  • Axes. Left guides development (prevents defects), right critiques the product (finds defects). Top is business-facing, bottom is technology-facing.
  • Quadrants. Q1 technology-facing and guiding, Q2 business-facing and guiding, Q3 business-facing and critiquing, Q4 technology-facing and critiquing.

Visualizing a Test Automation Strategy

The big idea: simple pictures, such as the pyramid and the iceberg, help the whole team talk about what to automate, at which level, and who should do it.

Test automation is a constant challenge for software teams. Many teams still have no automated regression tests (tests that re-check old features after a change). Some teams feel they have mastered it, then adopt new technology that their automation tools cannot handle. Many struggle to balance the value of their tests against the cost of maintaining them.

Wherever a team is on its automation journey, it helps to step back, think about priorities, and choose what to improve next. This chapter does not give a full introduction to automation. It shows how visual models help teams design their strategy.

Using visual models

To succeed with automation, get the whole team to help form a strategy for the different automation needs, and to carry it out. Visual models guide those conversations. The agile testing quadrants from Chapter 9 can help the team plan its automation. As they talk about the types of testing needed, they can also talk about the skills, tools and infrastructure required. This chapter adds some more models. They are thinking tools for starting conversations about how your team wants to automate tests.

The classic test automation pyramid

Mike Cohn's test automation pyramid has helped many teams since the early 2000s. The authors have adjusted it slightly (Figure 10.1) to make their intent clear. They added a cloud bubble on top to show that not all regression tests can be automated. Sometimes we need human-centric tests, which include exploratory tests (ET).

The model shows that in most contexts it pays to automate tests at the most granular level possible, which gives enough protection against regression failures. Teams that practice test-driven development (TDD) build a solid base of unit and component tests that also help guide code design. These tests run very fast, so the team gets quick feedback.

Most applications also need tests of how different layers of the architecture work together. For example, business logic usually has to talk to the database. It is generally most efficient to automate as much of this as possible at the service or API level, without going through a user interface (UI).

Some regressions only appear when two or more layers are involved. That may need workflow tests through the UI that involve the server, the database and/or an external system. In most contexts, keep end-to-end workflow tests to a minimum. They run slowly, are often the most brittle (they break easily), and usually need the most maintenance. The cloud at the top covers human-centric work such as exploratory testing and other tasks that cannot be automated.

Example (added here)

A team tests a discount rule: orders over 100 get 10% off. They write dozens of fast unit tests for the rule, including 99.99, 100 and 100.01. They add a few API tests that send a cart to the pricing service and check the total. They keep a single UI test that adds an item, goes to checkout and sees the discount. A tester explores odd cases by hand, such as a coupon plus a discount.

The pyramid encourages the team to "push tests lower." That means maximizing regression tests that are isolated to one part of the application, and minimizing those that involve many parts. When planning the testing for a feature, a team can look at the pyramid to see where each test is best automated.

The classic pyramid does not say how many tests, or what percentage, belong at each level. Seb Rose sees the model a little differently, as shown in Figure 10.2.

Seb's model makes clear that a test is more expensive when it needs more layers of the application to run. For example, you can have a unit-level test of the UI that involves no other layer. You can use TDD for each isolated layer, whether it is the server, the API, the UI or a microservice.

There have been many adaptations of the pyramid over the years (and yes, the classic one is really a triangle). Chapter 15 of More Agile Testing includes several, among them those from Alister Scott and Sharon Robson.

Teams that already have automated tests can draw the "shape" of their own pyramid to see where their current tests sit. Many teams start with mostly UI tests, which gives an "upside-down" pyramid, also called an "ice cream cone." Others have an hourglass. None of these shapes is necessarily wrong. But if the current automation does not meet the team's needs, the picture shows what needs to change. For teams just starting out, a conversation around a visual model helps them choose their eventual goal and first priorities.

Try it

Count your team's automated tests at the unit, API and UI levels and draw the shape they make. Is it a pyramid, an ice cream cone or an hourglass, and does that shape match what your team needs?

Automated tests as living documentation

Time, cost and effort are not the only things to weigh in an automation strategy. Another is who needs to read and understand the tests. Seb Rose's Test Automation Iceberg (Figure 10.3) reminds us that a very valuable property of automated tests is the living documentation they provide. It is always up to date, because the team keeps the tests passing all the time. At any moment you can tell exactly what your system does.

The parts of the iceberg above the waterline are business-readable tests. The parts below are written in technical language and are not. How much of each you need depends on the team. Lisa once worked on a team whose product was meant for other delivery teams. Everyone on that team, including the product owner, could understand the automated regression tests, even though they were written in low-level code. They did not need "business-readable" tests. In other domains it is critical that business stakeholders can understand the acceptance tests. The model reminds us to think about what our own context needs.

Extending the model

Even very thorough automated regression coverage can miss some regression failures. No test environment is exactly like production. Some bugs may be found only by "testing in production," as Chapter 8 describes.

In her book A Practical Guide to Testing in DevOps, Katrina Clokie presents her DevOps bug filter. It is a helpful picture. Unit tests can filter out only the small bugs. Different levels of integration and end-to-end tests catch progressively bigger ones. To find the fully formed bugs, teams need logging, alerting and monitoring of their production system.

Shared responsibility

The authors encourage teams to share the job of testing business rules and higher levels of integration at the API level. Testers should also be able to see the unit and component tests that developers write. As a team member, you need to understand your team's applications and how they work inside when you approach your automation strategy.

Automating through the UI is time-consuming, so it is tempting to hand it to a separate automation team, or to leave it all to the testers on the team. The authors recommend instead that developers, who write efficient, maintainable code, work together with testers, who are good at specifying test cases. Together they automate tests through the UI and through every other layer above the base of the pyramid.

Key idea

Like all the other models, the test automation pyramid is a guide. Teams with all roles in the room, asking questions, drawing on the whiteboard and designing experiments, have the best success with automation.

Visual models like these help teams talk about why they are automating tests, what their biggest pain points are, how people with different skills could help, and what their next experiments should be. In the authors' experience, a step-by-step approach works best.

  • Seb Rose's pyramid. Cost depends on how many layers a test needs, not on a fixed count per level.
  • Iceberg. Automated tests are living documentation. Decide how much must be business-readable (above the waterline).
  • DevOps bug filter. Katrina Clokie's picture: unit tests catch small bugs, bigger tests catch bigger ones, production monitoring catches fully formed bugs.

Section 4 · Chapter 11

A Tester's New Role

The big idea: a lone tester who does all the testing becomes a bottleneck, so the tester's job is shifting from doing all the testing to growing quality skills across the whole team, and seven experienced practitioners plus the authors describe what that looks like.

Many teams struggle with only one tester on the team. Some have it even worse: one tester who supports more than one delivery team. If the tester is the only person doing testing activities, that person becomes a bottleneck (a single point that everything has to squeeze through, so work piles up in front of it). Teams that deliver to production every iteration, or even more often with continuous delivery (releasing small changes to users all the time), cannot afford to have one tester do all the testing.

Nobody can predict the future, but it helps to look at how the tester's role is changing right now. The authors asked other experienced agile testing coaches and trainers what they think. Their different views may help you see how testers and teams can adapt and build a quality culture.

Example (added here)

A team ships twice a week and has one tester. By Wednesday afternoon, six finished stories are waiting for her to check them, and the release cannot go out until she is done. Nothing is wrong with her skill. The problem is that the system sends all the checking through one person.

Testers are quality glue for a team

Alex Schladebeck, Germany. When Alex started out, the tester in a team (if there was a tester on the team at all) was often solely responsible for UI automation and manual testing. Over the last 12 years she has seen the role diversify a great deal, always in a way that depends on how the team works. She sees testers doing more automation, even pairing with developers at the unit level (working side by side on the small pieces of code). She sees them involved at every point in the process, and organizing mob testing sessions, where the whole team tests together, to do exploratory testing (learning the product by exploring it while designing tests on the fly). She sees them campaigning for better feedback loops. She has even seen testers fix bugs or build features.

Make connections

The book's drawing for this part shows several people working together around a shared structure. Alex calls the diversification and role-blurring a huge freedom and a great responsibility. It makes you ask: "How do I, my skills, and my potential to learn best fit into this team's context?" The tester becomes "quality and communication glue", finding and filling the gaps in any team.

She now uses the titles "embedded quality engineer" or "embedded quality consultant". The word "tester" names only one of the many things the person does. That leads to questions like "If everyone is involved in testing, why do we need a tester?" and statements like "The developers are automating tests, so we don't need a tester role." Testing is just one of the many things a tester does.

Alex also argues against the idea that an agile team should be made of chimeras: a mix of roles in one person, a Swiss-Army-knife team member who does requirements, UX, test, security, front end and back end. Agile teams need to be diverse and cross-functional, with people of different backgrounds, interests and specialties, and without any one person being a silo (a person only they know a certain area) or a bottleneck. She thinks that balance is achievable.

She sees the tester role as many activities. Some have always been part of it:

  • working with stakeholders
  • bringing expertise to test activities
  • pairing with developers and other testers
  • supporting the product owner
  • organizing and adapting the overall quality strategy
  • getting good test data
  • identifying risk

She expects more tasks to be taken on or supported by testers in the future:

  • working with the team on testability and observability (being able to see what the running system is doing), for testing and monitoring in production
  • asking questions of the production system to explore how people use it
  • honing performance and teaching skills for exploratory testing
  • helping the team focus on value, and sometimes on minimalism, which is the value of something not done
  • adding "team health" to the quality attributes, watching the communication and stress levels of the whole team, since these affect quality greatly

In short, she believes the tester role stays important. The tester is the person whose main priority is quality, who has a passion for quality and testing, and who advocates and champions them.

An agile tester's professional journey

Paul Carvalho, Canada. When Paul coaches agile teams, he helps the whole team learn to work together and build on each other's strengths. A product owner brings business and industry knowledge. The fascinating path of evolving as testers

Claudia Badell, Uruguay. Testers can add value from many angles: as facilitators and evangelists for testing and quality in a product team, as coaches, as test consultants, and as experts in certain kinds of testing such as usability, accessibility, security and performance testing. Testers are no longer seen as gatekeepers of quality, so they can be seen and valued as quality advocates.

More and more often, testers are part of the development team and contribute from the start of development. Besides supporting manual testing and automated checks, they build bridges within the team so everyone reaches a common understanding of, and commitment to, testing. They define, follow up and adjust the testing strategies the whole team uses. They also share and evangelize their testing knowledge.

As technology, methods and processes change, and as teams and communities mature, Claudia believes testers need a proactive attitude. The future brings new challenges and chances. Depending on context, different skills and activities will be needed, but she sees core skills that keep testers in step with software development.

Experiment and find new opportunities

The core skills she lists are:

  • Be eager to learn and to try new experiments that improve the team's testing strategies.
  • Be an excellent question asker across the whole product lifecycle. Questions can be spoken or written, so clear, well-structured communication matters. They can also be asked programmatically, for example to check certain responses between two services, so technical skill matters too.
  • Have modeling skills, as a way to understand what to test and to define testing strategies that cover the different aspects needed.
  • Have some technical knowledge, so you can help define testability while the software is built, for example to support unit testing and automated integration testing.
  • Have a sharing and collaborative attitude.

She closes by saying this is an exciting time in which testers can shape part of their own future, and asks how you are getting prepared for it.

Be all that you can be

Mike Talks, New Zealand. Over six years, Mike's test team went from one group working on one waterfall project at a time to individuals working across several teams.

There is a lot of talk about a whole-team approach to quality and testing, but testers, as specialists, are still looked to for leadership here. That means making a first-pass approach for a new story or feature. It also means leading a discussion with the larger team about that approach, to get feedback and explore it. And if a testing task is too heavy for the testers alone, they can help organize a division of labor among willing teammates.

A frequent candidate is cross-browser and cross-device testing. The team usually tests stories in the iteration on its core devices, and occasionally tests on a much wider range. Here the team's fresh eyes help, and a little organization from the tester makes a huge difference.

Start with a conversation

Kathleen Naughton, United States. Kathleen says software testing has both evolved and stood still. It has evolved to the point that some organizations have cut the number of testers, or removed them entirely. It has stood still because those same organizations have struggled to value the specialty skills a tester brings.

Where testers have been reduced or removed, she has seen teams try to fill the gap by moving closer to TDD (test-driven development, writing the test before the code) so they have many automated unit tests. They do some intra-team testing, meaning they test each other's code, and rely heavily on the continuous delivery pipeline to run the automated tests. What often gets missed are the integration tests and user experience tests that high-quality products need. Where testers still exist, they tend to do manual testing after the code is finished, and their insights and suggestions get pushed down the product backlog in favor of feature work.

Be relevant and influence those around you

The first skill Kathleen thinks testers need to stay relevant is the ability to read and understand code. It lets them see what the unit and other automated tests check, spot testing gaps they can fill, and cut overlap. If unit or integration tests already exist, the tester can aim the testing at the end-user activities they do not cover.

The second is a soft skill: having conversations with programmers about their unit and integration tests. These conversations can influence design decisions, which leads to higher-quality deliverables. Knowing how to hold crucial conversations lets the whole team produce better software.

A tester reads the unit tests for a discount feature and sees they cover the maths but never a customer with an expired coupon in the cart. She asks the programmer about it over coffee. The world doesn't need more checkers

Aldo Rall, New Zealand. Aldo says testing has evolved, and the industry has built up test engineering skills and practices, though not smoothly. He thinks we have entered an era of "testing enlightenment" and that organizations and testers now think very differently about the testing role.

He sees a growing movement toward the importance of skills. The more skills a person has, the more valuable they are to a team or organization. People whose skills reach beyond test engineering contribute more than someone with only basic test design and execution. He ties this to Scott Ambler's generalizing specialists and to the T-shaped skills that Lisa and Janet discuss in their books. To future-proof a career, build your test engineering skills and also skills outside traditional testing. Forget titles: in ten years it will mean little whether you were a "test engineer", "test specialist", "tester", "test analyst" or "verification engineer". Skills are worth more than a collection of job titles, which he has found true in his own career.

T-shaped skills

A T-shaped person has depth in one area (the vertical bar) and breadth across many others (the horizontal bar). The book's drawing shows a block-letter T with "Breadth" along the top and "Depth" down the stem.

Aldo favors a holistic approach built on inclusive "and" conversations. Combine your skills in ways that suit your context, knowing the context will change. How can you combine test engineering and analysis skills in a given situation? How can you combine negotiating skills with teaching skills? How can you combine different test engineering skills to get better test coverage? His advice: think "and".

He believes a key modern skill is understanding a context and its changing nature, and adjusting. The true masters of the future workplace will observe a context, apply a fit-for-purpose mix of skills, and keep adjusting the mix as the context evolves. That intuition takes time to develop, and the field keeps changing. Learning new skills adds to the value such a person brings.

Testers bring many skills beyond test engineering. Borrowing from others, Aldo suggests a multifaceted approach, which you can call "Holistic agile testing skills", "The ten thinking hats of agile testing", or whatever suits you. The facets might be:

  • Consultant. Sometimes you consult inside your team or with another team to help solve problems.
  • Test engineering specialist. You need good, solid test engineering skills. He puts it as knowing "our testing onions from shallots".
  • Agile scholar. Keep studying agile, bring ideas to the team and experiment.
  • Coach. You will have chances to coach the team or co-workers inside and outside it. He calls this a life skill.
  • Mentor. Sometimes you mentor someone in testing.
  • Facilitator. Sometimes you step in to facilitate a decision, discussion or explanation.
  • Change agent. You may bring change and upheaval by advocating a new practice, technique or method to try and learn from.
  • Leader. You may need to step up and lead on behalf of the team.
  • Teacher. This goes without saying, especially when the team or organization is short of testing skills.
  • Business domain scholar / defender of common sense / big picture thinker. Sometimes it is good to step away and see the forest rather than the trees.

Section 4 · Chapter 12

Ingredients for Success

The big idea: every team travels its own learning journey, but a short list of success factors and confidence-building practices helps all of them, and a team gets there by running small experiments and being patient.

Each agile delivery team has the same aim: to keep getting better at delivering value to customers often, while holding the level of quality the business wants. Yet each team does this with its own mix of business domain, software product, technology stack (the set of languages and tools the product is built with), frameworks and practices. The authors have found that, despite all these differences, certain ingredients help every team.

Success factors

The authors' first book, Agile Testing, ended with a summary chapter of seven success factors. They thought these were necessary, though not sufficient, for delivering a quality product. Short delivery cycles make it easy to feel overwhelmed by all the testing to plan and do, so this chapter gives a short list of key success factors and core practices to steer by.

Whole-team approach

Elisabeth Hendrickson taught the authors that "testing is an activity, not a phase." Testing is part of building software, next to coding and many other activities. Seen this way, it is easy for anyone to pick up a testing task when needed.

Testers can teach others how to draw out concrete examples of wanted and unwanted behavior from business experts, how to judge different quality attributes (such as speed, security or usability), and how to do exploratory testing (learning the product by trying it, with no fixed script). Programmers can help testers understand the system architecture, so the testing improves, or even teach basic coding ideas. Each person can hand over some of their deep skills, whatever their role.

When a team accepts that testing and quality belong to everyone, it can combine its different skills. That builds trust and a feeling of safety, and it creates a place to learn, try experiments and keep improving.

Agile testing mindset

Testers are no longer the "quality police" who make the go or no-go call on a release. Whoever does the testing explains the risks and impact of the results, so the business can make an informed decision about releasing to production.

Having an agile testing mindset means you are curious and want to learn about everything that helps you do your job. You apply agile principles and values. You work with technical and business colleagues, and you keep the big picture in mind while the small feature increments are put together. You focus on preventing bugs, so you do not spend so much time hunting for them later.

Automate your regression tests

Regression tests re-check old features after a change. When a team starts to automate them, a few things help:

  • It is a team problem, so think "whole team" and collaborate to automate at all levels. Programmers are good at writing code, testers are good at specifying tests, and people with other skills can help with test data, infrastructure and more.
  • The test automation pyramid is a good visual model for forming and evolving the team's automation strategy.
  • Keep tests simple and easy to maintain. Then the team can work toward enough regression tests to feel confident about releasing.

Test automation is a check that you have not forgotten to change something. In other words, it is a change detector. A good automation strategy frees time for exploratory testing, so you find issues before your customer does.

Provide and obtain feedback

Successful software development depends on fast feedback. Teams need to know at once if a change has caused an unintended failure, and they want to know how customers react to a new feature. Testers are central to creating and shortening the various feedback loops. They do this by creating automated tests, doing exploratory testing, and watching production usage to learn how customers use the product.

People also need feedback about themselves, so they can find more ways to add value. Listening and observing are the key skills. The book's hint: as you collaborate, ask other team members which gaps you could fill and how you could contribute more effectively.

The drawing on this page is two figures talking through a tin-can telephone: feedback only works if it travels quickly between people.

Build a foundation of core practices

Some practices have proven effective at building quality into the product:

  • Continuous integration. Every team needs it to deliver at a steady, frequent pace over time. Each time someone commits a change to the source code control repository (the shared store of the code), a build should start. It merges all code changes and checks them with automated tests. Every team also has a deployment pipeline that creates a release candidate and deploys it to a test or production environment, even if some stages are manual.
  • Test environments. Many teams still struggle to get reliable test environments that look as much like production as possible and let them control which build version is deployed. Today's cloud infrastructure gives more options: temporary environments to test one specific build, and automatic deployment of new versions to permanent test environments.
  • Manage technical debt. Technical debt is like credit card debt: it keeps growing if you pay only the minimum or part of the interest. It grows when the team does not take time to refactor code (tidy its internal structure without changing behavior), write enough automated regression tests, upgrade frameworks and look after other infrastructure. Over time, even the smallest code change becomes a big risk and needs lots of manual testing. So invest time in managing the debt in both the code and the automated tests.
  • Small, frequent changes are usually less risky than large, rare ones. Less can go wrong, and failures are quick to diagnose. Teams that slice big feature ideas into small "learning releases" (early releases made to get feedback) and into even smaller stories are more likely to deliver what customers want, on time.
  • Coding and testing are part of one process. This is the core of the whole-team approach. Testing and coding happen together, from the feature idea to checking the feature in production.
  • Synergy between practices. The gain comes from doing the practices together. Test-driven development, collective code ownership and continuous integration give consistency and fast feedback. Refactoring depends on automated regression tests. Agile practices are tried and true, and designed to be done all together.
Example (added here)

A team refactors a pricing module every sprint and keeps its regression suite green. A second team skips both for six months. When a tax rule changes, the first team edits one class and ships the same day. The second team has to retest checkout by hand for three days, because nobody trusts what else the change might break. That is technical debt compounding like unpaid interest.

Key idea

The practices are not a menu. Agile practices support each other and are designed to be done all together. For instance, you cannot refactor safely without automated regression tests.

Collaborate with customers

Testers speak both the domain language of business stakeholders and the technical language of the delivery team. They help get the right people together when a conversation is needed about how a feature should behave or how a design should look. They can help product owners put the business rules for each story into words and illustrate them with concrete examples. This is one of the most valuable things testers do on a team.

Look at the big picture

Agile teams focus on small changes and thin slices of features, but they must also keep the big picture in mind. Testers are good at spotting which parts of the system a small change might affect, and they have the customer's point of view.

The agile testing quadrants model helps the team keep the big picture in view while planning testing. Exploratory testing can uncover unexpected consequences of a new capability. There are also good ways to analyze how customers use the product. All of this keeps the team focused on delivering the right value.

Confidence-building practices

In their second book, More Agile Testing, the authors named some core testing practices that give teams the confidence to release changes to production often. They matter even more as teams move toward continuous delivery (software is always ready to release) or continuous deployment (every passing change goes to production automatically).

Use examples

Concrete examples of how a capability should behave help everyone understand the business rules. They can be turned into tests that guide development, and automated so the team knows when a story or feature is done. Those automated tests join the regression suites, which give quick feedback on whether a new change has disturbed existing behavior. Examples keep teams on track. (The drawing here is a person with a thought bubble holding a puzzle piece, the idea that each example is a piece of the picture.)

Exploratory testing

Automating regression tests leaves more time for exploratory testing, one of the best ways to find the "unknown unknowns" that could cause serious production failures. Programmers can learn to explore each story before they call it finished, which is another fast feedback loop. Everyone on the team can learn exploratory skills. They will find unexpected problems and also missing capabilities, which feed new feature ideas.

Feature testing

You must test at all levels of detail. Because agile teams focus on the story, they must remember to test at the feature level too. A key part is working out what the feature really needs to include. Testers can help find what customers value by asking what the business should drop in order to deliver other highly valuable features.

Continual learning

Team success depends on psychological safety (feeling safe to speak up and make mistakes), trust and time to learn. The team works together to name the biggest obstacle to the quality it wants. It might be too little test automation, feedback that takes too long, or building features nobody wanted. Then it designs small experiments to start overcoming that obstacle. Testers help by passing on testing skills, and others help testers grow their T-shaped skills (deep in one area, with working knowledge across many) so they can contribute in more ways. The drawing shows a bee reading a book at a desk.

Context sensitivity

Every team works in a unique context: company size, business domain and its regulations, technology, infrastructure needs, and more. Do not adopt a tool or practice just because Google or Facebook does. Use what fits your context. (The drawing is a railway track that splits into different routes.)

Keep it real

Testers are good at giving feedback, and bad news is hard to deliver. Still, stay grounded in reality. If a change is risky and the team has not reduced the risk with testing and other activities, business stakeholders need to know. Testers can act as consultants, helping everyone improve their testing skills and making quality concerns visible to the business. When testing becomes a bottleneck, make it visible and make it a team problem to solve. Glossing over problems to keep executives happy backfires, because they will not be happy when customers feel pain.

Team morale also depends on knowing the team can say no. Two examples from the book: "No, we can't take in any more stories," and "No, we can't add a new story unless you remove one of the others."

Paths to success

Many testers and teams feel stressed, especially those still moving to agile values, principles and practices. Management may not yet have worked out how its own role must change, and may demand more frequent deliveries or set unrealistic deadlines. The testing still has to happen, and too often testers still carry all of the responsibility for it. It would be nice if a silver bullet tool existed, but it does not.

Turning testing problems into problems for the whole delivery team is vital for learning to build quality in and for lasting success. The success factors and confidence-building practices give the team a framework for choosing its next step on the path to improvement.

In the authors' experience it takes years for a team to reach the performance and quality it wants. They suggest an eighth success factor: patience. Frequent retrospectives (team meetings to review how work went), at least one a week for new teams, help find the biggest quality problem and design a small experiment against it. The mix of skills in a cross-functional team makes those problems much easier to solve.

An example

Example

The product owner rejects a high share of the stories the team delivers. The constant rework, sometimes days after the team thought a story was finished, slows them down. Cycle time (the time from starting a story to deploying it to production) is much longer than they want.

Which success factor helps? The whole-team approach: get the whole team, or a group representing all roles, together to discuss it. Which confidence-building practice helps? A rejected story usually means the behavior was not what the product owner wanted, so the team misunderstood the requirements. The team is continually learning, and one tester has just learned about example mapping (a way to build shared understanding of a story using examples). They decide to try it. Their hypothesis: example mapping will cut the story rejection rate by 20% over the next two weeks, giving a 10% saving in average cycle time.

They measured and experimented. The experiment succeeded and beat both goals: within two months, rejection rate and cycle time were each reduced by 50%. Example mapping brought extra benefits too, because it helped specify scenarios that guide development as behavior-driven development tests. Had the experiment failed, the team would have designed another one, guided by the same success factors and practices.

When the authors' own teams feel stuck, they fall back on the key success factors and confidence-building practices, together with the ten principles for agile testing from Chapter 1, to plan the next step. These guide them along their learning journey toward getting small, valuable changes to customers frequently and sustainably. The book closes by thanking the reader and wishing them success on their own journey. Its last drawing shows a dragon and a donkey holding shovels in a garden.

Test-driven development (TDD)The programmer writes and automates a small unit test that fails first, then writes the least code needed to make it pass, then refactors to an acceptable standard. The code grows one test at a time. TDD, also called test-driven design, is more a way of designing code than a testing activity, and it yields robust, maintainable code.
