A plain-English guide

The 10 checks of a good explanation

A BBC journalist runs every explanation past ten questions. Here they are, with examples, in language you can use today.

The goal, in two lines:

Give people the information they need. Give it in a way that is easy to take in.

  4. Efficiency

1Simplicity

Short words in short sentences give the reader the fewest obstacles to understanding. Every hard word or unneeded fact is a small wall in the way.

Simple does not mean short. It means clear.

Example

Hard: The organisation underwent a comprehensive restructuring of its operational framework.

Simple: The company changed how its teams work.

Ask yourselfIs this the simplest way I can say this?

2Essential detail

Details are your currency. They are the facts people came for. But only the details they need. Every extra interesting fact makes the important ones harder to find.

Example

A plumber should say: "The pipe is cracked. It needs replacing. That costs about £200."

They should not give a history of pipe materials.

Ask yourselfWhat detail is essential to this explanation?

3Complexity

Do not dodge the hard parts. If you do not understand something, you cannot explain it well. And if you feel the urge to skip a topic, that is a clue you are not ready to explain it.

Even if you end up not using what you learned, it makes you more confident in what you do say.

Example

During the Greek debt crisis, the author had to explain complex economics on live TV with no notes. He made three lists: what he understood, what he did not, and what he would discover later. He asked a colleague to explain each part, then repeated it back in simple words until it was right.

Ask yourselfAre there elements of this subject I don't understand?

4Efficiency

Give people as much useful information as you can for the time they spend. Steve Jobs once dropped an iPod prototype into a fish tank. Air bubbles rose. His point: "There's space in there. Make it smaller." Most explanations have air bubbles too.

Efficient does not mean brief. It means nothing is wasted.

Example

The author watched a TV report on an election in Cornwall. He rewound it and watched twice, but his notepad stayed empty. Lots of scenery, almost no facts or analysis. That is an inefficient report.

The fix is to "tighten" in several passes. Each time, look for words or facts that can go or be shortened.

Ask yourselfIs this the most succinct way I can say this?

5Precision

Choose the exact words that say what you mean. First work out what you want to say. Then find the right words. Vague thinking produces vague sentences.

Example

Vague: Sales were kind of up recently.

Precise: Sales rose 12% in March.

The author points to Joni Mitchell's album Blue. The music is spare, and the emotion is precise. When your content is strong, the words around it can be spare too.

Ask yourselfAm I saying exactly what I want to communicate?

6Context

Tell people why this matters and what led to it. Without context, people either do not care or do not understand.

Examples

At the dentist, it matters that your tooth has hurt for over a week.

In an interview, your sales figures mean more when you say what they were before you took over.

When asking for a team change, it matters that the structure has not changed in ten years.

When a politician was assassinated in Pakistan, a BBC colleague spent under a minute on the attack and the rest on background. Listeners around the world stayed glued, because he explained why it mattered.

Phrases that help: "The reason this matters is…", "To understand this, remember that…", "This is not happening in isolation."

Ask yourselfWhy does this matter to the people I'm addressing?

7No distractions

Anything that pulls attention away from your point hurts it. There are two kinds.

Words people don't know

"Sam has been angry ever since the project review."

Who is Sam? What review? The listener stops to wonder, and misses what you say next.

The NATO rule: if some people might not know a name or term, explain it or cut it. To decide, sort your words into four groups:

Support wordsthe, of, then. No effort.
Known wordstable, election. Easy.
Partly knowninflation. Some won't know. Explain or cut.
Unknownproroguing. Most won't know. Explain or cut.

For partly known words, try: "As some of you may know…". It respects those who know and helps those who don't.

Visual distractions

Only show a picture, chart or slide at the moment you talk about it. A slide crammed with text makes people read instead of listen. The author once prepared 60 slides for a 10-minute talk, with each shown only when needed.

Ask yourselfAre there verbal, written or visual distractions?

8Engaging

If people stop listening, you have explained nothing. The author uses the dial test. Imagine a dial that turns up when listeners are interested and down when they drift. Where would it drop?

Example

An editor felt one middle section of a video about Afghanistan was losing momentum. They cut and reordered it. The details removed were interesting, not essential. The video passed a million views.

Lose people in one section and they may not come back for the next.

Ask yourselfAre there moments when attention could waver?

9Useful

Before you explain, list the questions your audience is likely to have. Then answer them.

Example

During the Australian bushfires, the team made videos around what people were asking: Was arson responsible? Did a lack of hazard-reduction burning matter? Did the coal industry affect the response? Each video answered one real question, and they were widely shared.

Ask yourselfHave I answered the questions that people have?

10Clarity of purpose

Know your main point before you start. Then test every sentence: does it support that point? If not, cut it.

Examples

Essay: the exact question decides what goes in.

Budget briefing: include only what explains the budget and its effects.

Charity event: everything should move people toward donating.

The author once DJed a set of great tracks that did not fit together, so the crowd drifted. Good ingredients, no direction. Explanations fail the same way.

Ask yourselfAbove all else, what am I trying to explain?

An easy way to remember them:

1 to 5: what you say. Clear, essential, tight and accurate.
6 to 7: making it land. Give context and remove confusion.
8 to 10: the audience and the goal. Keep them listening, answer their questions, stay on point.

At first, ask the ten questions on purpose. In time, they become habit.
