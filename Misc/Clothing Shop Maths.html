01

Why maths decides if the shop survives

A shop can be full of customers and still lose money. This happens when the selling price is too close to what you paid, when you give discounts without checking the profit, or when you forget costs like rent, electricity and a helper's salary.

A shop can also be quiet and still earn well, if every sale carries enough profit. The difference is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is not hard. It uses addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and one idea called percentage.

Why this mattersYou will make hundreds of small decisions: what to buy, what to charge, how low to go when someone bargains. If you decide by feeling, you will sometimes sell below cost without knowing. If you decide by numbers, you will always know what each sale earned.

This guide has four calculators. Use them as you read. By the end you will be able to do most of it in your head.

03

Percentages from zero

The word percent means out of 100. So 20% means 20 out of every 100.

If something costs ₹100 and you take 20%, you take ₹20. If it costs ₹500, there are five hundreds in it, so you take 5 × ₹20 = ₹100. That is all a percentage is: a fraction with 100 at the bottom.

The one formula Percent of a number = number × percent ÷ 100
20% of ₹500 = 500 × 20 ÷ 100 = ₹100

Quick tricks to do it in your head

  • 10%: move the last digit away. 10% of ₹640 is ₹64.
  • 5%: half of 10%. 5% of ₹640 is ₹32.
  • 20%: double 10%. 20% of ₹640 is ₹128.
  • 25%: divide by 4. 25% of ₹640 is ₹160.
  • 50%: divide by 2.
  • 15%: 10% plus 5%. 15% of ₹640 is ₹64 + ₹32 = ₹96.

Going the other way: what percent is this?

A customer is given ₹50 off a ₹500 shirt. What percent discount is that?

Percent = part ÷ whole × 100
50 ÷ 500 × 100 = 10%
The most important habit

Every percentage is a percentage of something. Before you use one, ask: percent of what? That "what" is called the base.

Discount % uses the tag price as the base. Margin % uses the selling price. Markup % uses the cost. Different bases give different numbers for the same rupees. Most mistakes in shops come from forgetting this.

04

Profit: margin and markup

Profit on one piece is simple:

Profit = Selling Price − Landed Cost

Now take a shirt that cost you ₹330 landed and sells for ₹440. Profit is ₹110. Two different percentages describe it.

Same shirt, two percentages
Landed cost₹330
Selling price₹440
Profit₹110
Markup = 110 ÷ 330 × 100 (base: cost)33.3%
Margin = 110 ÷ 440 × 100 (base: selling price)25%
Margin % = (SP − Cost) ÷ SP × 100
Markup % = (SP − Cost) ÷ Cost × 100

Price from a target SP from target margin: SP = Cost ÷ (1 − margin)
SP from target markup: SP = Cost × (1 + markup)

Check the first one: cost ₹330, target margin 25% (which is 0.25). SP = 330 ÷ 0.75 = ₹440. Correct.

Use margin

Margin tells you how much of each ₹100 a customer hands over is yours before shop costs. A 25% margin means ₹25 of every ₹100. Discounts, bargaining and the customer's wallet all work on the selling price, so margin follows what is really happening at the counter. Use margin for your decisions.

Common trap

A supplier or friend says "keep 30% profit". Ask which one. 30% markup on ₹330 gives ₹429. 30% margin gives ₹471. That is ₹42 difference on one shirt, and over 500 shirts it is ₹21,000.

05

What one shirt really costs you

The price on the wholesaler's bill is not your cost. Suppose you buy 40 shirts at ₹300 each from a wholesale market, for example Fancy Bazar in Guwahati. Then you pay to bring them home.

Buying 40 shirts
40 shirts × ₹300₹12,000
Transport (bus or truck)₹800
Loading and unloading₹200
Polythene, tags, hangers₹200
Total spent₹13,200
Per shirt (₹13,200 ÷ 40)₹330

Now add one more truth. Some pieces get damaged, come with a stitching fault, or stay unsold forever. Suppose 2 of the 40 never sell. You spent ₹13,200 but only 38 pieces will pay you back.

Landed cost per sellable piece = total spent ÷ pieces you can sell
13,200 ÷ 38 = ₹347

That ₹347 is your real cost. Every price you set must start from it, not from ₹300.

Why this mattersIf you price from ₹300, you think you earn ₹140 on a ₹440 shirt. Starting from ₹347 you actually earn ₹93. The first number is a pleasant story. The second is the one that pays your rent.

In the calculators below, "damaged or unsold allowance" does this step for you. Start with 3% to 5% and adjust with experience.

06

Setting a price when customers bargain

In Goreswar you know the customer will ask for less. So the number you say first, the tag price, has to be high enough to leave room for the bargain and still give you profit at the lowest price you will accept.

Work backwards in three steps.

  1. Landed cost. From chapter 5. Example: ₹347.
  2. Floor price. The lowest price you accept. Choose a minimum margin you are happy with at the floor, say 20%. Floor = Cost ÷ (1 − 0.20) = 347 ÷ 0.80 = ₹434.
  3. Tag price. Decide the largest bargain you will allow, say 20% off the tag. Tag = Floor ÷ (1 − 0.20) = 434 ÷ 0.80 = ₹543. Round up to a friendly price like ₹549.
Floor = Cost ÷ (1 − minimum margin)
Tag = Floor ÷ (1 − biggest bargain)
Together Tag = Cost ÷ [(1 − minimum margin) × (1 − biggest bargain)]

At tag ₹549, a 20% bargain takes the price to ₹439. That is above your ₹434 floor, so you still earn about 21% margin. If the customer pays the full ₹549, your margin is about 37%. You do not need every customer to bargain. The ones who pay full price make up for the ones who push hard.

Rules for bargaining without losing money

  • Know your floor before opening the shop each day. Write it on the back of the tag in a code only you and your helper understand. Then nobody has to calculate while a customer is waiting.
  • Come down in small steps. From ₹549 go to ₹520, then ₹499, then ₹470. Small steps show the customer you are really moving, and they take longer to reach your floor.
  • Give a reason with each drop. "Because you are taking two" or "because it is your first purchase". The customer feels they earned it.
  • Sell more pieces, not a lower price. "Two shirts for ₹950" lowers the price per piece a little but doubles the profit rupees from one customer. Check the floor on each piece first.
  • It is fine to say no. Below your floor, smile and let them walk. Another customer will pay properly, and the one who left often comes back.
  • Let your helper bargain only inside the floor. Give them a written floor for each item type and a small reward for selling above it.
Why this mattersYour bargaining limit is a business rule, not a feeling. When the rule is set in advance, you lose no sleep over any individual sale.
08

Discounts and offers: the trap

Discounts look small and cost a lot. Take a shirt with landed cost ₹70 sold at ₹100. Margin is 30%, so you earn ₹30 per shirt. Now give 10% off: the price drops to ₹90 and the cost is still ₹70, so profit falls to ₹20.

A 10% discount cut your profit by one third, because profit is the small piece left after cost, and the discount comes out of that small piece.

If margin is m and discount is d (both as decimals) New margin = (m − d) ÷ (1 − d)
Sales needed to earn the same total profit = m ÷ (m − d) × your old sales

Here m = 0.30 and d = 0.10. New margin = 0.20 ÷ 0.90 = 22%. Sales needed = 0.30 ÷ 0.20 = 1.5 times. You must sell 50% more shirts just to earn what you earned before the discount.

Two discounts in a row do not add up

"10% off, and another 5% off on the bill" is not 15% off. The second discount applies to the already reduced price. On ₹1,000: 10% off gives ₹900, then 5% of ₹900 is ₹45, so you pay ₹855. That is 14.5% off in total. Smaller than 15%, which is good for you, but know the real number.

Safer ways to give value
  • Offer a bundle: "2 for ₹950". The customer feels a deal and you move two pieces.
  • Add something cheap that costs you little: free alteration, a pair of socks that cost you ₹15.
  • Run clearance on old stock only, not on new arrivals (chapter 10).
  • Never advertise "flat 50% off" unless you have checked that your tag price allows it. Use calculator 1 first.
09

Break-even: how much must you sell

Profit per shirt is not your profit. From the money made on shirts you must pay the shop's fixed monthly costs. Only what remains is yours.

Break-even is the amount of sales where you have paid every fixed cost and earned exactly zero. Below it you lose money. Above it you earn.

Break-even sales per month = fixed costs per month ÷ average margin
Example: costs ₹30,000, average margin 28% → 30,000 ÷ 0.28 = ₹1,07,143

That is about ₹4,121 of sales on each of 26 working days, only to reach zero. Everything above that is profit. If this number feels scary, good. It is far better to know it now than in month three.

Include your own pay in the costs. If you do not, the shop looks profitable while you live on nothing. Also include loan interest if you borrowed to start.

10

Stock, cash and dead stock

Stock is money you have turned into cloth. Until it sells, you cannot use that money for rent, salary or new buying. A shop with ₹2 lakh of stock and ₹500 in the drawer is in trouble even if it is "worth" a lot.

How fast stock sells

Count how many pieces you sold this month and divide by pieces on hand at month end.

Months to sell the stock = pieces in stock ÷ pieces sold per month
300 pieces in stock, 100 sold per month → 3 months

If one type of item takes 6 months or more to sell, buy less of it next time. If another sells out in two weeks, buy more and buy earlier.

A markdown plan for slow pieces

Decide in advance how long a piece may stay before its price drops. For example (adjust to your own numbers):

  • Months 1 to 2: full tag price with normal bargaining.
  • Month 3: lower the tag by about 15% to 20%.
  • Month 4: clear it at close to landed cost and put the money into something that sells.

Selling an old shirt at cost is better than keeping it forever, because the cash can buy a new shirt that earns profit.

Buy for your customers and the calendar

Demand in Assam moves with festivals and seasons: Bohag Bihu, Magh Bihu, Durga Puja, Eid, Diwali, Christmas, wedding months, school reopening and the winter months. Start buying a few weeks ahead for each. Plan sizes too: a store with only medium and large will lose the shoppers who need small and extra large.

Keep a cash reserve

A common safety rule is to hold several months of fixed costs in cash outside your stock, so a slow month does not force you to sell below cost. Aim for at least two to three months if you can.

Be careful with credit (udhar)

If a customer pays later, your profit is on paper but your cash is stuck. Give credit only to people you know well, set a limit, and write every amount in a notebook with the date. Do not give credit on thin-margin items.

11

GST and billing, briefly

GST is a tax added to what the customer pays. You collect it and pass it to the government. It is not your profit and it is not your cost, so never mix it into your margin calculation.

As of the rate changes that took effect on 22 September 2025, readymade garments are reported as taxed at 5% when the price per piece is up to ₹2,500, and 18% above that (CAclubindia report). Rates and thresholds change, so confirm the current rule before you print your first bill.

If your tag price already includes 5% GST Price before tax = price with GST ÷ 1.05
₹549 ÷ 1.05 = ₹523 before tax, ₹26 is GST.

Decide now whether your tag prices include GST. Your margin should be worked out on the price before GST, because the tax goes to the government. Whether you must register depends on your yearly sales and your state. Ask a local chartered accountant (CA) in your first week. A short visit costs little and prevents mistakes that cost a lot.

12

Competing with M Baazar and the tiny shops

You have a larger shop to compete against and smaller ones beside you. The maths helps you choose where to compete. These are suggestions to test, not guarantees.

Do a price survey in week one

Walk into M Baazar and two of the tiny shops. For 20 popular items (basic T-shirt, formal shirt, jeans, kurta, nightwear, school wear, saree, innerwear), note the tag price and the price they come down to when you bargain. Put it in a notebook. Then compare with your own landed cost for similar pieces.

  • If their price is below your cost for an item, do not copy it. Do not stock that item, or stock it only to bring people in.
  • If their price leaves you a healthy margin, you can match it and compete on service.
  • If an item has no good version nearby, you have found a gap to fill.

Where a smaller shop can win

  • Range and sizes. A tiny shop cannot hold every size. You can. Customers who cannot find their size elsewhere will pay your price.
  • Specific customers. Pick one or two groups to serve well: school and college students, young men's casual wear, women's kurtis and ethnic wear, festival and wedding wear. Doing a few things well beats doing everything average.
  • Quick service. Alterations, easy exchanges, a friendly trial room, a clean shop. These cost little and customers talk about them.
  • Trust. If your quality is steady and your prices are fair, regular customers stop comparing every shirt.
  • Value you can explain. If your cloth is better, say why: the stitching, the fabric, the wash. Reasons let you hold a higher price.
Avoid a price war

M Baazar can probably buy in larger quantity and accept lower margins. A price war makes both shops poorer and the bigger one lasts longer. Use calculator 4 to find your break-even, then compete where you can still reach it.

Fixed price or bargaining?

Bargaining wins customers who like the game and lets you earn more from those who pay readily. A clearly printed fixed price saves time and builds trust, and suits busy buyers and anyone who dislikes bargaining. You can mix: bargain on some items and put "fixed price" on basics. Whichever you choose, set the price with the method in chapter 6.

13

Practice problems

Try each one on paper first. Then open the answer. Use the formulas from this guide.

1. A shirt has landed cost ₹360 and sells for ₹480. Find profit, margin and markup.

Profit = 480 − 360 = ₹120.

Margin = 120 ÷ 480 × 100 = 25%.

Markup = 120 ÷ 360 × 100 = 33.3%.

2. Landed cost is ₹350. You want a 30% margin. What selling price?

SP = 350 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = 350 ÷ 0.70 = ₹500. Check: profit ₹150, and 150 ÷ 500 = 30%.

3. Tag ₹799. The customer pays ₹650. What discount percent did they get?

Discount = 799 − 650 = ₹149. 149 ÷ 799 × 100 = 18.6%.

4. Landed cost ₹400. What is the lowest price that still gives 15% margin?

Floor = 400 ÷ 0.85 = 470.6, so ₹471. Anything lower gives less than 15%.

5. Jeans: landed cost ₹580, tag ₹1,299. A customer offers ₹900. What are your profit and margin? Would you accept if your minimum margin is 25%?

Profit = 900 − 580 = ₹320. Margin = 320 ÷ 900 × 100 = 35.6%. Yes, accept. It is above 25%.

6. Landed cost ₹250. Minimum margin at floor 20%, biggest bargain 15%. What tag price?

Floor = 250 ÷ 0.80 = ₹312.5. Tag = 312.5 ÷ 0.85 = 367.6. Round up to ₹369 or ₹379.

7. You give 10% off, then an extra 5% off, on ₹1,000. What does the customer pay?

After 10%: ₹900. 5% of 900 = ₹45. Pays ₹855, which is 14.5% off, not 15%.

8. Margin is 25%. You give a 10% discount. How many times your normal sales do you need to earn the same profit?

m ÷ (m − d) = 0.25 ÷ 0.15 = 1.67 times. You must sell about two-thirds more.

9. Fixed costs are ₹30,000 a month. Average margin is 28%. What monthly sales to break even, and per day over 26 days?

30,000 ÷ 0.28 = ₹1,07,143 a month. ÷ 26 = ₹4,121 a day.

10. You buy 50 kurtis at ₹400 each. Transport and packing come to ₹1,500. You expect 3 to stay unsold. What is the landed cost per sellable kurti?

Total = 50 × 400 + 1,500 = ₹21,500. Sellable = 47. 21,500 ÷ 47 = ₹457.

Seven habits of a shop that survives
  1. Price from landed cost, never from the bill price alone.
  2. Fix your floor price before the customer arrives.
  3. Always ask "percent of what?"
  4. Check the profit before any discount.
  5. Know your daily break-even number.
  6. Do not let stock sit. Mark down on schedule.
  7. Write everything down: purchases, sales, credit.

Keep a notebook for the first three months. Write each day's sales, the number of pieces sold and what you bought. After a month, update the margin and break-even numbers in calculators 1 and 4 with your real figures. That is how the maths becomes your own.
