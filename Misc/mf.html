Chapter One

Reading the fact sheet: 3 key metrics

Everyone looks at just the returns of a mutual fund. That's a mistake. A fund's "fact sheet" is like its report card, and it tells you a lot more than the return number — mainly, how it got that return.

Analogy

Two bikers can reach Ladakh — one via a smooth road through Kashmir, one via a rocky, accident-prone road through Manali. Both arrive, but one had a much bumpier ride. A fund's volatility is that bumpy road.

1. Standard Deviation (how bumpy the ride is)

This tells you how much a fund's returns swing away from its average. Example from the video: a fund with 17.59% 3-year return and a standard deviation of 14.78% means you can expect that return to swing roughly ±14.78% going forward. A very high number (like 40%) means a wild, unpredictable ride — not necessarily a good thing.

2. Beta (how sensitive it is to the market)

Beta compares your fund to a benchmark index (like Nifty 50). A beta of 1.05 means if the index moves 10%, your fund moves 10.5%. A beta of 0.95 means it only moves 9.5%. Higher beta = more aggressive, more risk, but potentially more reward. There's no "correct" beta — it depends on how much risk you're okay with.

3. Sharpe Ratio (reward for the risk taken)

This checks how much extra return a fund earned over a safe government bond, relative to the risk (standard deviation) it took. A higher Sharpe ratio means the fund manager got more reward per unit of risk — so even if two funds have similar returns, the one with the higher Sharpe ratio managed risk better.

📌 Key takeaway

All these numbers are based on the past — they don't predict the future. Don't fixate on one number; look at a range of metrics together, and compare funds only within the same category (e.g. flexi-cap vs. flexi-cap).

Chapter Two

Qualitative factors that matter

Beyond the numbers, funds also differ in investing style — and in how disciplined you are as an investor.

Funds are often mapped on two axes: company size (large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap) and investing style (value vs. growth). A value approach bets on undervalued companies that the market will eventually price correctly — this can take time. A growth approach bets on companies already growing fast, which is more aggressive.

"More than 50% of people who start an SIP never continue it — as soon as the market dips, they exit."

The video argues this is the single most important qualitative factor: staying invested through market dips instead of panic-exiting your SIP (a Systematic Investment Plan — basically auto-investing a fixed amount every month).

Chapter Three

Common mistakes to avoid

Three mistakes almost every investor makes, according to the video:

1
Comparing incomparable funds Don't compare a sectoral/defense fund to a large-cap fund, or small-cap to large-cap. Only compare funds within the same category, against the right benchmark.
2
Fake diversification (overlap) Owning 5 funds means nothing if they all hold the same big stocks (e.g. all loaded with HDFC Bank). Check for stock overlap and diversify across asset types and market caps, not just fund count.
3
Overthinking and over-monitoring Constantly checking whether your fund beat the index adds stress, not returns. Pick 2-3 solid funds (active or index), automate your SIPs, and let compounding work quietly over years.

Closing

The less you overthink it, the more you make.

Pick funds by comparing the right metrics and categories, avoid overlap, stay invested through the dips, and don't obsess over daily returns.
