The Gym Trick I Forgot to Use on My Business

Chapter One

The gym worked. The business didn't. Why?

I wasn't serious about the gym either — until I actually saw a change. A bit of shape, a bit of muscle. Nothing impressive, but enough that I could feel it, and enough that it beat most people just showing up and going through the motions. That one bit of proof was enough to keep me showing up, every day, for years.

Then life happened — a year off for marriage — and I lost the shape. Now I can barely manage the gym once or twice a week. But here's the part that actually bothered me: it's not really about the gym. It's the same story everywhere else in my life. I quit an Amazon and Meesho reselling business in a month because nothing sold. I keep starting apps and side projects I imagine publishing on the App Store, and I never finish a single one.

My instinct was to blame myself — not disciplined enough, not passionate enough, not cut out for business. But when I actually laid it next to the gym story, a different explanation showed up.

💬 What I brought to the conversation

"I think I have to copy some already existing product and sell it cheaper. That way at least I'll finish it, because someone else has already proven it's possible. But then I fear I won't be able to defend it — people will say 'this already exists.'"

Chapter Two

It's not discipline — it's a broken loop

Here's the reframe that changed how I see this whole problem: it isn't a discipline problem, it's a feedback loop problem. Some people can grind for years with no external sign that it's working. I can't — and pretending otherwise was the real reason I kept quitting.

💡 What's a "feedback loop"?

It just means: you do something, the world shows you a result, and that result changes what you do next. A thermostat is a feedback loop — it checks the temperature and adjusts the heater. Your motivation runs on one too: action, then evidence, then a decision to keep going or stop.

My Actual Motivation Loop

In the gym, this loop closes in weeks — a mirror gives you a "result" for free. In business, nothing closes the loop unless you deliberately build one.

The gym gave me this loop automatically, for free: I worked out, my body changed, I saw it in the mirror, other people noticed. Business and side projects never gave me that. I'd build something, get no users, get no sale, and the loop simply never closed. No result means no confidence, which means no motivation, which means the project quietly dies.

That's the actual diagnosis: I don't lack discipline. I lack a project that hands me small, frequent proof the way a mirror does. So the real question isn't "how do I try harder" — it's "how do I design a project that gives me a mirror."

Chapter Three

Copying an app isn't cheating (if you do it right)

My instinct — "just copy something that already exists and sell it cheaper" — turned out to be a genuinely reasonable idea. Not as "steal the market with a cheap clone," but as a shortcut around the scariest part of building anything: not knowing if anyone even wants it.

Existing product → proven problem → proven demand → smaller version → launch → real users → improve.versus

Compare that to the model I kept unconsciously choosing instead: "I have a brilliant idea nobody's thought of, spend six months building it, and hope someone wants it." That second model asks you to stay motivated for months with zero outside evidence. For someone whose motivation runs on visible proof, that's close to designing your own failure.

But there was a real catch in my own worry, and it turned out to matter more than the "copying" part: don't pick a product just because it has a lot of downloads. Pick one where you can answer a much smaller, more personal question first.

🎯 The one question that changes everything

"Why would I personally care about making this better?" Not "is this popular," not "will this make money" — just, do you personally feel the itch this product doesn't scratch?

❌ Defending a random clone

"Why should I use yours instead of QuickBooks?" — and you have no real answer, because you picked it for the download count, not because you use it or care about it. You'll feel embarrassed marketing something you don't believe in.

✅ Defending your own itch

"I use this myself, and three things about it annoy me — so I built the version that fixes them for people like me." That's not defending a clone. That's telling a true story, and true stories are easy to say out loud.

And the "someone will say it already exists" fear? Nearly every useful product already exists in some form. Google wasn't the first search engine. Facebook wasn't the first social network. Zoom wasn't the first video call app. A product doesn't need to be new — it needs to be useful to someone, for a reason you can actually explain.

Chapter Four

Redefine "success": the ladder of evidence

Here's where I'd been quietly sabotaging myself without realizing it. My definition of "success" for any project was: money, users, downloads. That's a terrible target for someone like me, because that kind of feedback arrives too late — often months after the effort, if it arrives at all.

The fix is to stop treating success as one giant finish line, and start treating it as a ladder — a series of smaller "wins" that each count as real proof, long before any money shows up.

1
I built itThe thing exists outside my head now.
2
I deployed it publiclyIt has a link. Anyone could open it.
3
A total stranger used itNot me, not a friend I begged. A real outsider.
4
10 people used itOne wasn't a fluke. Ten is a pattern.
5
Someone came backThey opened it a second time, on their own.
6
Someone asked for a featureThey care enough to want it better.
7
Someone paidThe furthest rung — and now, not the only one that counts.

Look at that list again and notice something: I hit six separate forms of real validation before a single rupee shows up. Compare that to the gym. I never went from "doesn't exercise" straight to "has an impressive physique" — there were dozens of tiny signals along the way. Weight moved. A muscle showed up. Clothes fit differently. Someone noticed. Those small signals are exactly what kept the loop alive for years. I just never built the equivalent ladder for anything else.

Chapter Five

Stop starting at the finish line

There was one sentence I said in the conversation that, once it was pointed back at me, explained almost everything: "I always think of developing something and publishing it on the App Store."

My brain was quietly running this sequence: idea → build → App Store → users → business. The App Store was the goal. But the App Store isn't a milestone at all — it's a distribution channel, and a fairly late one. The real first milestone, the one that's supposed to feel like seeing your first bit of muscle in the mirror, is much smaller than that.

🏁 The actual first finish line

Getting one real human being — a stranger, not a friend doing you a favor — to use something you made. That's it. Not a launch. Not a business. One person, one use.

The correct sequence looks a lot less impressive on paper, but it's the one that can actually survive contact with my own motivation pattern: problem → tiny solution → 1 user → feedback → improve → 10 users → improve → maybe, eventually, a business. Even my failed Amazon and Meesho attempt isn't proof I "can't do business" — it's proof of something narrower and much more fixable: I picked a product I didn't care about, and I stopped the moment the feedback (a single sale) didn't arrive fast enough. That's a project-selection problem, not a character flaw.

💡 What's an "MVP"?

Short for Minimum Viable Product — the smallest version of an idea that's still genuinely useful to someone. Not a rough sketch nobody could use, and not the "real" full-featured product either. Just enough to test whether the core idea is worth anything.

Chapter Six

How do you actually find the thing to build?

My first instinct was: search a category, try a few competing apps, and remix the features I personally like from each one. That turns out to be close, but backwards in one important way. Features aren't where you should start.

Don't ask "which features do I like?" Ask "which problem — and which person — do I actually want to understand?"

A five-step version of the process

1. Pick a problem area, not a product. Personal finance, fitness, job-hunting, developer tools — anywhere you already have a bit of natural curiosity.

2. Actually use 5–10 existing products for several days. Not screenshots, not reviews — live in them. What are they trying to help you do? What annoys you? What feels overbuilt? Who does this really seem designed for?

3. Look for the gap between them. Somewhere between five imperfect products is a shape none of them quite cover.

Example: testing five fitness apps
AppWhat you likeWhat you dislike
App AGreat workout trackingToo complicated
App BBeautiful designPoor progress tracking
App CExcellent statisticsExpensive
App DVery simpleNo nutrition features
App EStrong communityToo much social noise

Now the idea isn't "copy App A." It's something sharper: a simple fitness tracker for people who want workout and progress tracking without all the social complexity nobody asked for.

4. Pick one specific person, not a broad audience. "People who work out" is enormous and useless. "People who recently came back to the gym after a long break" is specific enough that you can actually list what they struggle with: they don't know what to follow, they've forgotten their old weights, they get demotivated fast because progress feels invisible. Notice something — that specific person is basically me, right now. Understanding them isn't research. It's memory.

5. Remix around one job, not a pile of random features. "App A's calendar plus App B's AI plus App C's chat plus App D's payments" is a Frankenstein product nobody asked for. "Help beginner gym-goers consistently follow a simple 3-day plan" is one job — and every borrowed feature has to serve that one job or it doesn't make the cut.

💡 What does "iteration" mean here?

Building a small version, watching how real people actually use it, then adjusting based on what you saw — instead of guessing every feature up front and building it all at once.

Chapter Seven

The product discovery scorecard

Once you've got a candidate idea, run it through five quick questions before committing weeks to it. You don't need a perfect score on all five — you're looking for a combination that feels interesting, buildable, and testable at the same time.

1
Existing demand
Are people already paying or using things like this?
2
Personal interest
Would you actually enjoy building and using it?
3
User clarity
Can you name one specific type of person for this?
4
Small MVP
Can a useful first version ship in a few weeks?
5
Distribution
Can you picture reaching your first 10–100 users?

💡 What's "distribution," quickly?

It's simply how the people you're building for actually find out the thing exists. A great product with zero distribution is a secret, not a business.

And one rule to add specifically for a motivation pattern like mine: never choose a project whose feedback takes six months to arrive. Skip "I'll build a whole platform and eventually someone will buy it." Prefer "I can ship this in two to four weeks and get real users." The goal of a first project isn't to find the business — it's to prove, once, that you personally can go from idea to a stranger's hands and back. Everything after that gets easier.

Chapter Eight

Your personal "project gym": 30 days

Take an existing product, shrink it to three features instead of fifty, and give yourself thirty days. No thirty days of learning new frameworks. No thirty days perfecting the architecture. No thirty days of tutorials. Just: build, deploy, get one person, then another, then another.

Day 1 — Idea selectedOne problem, one specific person, three must-have features.
Day 5 — First working versionUgly is fine. Working is not optional.
Day 10 — DeployedIt has a public link. Anyone could open it right now.
Day 15 — First real userA stranger, not a friend doing you a favor.
Day 20 — Five usersAsk every one of them: "what's the one thing you wish this had?"
Day 30 — Ten usersYour roadmap now comes from them, not from your imagination.

That scoreboard is the whole trick. It replaces the impossibly vague target of "build a successful startup" with a target your brain can actually see happening in real time — the exact same way it could see a shoulder slowly filling out a t-shirt.

Chapter Nine

Starting a YouTube channel: same playbook, different medium

Everything so far applies almost word for word to starting a YouTube channel — because a channel is just another feedback-loop machine, and honestly a faster one than most apps. Views, comments, and subscribers are a mirror that updates within hours, not months.

💡 Quick terms

Niche — the specific topic and audience your channel is known for. Algorithm — YouTube's system that decides who gets shown your video, based on how well it holds attention. Retention — how much of a video people actually watch before leaving.

Pick a niche the same way you'd pick a product

Don't start with "what should my channel be about?" Start with the same question from Chapter Six: which problem, and which person, do you actually understand? Then watch 5–10 existing channels in that space for real, the same way you'd trial 5–10 competing apps — what do they cover well, what do they skip, what feels missing for a specific kind of viewer?

1
Existing demand
Are other channels already getting views in this niche?
2
Personal interest
Could you talk about this for a year without getting bored?
3
Viewer clarity
Can you picture exactly who's watching?
4
Small MVP
Can you film and post something this week, on a phone?
5
Distribution
Does the topic lend itself to Shorts, search, or a trend?

Your ladder of evidence for a channel

Just like the product ladder in Chapter Four, don't wait for "monetized creator" to count as success. There are several real wins before that.

1
You uploaded a videoIt exists publicly. That alone beats most "someday" channels.
2
A stranger watched itNot a friend who felt obligated — someone who found you.
3
Someone left a commentThey cared enough to respond.
4
Someone subscribedThey want to see what you make next.
5
Someone watched several videosYou're becoming a habit, not a one-off.
6
Someone requested a topicYour content plan starts coming from viewers, not guesswork.
7
First revenue or brand dealThe furthest rung — arriving only after six real wins already happened.

🎥 Equipment is not the blocker

A phone, a quiet room, and decent light beat a fancy camera you're still researching. The feedback loop only starts once something is posted — every day spent comparing microphones is a day the loop stays closed.

Your first 30 days on a channel

Day 1 — Niche and viewer chosenOne topic, one specific person you're making this for.
Day 5 — First video scripted and filmedShort and rough beats long and unfinished.
Day 10 — First three videos postedOne video teaches you nothing. Three starts a pattern.
Day 15 — First non-friend viewShared in a relevant community or under a Short, not just to contacts.
Day 20 — First comment or subscriberRead it twice. This is your mirror.
Day 30 — Ten videos postedEnough data to see which topics actually held attention.

Notice the shape is identical to Chapter Eight's product plan — small, dated, visible checkpoints instead of one distant goal called "become a YouTuber." Post consistently, watch retention on each video like a scoreboard, and let the topics that hold attention tell you what to make more of. The channel that survives isn't the one with the best camera. It's the one that kept a feedback loop alive long enough to compound.

Chapter Ten

Build to get evidence, not to be right.

I don't need to find the perfect idea before I start, and I don't need to feel passionate about it on day one either. My own gym story already proves the opposite order works: the results made me interested, not the other way around. Progress creates interest. So the plan isn't "wait to care." It's "pick something small and specific enough to give me a mirror — and let the caring show up on its own."

— Back to day one. Let's see what thirty days of proof actually looks like.
