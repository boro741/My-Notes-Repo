TLDR · Retail maths for a new shop

Clothing Shop Maths, in five minutes

The full guide has 16 chapters, 5 calculators and a lot of doubts answered. This is the short version: what to know before you tag your first shirt.

Based on the guide written for a new shop in Goreswar, Assam. All rupee figures are examples. Use your own costs.

The whole guide in one line

Price from your landed cost, decide your floor price before the customer walks in, never discount without checking the profit, and know the sales you need each day to cover rent.

1. Your cost is not the bill price

The wholesaler's rate is only part of it. Add transport, loading, packing and tags. Then divide by the pieces you can actually sell, because some will be damaged or stay unsold.

Landed cost = total spent ÷ sellable pieces
  • 40 shirts at ₹300Bill price₹12,000
  • Transport, loading, packing₹800 + ₹200 + ₹200₹1,200
  • Total spent₹13,200
  • ÷ 38 sellable shirts2 of 40 will not sell₹347

Start every price from ₹347, not ₹300. Rent, salary and electricity are not in this number. They come in at step 5. Start with a 5% allowance for damaged or unsold pieces and adjust after a month or two.

2. Margin and markup are the same profit, divided differently

A shirt landed at ₹330 sells for ₹440. Profit is ₹110. The only question is what you divide it by.

Cost ₹330Profit ₹110

Markup: 110 ÷ 330 (cost) = 33%. Margin: 110 ÷ 440 (selling price) = 25%.

  • Margin answers "out of every ₹100 the customer pays, how much is mine?" Use it for floor price, bargaining, discounts and break-even.
  • Markup is what suppliers quote. Convert it before you decide anything: margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup). A 25% markup is a 20% margin.
  • Doubling your cost is a 100% markup but only a 50% margin.
Price from target margin: SP = cost ÷ (1 − margin)
₹350 cost, 30% margin → 350 ÷ 0.70 = ₹500
Trap

"Keep 30% profit" on a ₹330 shirt means ₹429 if it is markup and ₹471 if it is margin. That is ₹42 apart on every shirt. Always ask "30% of what?"

The same trap catches target margins. Adding 40% to cost is a 40% markup, which gives only 28.57% margin. To get a 40% margin on ₹1,000 cost you need ₹1,667, a 66.67% markup. Use this table at the counter:

20% margin25% markup → ₹1,250
25% margin33.33% markup → ₹1,333
30% margin42.86% markup → ₹1,429
33.33% margin50% markup → ₹1,500
40% margin66.67% markup → ₹1,667
50% margin100% markup → ₹2,000

In short: markup sets the tag (you know the cost, so it is easy to work forward), and margin judges the profit at month end, at the counter and in break-even. Margin is always the smaller number, and it can never reach 100%. Never tell the customer either number.

3. Price for bargaining: work backwards

Customers in a bargaining town will ask for less. So set the tag high enough that the lowest price you accept still earns your minimum margin.

Floor = cost ÷ (1 − minimum margin)
Tag = floor ÷ (1 − biggest bargain you allow)
  • Landed cost₹347
  • Floor price20% minimum margin: 347 ÷ 0.80₹434
  • Tag price20% bargain room: 434 ÷ 0.80 = ₹543, rounded up₹549

Now check any offer against the floor:

₹350₹3 profit · 0.8%
  • Write the floor on the tag in a private code. Then nobody calculates at the counter.
  • Come down in small steps, give a reason for each, and counter even a good first offer.
  • Sell more pieces instead of cutting price: "2 for ₹950". Check the floor on each piece first.
  • Walking away from a below-floor offer is fine. A sale near cost is a gift.

4. Discounts cost more than they look

A discount comes out of your profit, not out of your cost. At 30% margin, a 10% discount takes the margin to 22% and cuts profit per piece by a third. To earn the same total profit you must sell 50% more pieces.

New margin = (m − d) ÷ (1 − d)
Sales needed = m ÷ (m − d) × old sales
30% margin, 10% off1.5×sales to earn the same profit
25% margin, 10% off1.67×sales to earn the same profit
20% margin, 20% offNeverthe discount is your whole margin

Another example: jeans cost ₹1,000 and the tag is ₹1,600 (37.5% margin). The customer asks for ₹200 off and you agree. The customer got 12.5% off, but profit fell from ₹600 to ₹400, which is a third gone, and margin dropped to 28.57%. Always think of a discount as a cut in profit, not a cut in the tag.

  • Two discounts do not add. 10% then 5% off ₹1,000 is ₹855, which is 14.5% off, not 15%.
  • Prefer bundles or a small freebie. Clear only old stock, not new arrivals.
  • Do not advertise "Flat 30% off" before checking it. On the ₹549 shirt it leaves a 10% margin.

5. Rent comes out of your margin

Rent, salary, electricity, loan interest and your own pay are due even on a day with no customers. They are paid from the profit on clothes, so the margin decides how much you must sell.

Break-even sales per month = fixed costs ÷ average margin
₹30,000 ÷ 0.28 = ₹1,07,143 a month, about ₹4,121 a day over 26 days

Sales needed to cover ₹30,000 a month: bar = sales

20% margin₹1,50,000
28% margin₹1,07,143
40% margin₹75,000

Why a higher margin matters: say fixed costs are ₹60,000 a month (rent ₹20,000, salaries ₹25,000, electricity ₹5,000, other ₹10,000). Out of every ₹100 of sales at 20% margin, ₹80 goes to the supplier and only ₹20 is left to pay these. At 40% margin ₹40 is left.

20% margin₹3,00,000 · ₹10,000 a day

40% margin₹1,50,000 · ₹5,000 a day

In shirts: on a ₹1,500 shirt with ₹500 gross profit, ₹60,000 ÷ ₹500 = 120 shirts a month, about 4 a day, just to reach zero. To also earn ₹30,000 for yourself: sales needed = (expenses + desired profit) ÷ margin, so (60,000 + 30,000) ÷ 0.40 = ₹2,25,000. This is gross margin only. Tax and interest still come out.

Know three margins for each item:

  • Survival = fixed costs ÷ expected sales. Below this you lose money every month.
  • Target = (fixed costs + profit you want) ÷ expected sales.
  • Ceiling = (market price − your cost) ÷ market price. This is what nearby shops allow.

If the ceiling is below survival, the item cannot pay for itself. Renegotiate the cost or drop it. Use a slow month for expected sales, not Bihu or Puja. Include your own salary in fixed costs, and use the margin you get after bargaining.

6. Stock is cash sitting on a shelf

  • Months to sell = pieces in stock ÷ pieces sold per month. Six months or more means buy less next time.
  • Set a markdown plan in advance: full tag for months 1 to 2, about 15 to 20% off in month 3, near cost in month 4. Cash from an old shirt buys one that earns.
  • First order: about 4 to 6 weeks of expected sales. Buy variety (2 or 3 pieces of many designs), then reorder only winners. Keep missing sizes first on the list.
  • Keep 2 to 3 months of fixed costs as cash reserve. It is the item new owners forget most.
  • Track margin by item, not just overall. A fast seller at 25% margin can earn more than a slow one at 50%, because the stock turns over sooner. Keep basics (innerwear, plain tees) at lower margin to bring people in, and festive and wedding wear at higher margin.
  • Negotiate with suppliers. Cutting cost from ₹1,000 to ₹950 on a ₹1,500 tag lifts margin from 33.3% to 36.7%. Ask for bulk, advance-payment or repeat-order rates.
  • Keep one simple register with each item's cost, selling price and margin. Count small costs too: packing bags, transport, alterations, UPI charges.
  • Keep shop money and personal money apart. Decide a fixed monthly salary and leave the rest in the business.
  • Do a 30-minute weekly review: top 5 and slowest 5 items, the week's margin, and cash against bills due. Write down your return and alteration policy and train staff on the floor price.
  • Give udhar only to people you know, with a limit, and write every amount with a date.
  • GST is not your profit or your cost. Work out margin on the price before tax (₹549 ÷ 1.05 = ₹523 at 5%). Rates change, so confirm with a CA in week one.

7. Competing with M Baazar and the small shops

  • In week one, note the price and the lowest bargain price of 20 popular items at three nearby shops.
  • If a rival's price is under your landed cost, do not copy it. Skip the item or stock it only to draw people in.
  • Win on what a big shop skips: all sizes, one or two customer groups done well, alterations, easy exchange, and honest quality you can explain.
  • Avoid a price war. The bigger shop lasts longer. Compete only where you stay above your break-even.

8. Billing: app for bills, Excel for decisions

Start with a free billing app from day one. It records every bill, reduces stock automatically and gives a daily sales report. Keep Excel for the price survey, margin tracker and break-even. Skip full ERP software for now. Close the cash every evening against the app's report. Prices and plan limits in the guide come from third-party sites, so check the official page before buying.

Six mistakes new owners make

  1. Tagging from the bill price and forgetting transport and packing.
  2. Doubling the cost without checking what the shop next door charges.
  3. Having no floor price when bargaining starts.
  4. Mixing up margin and markup.
  5. Buying too much stock in the first order.
  6. Mixing shop money with home money and keeping no records.
