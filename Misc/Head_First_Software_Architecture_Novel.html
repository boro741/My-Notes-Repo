The Glass Room

Book chapter 1 · What software architecture is

About 6 min read

Alex's first morning. A client, a very confident developer, and a whiteboard.

Bengaluru · Monday · 9:10 AM · Rain on the glass

You had been Principal Architect for exactly one hour and ten minutes.

Your badge was still warm from the printer. Your coffee was too hot to drink. And now you were standing in the Glass Room, the fourth-floor war room at Lighthouse Labs. The walls were glass. The whiteboards were huge. The rain outside was louder than the air conditioner.

Vikram, the VP of Product, uncapped a red marker and slapped the whiteboard with it.

Vikram“Good morning, Alex! Welcome to the team! Big news. Our first client is ZipEats, a hyperlocal food-ordering app. Think Zomato, but built for one neighbourhood at a time. They want a beta in thirty days. Rohan, tell Alex the plan.”

Rohan, the Senior Developer, leaned forward. He was a brilliant coder with a very loud keyboard and a very big heart for new tools.

Rohaneyes shining “Fifty microservices. Written in Go and Rust. Deployed on Kubernetes. They talk over gRPC. And a graph database, because it was trending on Hacker News this morning.”

Vikramnodding like he understood every word “Love it. Very scalable.”

Everyone turned to look at you.

In the corner, Meera took a slow sip of black coffee. She was the Distinguished Architect who had hired you. She carried a battered grid notebook and she never gave an answer when a question would do. Today she gave you nothing at all. Not a word, not a hint.

This was your room now.

You stood up, walked to the whiteboard, and picked up a blue marker.

You“Rohan, a question. If I ask what kind of house you are building, and you say ‘Victorian style’, have you given me the blueprint?”

Rohan“Well... I told you the style.”

You“You gave me one quarter of the picture.”

The four pieces of every architecture

You drew four boxes on the glass. Then you tapped them one by one.

   THE FOUR DIMENSIONS OF ARCHITECTURE

  +----------------------+   +----------------------+
  | 1. CHARACTERISTICS   |   | 2. DECISIONS         |
  | What must the system |   | The big rules, e.g.  |
  | be great at?         |   | "UI never talks to   |
  |                      |   |  the database"       |
  +----------------------+   +----------------------+
  +----------------------+   +----------------------+
  | 3. LOGICAL           |   | 4. STYLE             |
  |    COMPONENTS        |   | The overall shape:   |
  | The "rooms": Menu,   |   | layered, microkernel,|
  | Orders, Payments     |   | microservices...     |
  +----------------------+   +----------------------+

You“Every software architecture has four dimensions. A room has length, width, and height. A system has these four. Characteristics are the qualities the system must have, like speed, safety, or the ability to handle crowds. Decisions are the big rules the team must follow. Logical components are the building blocks of the business, like Order Placement or Payment. And style is the overall shape of the system.”

You“Rohan said ‘microservices’. That is only one box. It is the style. We still don’t know what ZipEats must be great at. We don’t know the rooms. We don’t know the rules.”

Rohandefensive “Fine. Then I am making a design decision. Microservices and a graph database.”

Meera lowered her cup.

Meera“Is that design? Or is that architecture?”

Rohan frowned.

Rohan“Aren’t they the same thing?”

The house test

You walked to the window. Rain was running down the glass in long lines.

You“Think of a house. The colour of the walls, the place of the sofa, the rug in the hall. That is design. It is cheap to change. You can repaint on a Sunday. Now think of the poured concrete, the load-bearing walls, and the roof. That is architecture. If you start building a one-storey ranch house and, halfway, decide you want a three-storey Victorian mansion, you break the budget and you miss your move-in date.”

You“Martin Fowler said it best: software architecture is ‘the stuff that’s hard to change.’”

In software it is the same. Design is how a screen looks, which colours you pick, or how you arrange the classes inside one feature. Architecture is how the web pages talk to the services, how the services talk to the databases, and how everything is shipped to servers.

Meera“Most real choices sit somewhere between the two, Alex. So how does a developer know when to decide alone, and when to call the architect?”

You wrote three questions on the board.

  IS THIS DECISION "ARCHITECTURE"?

  1. Strategic or tactical?
     A chat between two devs = tactical.
     Weeks of planning with many people = strategic.

  2. How hard is it to change?
     Move a button      -> 10 minutes.
     Monolith -> micro  -> a year.

  3. How big are the trade-offs?
     Rug colour    -> tiny.
     City to live in -> huge.

You“The more strategic, the harder to change, and the bigger the trade-offs, the more architectural it is. Let me show you with a real case. Rohan, you want to put an asynchronous queue between Order Placement and Inventory. The customer places an order, the order service drops a message in the queue, and it does not wait for the stock check. Ordering feels instant. Is this architecture?”

Rohan“It’s just a queue! Ten lines of code. That’s design.”

You“Run the three questions. Strategic or tactical? Tactical. Two developers could do it in an afternoon. Effort to change? Low. Now trade-offs. Ordering feels fast, but the stock count updates late. Two people can buy the last pizza base at the same time. That means angry customers and back-orders. Big trade-off.”

You“So this decision sits in the middle, leaning a little towards architecture. A developer can build it, but an architect must be in the room. You need all three questions. Any one alone would lie to you.”

Rohan looked at the board for a long moment.

Rohanquietly “Okay. That’s... actually useful.”

Your move, AlexIs “use a different font on the checkout button” an architectural decision?

No. It is tactical, it takes minutes to undo, and the trade-offs are tiny. It is pure design. Compare it with “split Payments into its own separate service”. That needs weeks of planning, it is hard to undo, and the trade-offs are big. That one is architecture.

The puzzle

You“One more thing. These four boxes are pieces of a puzzle. They must fit together. If I choose ‘very high scalability’ as a characteristic, it must show up in the style I pick, in the components I draw, and in the decisions I write. Change one piece, and you must check the other three. And you cannot skip a piece.”

Vikramtapping his watch “Fascinating lecture. Truly. But I have a thirty-day deadline, and I want to know one thing. Why are we not doing Rohan’s fifty microservices?”

You smiled. You had been waiting for this question.

You“Because we don’t have a map yet, Vikram. And you should never pick a road without a map.”

Meera closed her notebook, just a little, the way a person does when she is trying not to smile.

Before you choose a style, you must be able to place it on the map. And the map has just two questions.

  • Architecture is the structure of a system, the part that is hard and costly to change. Design is the detail and the looks, which is easy to change.
  • Architecture has four dimensions: characteristics, decisions, logical components, and style. Saying “we use microservices” only gives you one of the four.
  • The four pieces fit like a puzzle. Change one, and check the other three.
  • To find out if a choice is architectural, ask three questions: Is it strategic? Is it hard to change? Are the trade-offs big? You need all three answers.
  • Characteristics come first. You cannot choose a database or a style until you know what the system must be great at.

The Map

Book chapter 5 · Architectural styles, a simple map

About 7 min read

Two questions that place every architectural style on one map.

The Glass Room · 9:55 AM · The rain has not stopped

You drew a big cross on the whiteboard. A simple two-by-two grid, four empty boxes.

You“There are hundreds of architectural styles, and new ones appear every year. Nobody can remember all of them. You don’t need to. You only need two questions, and with them you can place any style on a map.”

                 HOW IS IT SHIPPED? (Deployment)
                 Monolith          Distributed
              +---------------+-----------------+
   Technical  |               |                 |
 HOW IS THE   |      ?        |        ?        |
 CODE SLICED? +---------------+-----------------+
 (Partitioning)               |                 |
   Domain     |      ?        |        ?        |
              +---------------+-----------------+

Question 1: How do you slice the code?

You“There are only two ways to slice a system. The first is technical partitioning. You cut the system by technical job. All the screen code lives together. All the business rules live together. All the database code lives together. Think of a giant wedding kitchen. One crew only chops vegetables. One crew only cooks. One crew only plates the food.”

Rohan“That’s separation of concerns. I like it.”

You“You should. Now the second way is domain partitioning. You cut the system by the business problem. Everything about Customers lives together. Everything about Payments lives together. Think of a food court. The dosa stall has its own griddle and its own cashier. The biryani stall has its own pot and its own cashier. Every stall is complete.”

You showed them how it looks in the code folders.

  Technical slicing        Domain slicing
  app.presentation.customer   app.customer.presentation
  app.services.customer       app.customer.services
  app.persistence.customer    app.customer.persistence

You“The customer code appears in both. The only difference is what comes first in the name: the technical job, or the business area.”

Meera“And which one is better?”

You“Technical slicing works well when your team is split by skill: front-end people, back-end people, and database people. Domain slicing matches the real business problem more closely. Neither is always better. Your team and your problem decide.”

Question 2: How many things do you ship?

You“The second question is about deployment. Deployment means putting your software on servers so people can use it. Do you ship one package, or many packages that run separately?”

You“A monolith is shipped as one unit. One JAR file. One .exe. One running process. A distributed system is split into many units, and they talk to each other over a network.”

Rohan was already shaking his head.

Rohan“Monolith is a dirty word. Nobody serious builds monoliths anymore.”

Meeracalmly “Rohan, say that again after you hear what a monolith is good at.”

You wrote two lists on the board. Left side first.

  MONOLITH - the good
  + Simple: one codebase, easy to understand
  + Cheap: less infrastructure to pay for
  + Fast to start: perfect when you must launch soon
  + Easy to debug: one stack trace tells the story
  + Reliable early: almost no network calls to fail

You“A stack trace is just the list of steps the program took before it failed. In a monolith, you read one list, from start to end. Remember this for later.”

Priya, the Lead Ops and Database engineer, had been silent until now. She sat at the back with her arms folded. She had the tired eyes of a person who had seen every midnight server crash of the last ten years.

Priya“Now tell them the bad part.”

You“The trouble begins when the app grows.”

  MONOLITH - the bad (as it grows)
  - Scaling is all-or-nothing. If the reports page eats
    memory, you must copy the WHOLE app.
  - One bad bug can crash everything.
  - Every tiny change = deploy the whole thing again.
  - One codebase = one technology stack for all.

You“Now the distributed side. Each part is deployed on its own. So you can scale only the busy parts. A crash can stay inside one service. Each unit is small, so testing is easier. And you can deploy a single change without touching the rest.”

Rohan“See? That’s what I said!”

Priyaflatly “You forgot the bill.”

The price of the network

Priya stood up. She walked to the board and wrote a title in big letters: THE FALLACIES OF DISTRIBUTED COMPUTING.

Priya“In the 1990s, some engineers at Sun Microsystems, including L. Peter Deutsch, made a list of false ideas that developers believe about networks. Developers always think the network is reliable, fast, and free. It is none of those. Read that list before you build anything distributed.”

She turned to Rohan.

Priya“Here is what the network costs you. Performance: many small services calling each other over a network are slower than one program calling itself. Cost: more servers, more network setup, bigger cloud bills. Simplicity: it is the opposite of simple. And debugging: when an order fails across twelve services, the error could be in any of them. You will need log aggregation, which means collecting logs from every service in one place. That is extra cost too.”

Priya“I once spent a whole night finding one failed request. At 3 AM, I promised myself I would never again say ‘just split it into services.’”

Rohan, for the first time that day, said nothing.

Many teams choose a distributed design because it sounds modern. Later they discover they bought a lot of network trouble that they never needed.

Four boxes, no winners

You filled the grid.

                  Monolith              Distributed
              +-------------------+-----------------------+
   Technical  | Layered           | Microkernel, when its |
              | Microkernel (the  | plugins run as their  |
              | usual way)        | own services          |
              +-------------------+-----------------------+
   Domain     | Modular monolith  | Microservices         |
              +-------------------+-----------------------+

You“Event-driven architecture is distributed too. It is a different kind of animal, and we will meet it last.”

You“These are the styles we will walk through, one by one. Layered. Modular monolith. Microkernel. Microservices. Event-driven. And notice, no box says ‘best’. Every box has strengths, and every box has a bill.”

Meera“A style is a bundle of trade-offs, Alex. That is all it is. The monolith and the distributed system could talk all day. The monolith says, ‘I am simpler, cheaper, and faster to start.’ The distributed system says, ‘True, but I can keep growing when you cannot.’ Both are right.”

Vikram had been counting on his fingers.

Vikram“Okay, okay. But our project. Thirty days. One UI person, two back-end developers, and Priya on the database. And a very small budget.”

You looked at the team. You looked at the calendar. Then you looked at Rohan, kindly.

You“With this team, Rohan’s fifty services would mean fifty deployments, fifty pipelines, and a network in the middle of every call. We would still be setting up our servers when the funding runs out. We need structure, but we also need speed.”

Vikram“So what do we build?”

You capped the blue marker.

You“We start with the simplest style that still gives real structure. Layered. Technically sliced. Shipped as one monolith.”

Rohan opened his mouth. Then he closed it. Then he opened his laptop, very slowly, and typed something.

Rohan“I’m... writing ‘Fallacies of Distributed Computing’ in my to-read list.”

Meera smiled into her coffee.

Thirty days later, ZipEats went live on a layered monolith. For six months everything was perfect. And then came month eight.

  • Place any style on a map with two questions: How is the code sliced (partitioning)? How is it shipped (deployment)?
  • Technical partitioning slices by technical job (presentation, business rules, data). Domain partitioning slices by business area (customers, payments, orders).
  • A monolith ships as one unit. It is simple, cheap, fast to start, and easy to debug. But scaling is all-or-nothing and every change means a full redeploy.
  • A distributed system ships as many units over a network. It scales the busy parts and keeps crashes local. But it is slower, costlier, harder to debug, and far from simple.
  • The network is the hidden danger. Remember the Fallacies of Distributed Computing.
  • No style is the best. Every style is a set of trade-offs. Match the style to your needs, not to the hype.

The Layer Cake

Book chapter 6 · Layered architecture

About 6 min read

The simplest style that still gives you structure, and the day it started to hurt.

Lighthouse Labs · Day 3 of the project · Chai o’clock

The next morning, you sketched ZipEats on the glass wall. Three boxes, stacked like a tiffin carrier, with a database underneath.

   [ Customer phone / browser ]
              |
              v
  +-------------------------------+
  | PRESENTATION LAYER            |  screens, forms
  +-------------------------------+
              |
              v
  +-------------------------------+
  | WORKFLOW (BUSINESS) LAYER     |  order rules, coupon maths
  +-------------------------------+
              |
              v
  +-------------------------------+
  | PERSISTENCE LAYER             |  SQL, reading/saving data
  +-------------------------------+
              |
              v
        ( ONE DATABASE )

Rohan“Wait. This is just MVC. Model, View, Controller.”

You“MVC is a design pattern. It is about roles inside your code. Layered architecture goes one step further. It also decides where the browser, the app server, and the database live. In fact, layers are so old that the book thinks they probably inspired MVC, not the other way round. Your Presentation layer plays the View. The Workflow layer holds the business rules. And Persistence talks to the database.”

The relay race

You“Think of a relay race where every runner must touch the baton. A hungry customer taps ‘Place Order’. The baton drops from Presentation to Workflow. The Workflow layer checks the rules and passes it down to Persistence. Persistence saves it into the database. Then the answer runs back up the same road to the customer’s screen.”

Rohan“Can we add more layers? Like one for restaurant partner APIs? Or loyalty points?”

You“Yes. Teams often add a services layer for partners or an integration layer for outside systems. But remember the golden rule: every request passes through every layer. So only add a layer if almost every request needs it. A layer that matters for only a few requests slows down everything else.”

Priya looked up from her laptop.

Priya“And how do we ship it?”

You“We have three options.”

  THREE WAYS TO SHIP A LAYERED MONOLITH

  Two-tier       All layers in ONE unit, talking to a
                 database. Like old accounting software.
                 + simple, fast     - weak scaling

  Three-tier     Web front end (JavaScript) | App server
                 (workflow + persistence)   | Database
                 + clean split, better scale
                 - more parts, slower to start

  Embedded       Everything + database inside ONE device.
                 A mobile game, a vending machine.
                 + self-contained   - hard to extend

You chose three-tier: a JavaScript front end, a Java app server, and Priya’s MySQL database.

Six good months

And for six months, it was magic.

The UI specialist lived happily in Presentation. Rohan’s two backend developers lived in Workflow. Priya tuned Persistence. Every database helper she wrote was reused by the whole app. In month three, Vikram asked for a brand-new mobile screen. You built a new Presentation layer and did not touch the other two. It took a week.

Meerareading her notebook “That is layered architecture at its best. A new kind of screen touches only one layer.”

You were proud. You even bought a framed print of the layered cake and hung it on your desk.

Month eight

Then, on a Monday morning, Vikram stood outside your desk. He was holding a crumpled printout.

Vikram“Why does it take three weeks to add Custom Pizza Toppings and ZipEats Plus discounts?! It is one feature!”

You opened the code. Your stomach sank.

Yes, it was one feature for Vikram. But the code was sliced by technology, not by business. The pizza menu, the discount rules, the delivery address, the loyalty rewards: each business idea had been cut into pieces and spread across every layer.

Youto Meera, over lunch “To add toppings, we change the screens in Presentation, the pricing rules in Workflow, the entities in the model, the SQL in Persistence, and the tables in the database. Five places. So the UI person, the backend people, and Priya must all sit in meetings for days before anyone writes a line of code.”

Meera“That is the big trade-off of layers. Technical changes are easy. If you swap your UI framework, only one layer is touched. But business changes are painful, because a business idea is not technical. It cuts across all layers, like a tiered wedding cake. You can change the icing without touching the sponge. But if you want a new flavour, you must redo every tier.”

Layers make technical changes easy and domain changes hard. If your business keeps changing its products and rules, expect layers to fight you.

It got worse. That night, Meera pointed at a commit on your laptop.

Meera“Look at what Rohan did to meet the deadline.”

Rohan had skipped the Workflow layer completely. A button in the UI called a SQL method in Persistence directly. Three other developers had copied him. Soon everything was connected to everything.

Your neat layered cake was turning into a big ball of mud: a system where so many parts depend on each other that the picture looks like a tangle of electric wires on a street pole.

Rohanvery small voice “It was only one shortcut...”

Priyanot unkindly “It is always one shortcut.”

The scorecard

Meera took out her grid notebook and drew a quick scorecard. The book rates every style on nine traits, from one star (poorly supported) to five stars (very well supported).

Scorecard: Layered Overall cost $

  • Maintainability★★★★★
  • Testability★★★★★
  • Deployability★★★★★
  • Simplicity★★★★★
  • Evolvability★★★★★
  • Performance★★★★★
  • Scalability★★★★★
  • Elasticity★★★★★
  • Fault tolerance★★★★★

One star = poorly supported. Five stars = very well supported.

Meera“You got a bargain, Alex. The cost is one dollar sign, the lowest. Simplicity is five stars. Performance is three, because a well-built monolith makes no network calls. Testability is only two. After that, almost everything is one star: maintainability, deployability, evolvability, scalability, elasticity, and fault tolerance. Layered is very easy to start, and very hard to grow.”

You sighed.

You“Rohan is begging me to split it into microservices right now.”

Meeraraising one eyebrow “With Priya as your only Ops engineer? You will kill the company. Fix the real problem. The pain is that the business is smeared across the layers. You don’t need a network to fix that.”

You stared at the cake print on your desk for a long minute. Then, slowly, you took a marker and turned it ninety degrees.

You“We don’t change the shipping. We change the slicing.”

Your move, AlexYour business logic is spread across every layer, and every feature touches five places. What is the smallest change that could help, without the network bill of microservices?

Keep one deployment (still a monolith), but slice the code by business area instead of by technical job. That is exactly the next idea: the modular monolith.

You told the team to meet in the all-hands area at 4 PM. “Bring your laptops,” you said. “We are going to draw lines in the concrete.”

  • Layered architecture is technically partitioned and usually a monolith. The classic layers are presentation, workflow (business rules), and persistence.
  • A request goes down through every layer and comes back up. So only add a layer when almost every request needs it. Common extra layers are services and integration.
  • It can be shipped as two-tier, three-tier, or embedded/mobile. Each has trade-offs.
  • Strengths: very simple, quick to build, cheap, good fit for teams split by skill, easy technical reuse and technical changes, and good performance (no network).
  • Weakness: the business domain is spread across all layers, so business changes are painful. It can rot into a big ball of mud, and it scales and deploys poorly.
  • Scorecard: Simplicity 5, Performance 3, Testability 2, all others 1. Cost: $.

Lines in the Concrete

Book chapter 7 · Modular monolith

About 6 min read

The same single deployment, sliced in a smarter way.

The all-hands area · 4:00 PM · Forty developers and one very large whiteboard

By now ZipEats had forty developers. They sat on bean bags, on desks, and on the floor. Somebody had brought samosas.

You“Here is the plan. We keep our single deployment. No network calls between parts, no distributed headaches. But we stop slicing the code by technology. From this sprint, ZipEats becomes a modular monolith.”

You erased the horizontal layers and drew tall vertical columns, all inside one big box.

 +-------------------- ONE DEPLOYMENT --------------------+
 |                                                        |
 |  +-------------+  +-------------+  +-------------+     |
 |  | ORDER       |  | MENU        |  | PAYMENT     |     |
 |  | module      |  | module      |  | module      |     |
 |  |  screens    |  |  screens    |  |  screens    |     |
 |  |  rules      |  |  rules      |  |  rules      |     |
 |  |  data code  |  |  data code  |  |  data code  |     |
 |  +------+------+  +------+------+  +------+------+     |
 +---------|----------------|----------------|------------+
           v                v                v
     orders_schema     menu_schema     payments_schema
        (all inside ONE database server)

Rohan“Hold on. Each column still has screens, rules, and data code inside?”

You“Yes. Inside a module, those layers are a private detail. Think of a vada pav. One bite goes through the bun, the vada, and the chutney together. Each module is one vertical bite of the business: Orders, Menu, Payment, Inventory. We will also change our teams. Instead of a UI team, a backend team, and a database team, we make cross-functional teams. The Order team will have UI, backend, and data skills in one pod. They own the whole Order slice, from screen to table.”

You“Next time Vikram asks for pizza toppings, the Menu and Order teams handle it together. Payments and Inventory won’t even notice.”

A developer in the third row raised her hand.

Developer“What if my module needs something from another module?”

You“Good question. That is the hard part, and I will show you why in two weeks.”

The Friday build failure

Two weeks later, you were doing code review when you saw a disaster in the making.

The Order module needed to know whether a restaurant still had mozzarella. Everything lived in one codebase, so a developer’s editor had helpfully auto-imported a class from the Inventory module, StockTableDAO, straight into the Order module. It worked on the first try. The developer was very proud.

Youto Rohan “If modules reach inside each other’s private classes, we don’t have a modular monolith. We have a big ball of mud with nicer folder names.”

You and Priya spent the next 48 hours building three fences.

  THREE FENCES AROUND EVERY MODULE

  1. PUBLIC API ONLY
     InventoryApi.isInStock(itemId)  <- allowed
     everything else inside Inventory <- private

  2. COMPILER / BUILD WALLS
     Java: JPMS (Java Platform Module System)
     .NET: namespaces + the "internal" keyword
     Each module = its own Gradle subproject, so
     private classes cannot even be imported.

  3. AUTOMATED WATCHDOGS
     ArchUnit (Java), ArchUnitNET (.NET),
     ts-arch (TypeScript). Break a rule and
     the build FAILS.

A monolithic deployment does not need a monolithic codebase. You can build many modules and join them at build time.

Rohanwatching the first red build “Whoa. It literally yelled at me.”

Priya“Good. That is what a watchdog is for. Now come and see how they cheat in my database.”

The sneaky SQL join

Priya opened a query on her screen.

  SELECT o.id, r.name, r.price
  FROM   orders o
  JOIN   recipes r ON o.recipe_id = r.id;   -- !!! cross-module join

Priya“The Order module is joining its table straight to the Menu module’s table. If the Menu team renames one column tomorrow, orders will crash, and nobody will know why.”

You nodded. Code was now modular. Data had to follow.

Context: all the forces that shaped the choice. Decision: a firm voice, ‘We will use...’, and always the reason. Consequences: both the good and the bad. Governance: how we will check that people really follow it. And Notes: the author, dates, and approvers.”

Priya“Governance and Notes are not in the classic template.”

You“No. The book’s authors added them. A decision without enforcement is just a wish. And metadata inside the file is easier to find than digging through Git logs.”

Two weeks later, Rohan came to your desk, looking pleased with himself.

Rohan“Remember ADR 007, where the Survey team chose a relational database? Their survey data is much more varied now, so they moved to a document store. I opened 007 and changed it to say ‘Document Store’. All done!”

Youstanding up “Undo that commit. Now.”

Rohan“What? It’s the same file. Why does it matter?”

You“Because we are on ADR 013. If you edit 007, anyone who reads the history will think the project started with a document store. You never rewrite an Accepted ADR. Write a brand-new ADR 014, Accepted, with its own trade-off analysis. Then change ADR 007’s status to Superseded by ADR 014, and link them in both directions.”

Rohan“A chain.”

You“That chain is the institutional memory of the company. Anyone who finds the old record sees at once that it is out of date, and follows the link to the new one. The only expected change to an Accepted ADR is its status moving to Superseded. Adding a missing fact is fine. Changing the decision itself is not.”

Your move, AlexADR 007 chose a relational database. The team now needs a document store. What should you do with ADR 007?

Don’t rewrite it. Write ADR 014 for the new decision, set ADR 007’s status to “Superseded by ADR 014”, and link the two records both ways.

Where do ADRs live?

You“Store them where people can add, edit, and search them easily, and where changes are tracked. The simplest choice is plain Markdown files in version control, in their own repository, not mixed with source code. The file name is the number, then the lower-case title with hyphens. Lower case avoids trouble across operating systems, and sorting by name shows the decisions in order. A wiki also works, if it tracks edit history. Many good ADR efforts die because nobody can find the document when they need it. Start without special tools. Learn what your team needs. Then look for a tool.”

You“And keep the process light. If it feels heavy, people stop writing ADRs. A short ADR that exists is better than a perfect one that never gets written.”

Priya“How far does an ADR reach?”

You“It can be small, just one team. Or very wide. The book mentions the famous API mandate at Amazon: every service must talk to others only through APIs. That touched the whole company. ADRs about security or legal compliance often cross many teams. Those need a lot of teamwork, and often approval from a central architecture review board.”

The sneaker story ended well. Customers loved the real-time alerts. The analytics helped the security team catch scammers early. And a year later, when the Finance department did ask for the feed, they subscribed to the topic. Nobody touched the trading service.

Six months later, you opened ADR 042 again to check something. Everything you needed was right there, even Priya’s note. You smiled. Then Meera walked in, wearing her travel jacket, with a small leather notebook in her hand.

  • First Law: everything in software architecture is a trade-off. If you see no trade-off, look again. Watch out for two traps: always using the same tool, and shiny object syndrome.
  • Queues: point-to-point; good for security, custom messages, and independent scaling; bad for coupling and extensibility. Topics: broadcast; easy to extend and loosely coupled; same message for all, all-or-nothing scaling, weaker security.
  • Trade-off analysis finds the answer that fits your business. Use your top three characteristics as the judge. ATAM and CBAM are formal helpers.
  • Architectural decisions are guideposts. They are significant if they affect structure, qualities, dependencies, interfaces, or build techniques.
  • Second Law: why is more important than how. Record the reason with an ADR.
  • ADR sections: Title, Status, Context, Decision, Consequences, Governance, Notes. Titles start with a three-digit number.
  • Statuses: RFC → Proposed → Accepted → Superseded. Never rewrite an Accepted ADR; write a new one and link both ways.
  • Keep ADRs in plain text under version control or a wiki with history. Keep the process light.

Rule 1. Keep writing code, but stay off the critical path

Never stop coding, or you will forget the pain your decisions cause developers. But never own a feature on the critical path, the chain of work that decides the release date. If executive meetings eat three days of your week, you will block the whole team.

  HOW TO STAY HANDS-ON SAFELY
  * Write proof-of-concept (PoC) code to test trade-offs.
    Write it like production code. PoCs love to get shipped!
  * Pay down technical debt a little at a time.
  * Join production outages and find the ROOT cause.
  * Do lots of code reviews. They keep the code aligned
    with your ADRs.

Rule 2. Know what the job demands

Whatever your title says, eight things sit on your shoulders:

  1. Make architecture decisions, set design principles.
  2. Keep analysing so the architecture doesn't rot
     (architectural vitality).
  3. Keep up with industry trends.
  4. Make sure decisions are followed
     (architectural governance).
  5. Know a little about MANY technologies.
  6. Know the business domain well.
  7. Handle office politics (explain why a design is
     worth its cost).
  8. Lead people. Gerald Weinberg wrote: "No matter what
     they tell you, it's always a people problem."

Meeraleaning on the railing “Here is one example. A product owner wants the cheapest database. You know it will fail in the holiday rush. Do not just say ‘no’. Bring a short test that shows what breaks. Show what it would cost in lost sales. Now the talk is about money, not opinions.”

Rule 3. People skills keep designs alive

These sound easy. They take years of practice and mistakes.

  * SHOW, DON'T ARGUE. A Google search cannot settle an
    argument about YOUR system. Build a quick PoC in a
    setup close to production and show the numbers.
  * PICK YOUR BATTLES. Fight hard for what the
    architecture cannot live without. Let the rest go.
  * SPEAK BUSINESS VALUE. Stakeholders don't care about
    "fault tolerance". They care about time to market,
    compliance, and mergers.
  * INVOLVE DEVELOPERS EARLY. If they don't know WHY,
    they won't agree. If they weren't involved, they
    won't follow. Use ADR RFCs and risk-storming.
  * DIVIDE AND CONQUER (Sun Tzu). "400 ms and 99.999%
    for everything!" Split the system. Checkout needs
    five-nines. Editing a user bio does not.
  * THE FOUR Cs: be Clear, Concise, Communicate,
    Collaborate. And keep a calendar block free, early
    morning or late afternoon, for your team's questions.

Meera“Don’t say, ‘We need a message queue for fault tolerance.’ Say, ‘If payments are down for an hour, this queue keeps taking orders, so we lose no sales.’ The idea is the same. But now the business is on your side.”

Rule 4. Draw diagrams people can actually read

An ADR explains the why. A diagram shows the what. Avoid the “hairball effect”, where tangled lines hide the picture.

  SEVEN RULES FOR READABLE DIAGRAMS
  1. Always add a TITLE (which view of the system?)
  2. Always add a KEY / legend. (Only the database
     cylinder is a shape everyone agrees on.)
  3. Use one-way arrows. No double-headed arrows.
  4. Use full labels, not acronyms.
  5. Solid line  ====>   = synchronous (caller waits)
  6. Dotted line - - ->  = asynchronous (fire and forget)
  7. Keep shapes and colours consistent.

Rule 5. Trade depth for breadth

Picture a pyramid. At the top is the stuff you know. In the middle is the stuff you know that you don’t know: solutions you know exist, with their rough trade-offs. At the bottom is the stuff you don’t know that you don’t know.

Meera“Developers grow by making the top tip taller. Architects grow by making the middle band wider. Your deep skill in one language will fade a little. That hurts. But knowing five ways to solve a problem, with their trade-offs, is far more valuable to an architect than knowing one way deeply. If you are a .NET expert, volunteer for a Java project. Pick up the JavaScript-heavy UI. Take a hard data problem.”

Rule 6. Sharpen the blade with katas

Bruce Lee said he did not fear the man who practised ten thousand different kicks once. He feared the man who practised one kick ten thousand times. An architectural kata is a practice exercise where you design a real system, so you can repeat the process.

  HOW A KATA RUNS
  * Teams of 3-5 people. Odd numbers, so a vote settles ties.
    Mix people who don't work together every day.
  * A moderator plays the CUSTOMER and answers questions.
  * Run the whole loop: find the characteristics ->
    find the logical components -> choose a style ->
    write ADRs and sketch the diagram.
  * Present. Defend your trade-offs against tough
    (but fair) questions. Give balanced feedback.
  * Time: from 45 minutes to several weeks.

Layered

Pick it when: Your time and budget are tight, the system is small, and most changes are technical (a new screen, a new database).

Think twice when: The business changes its products and rules often, or you must scale a lot.

Modular monolith

Pick it when: You want one simple deployment, but business changes keep hurting, and you can form cross-functional teams.

Think twice when: Parts of the system need different qualities, or you need strong elasticity and fault tolerance.

Microkernel

Pick it when: You have a small stable core and many separate, changing customisations (devices, tax rules, extensions).

Think twice when: The core changes all the time, or your plugins must talk to each other a lot.

Microservices

Pick it when: You need to scale parts separately, survive crashes, and move fast, with cross-functional teams and data you can split.

Think twice when: Simplicity and cost matter most, the system is small, or the data cannot be split.

Event-driven

Pick it when: You need speed, scale, and easy extension, and the work can happen at the same time in the background.

Think twice when: You need many synchronous calls, or you must test everything very simply.

You“Notice how the whole story fits on this one page. Layered is the cheapest and easiest, but weak at growing. The modular monolith keeps the cost down and fixes domain changes. The microkernel is the specialist for plugins. Microservices is the champion at scaling and surviving crashes, and it sends a very large bill. Event-driven is the fastest and the most extensible, but the hardest to test.”
