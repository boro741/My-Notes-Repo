Managerial Accounting

> The internal numbers only Rakesh needs to see — fixed costs, variable costs, break-even, and where his profit is really coming from. All figures in ₹ INR.

Currency: ₹ INR Reading time: ~10 min Concepts: 5
Why this chapter matters

Internal Numbers vs. External Numbers

Chapter 2's Balance Sheet and P&L were built for outsiders — banks, investors, tax authorities. Managerial accounting is different: it's built for Rakesh himself, to make better day-to-day decisions. Nobody outside the tapri ever needs to see this worksheet — but Rakesh needs it constantly.

The first, most important shift in mindset: stop treating all costs the same. Split every cost into Fixed and Variable — they behave completely differently, and mixing them together hides the decisions that actually matter.

Concept 1

Fixed Costs vs. Variable Costs

A Fixed Cost stays the same no matter how much chai Rakesh sells. A Variable Cost moves in direct proportion to how much he sells. Confusing the two leads to bad pricing and bad decisions.

FIXED COST

Daily stall rent: ₹100
Doesn't change whether Rakesh sells 5 cups or 500 cups today.

VARIABLE COST

Milk, tea leaves, sugar: ₹7.50/cup
Every extra cup sold adds exactly ₹7.50 more cost. Sell zero cups, pay zero.

Why the split matters If Rakesh treats his rent as "just another cost per cup," he'll misprice chai on slow days and overprice it on busy days, since rent-per-cup keeps changing with volume. Keeping Fixed and Variable separate is what makes the rest of this chapter's math possible.

Concept 2

CVP Analysis & Contribution Margin

CVP (Cost-Volume-Profit) analysis sounds technical, but it's just: take your selling price, subtract your per-unit variable cost, and see what's left. That leftover amount is called the Contribution Margin — because it "contributes" toward paying off the fixed cost.

Revenue (Price) − Variable Cost = Contribution Margin ₹10.00 − ₹7.50 = ₹2.50 per cup

Every cup Rakesh sells generates ₹2.50 that goes toward paying off his ₹100 daily rent. Once enough cups have "contributed" ₹100 total, every cup after that is pure profit.

Concept 3

Break-Even Point — the magic question

"How many cups do I need to sell just to cover my rent?" is one of the most useful questions in business, and CVP answers it directly.

Break-Even Units = Fixed Cost ÷ Contribution Margin per unit ₹100 ÷ ₹2.50 = 40 cups
Where the Revenue line crosses the Total Cost line = the exact point profit turns positive.

Common mistake Assuming break-even is a one-time calculation. The moment rent changes, or Rakesh's ingredient cost changes (say, milk prices rise), the break-even point shifts too — it needs to be recalculated, not memorized as a fixed number.

Concept 4

Activity-Based Costing (ABC)

Once Rakesh adds samosas to the menu, a new problem appears: overhead costs (gas, frying oil, extra prep time) don't split evenly across chai and samosas — but a lazy accounting approach might just divide them 50/50 anyway. Activity-Based Costing means tracing overhead to the actual activity that causes it, so you know the real cost — and real profit — of each product.

Rakesh's example Frying samosas uses far more gas and oil than boiling chai, and takes longer per unit. When Rakesh actually traces that overhead to samosas specifically (instead of splitting it evenly with chai), he discovers samosas are less profitable than they looked — which tells him either to raise the samosa price, or streamline the frying process, rather than guessing.
Common mistake Splitting shared overhead evenly "for simplicity." It feels fair, but it hides which specific product or activity is actually dragging down profitability — which defeats the entire point of tracking costs in the first place.
Concept 5

The Management Process — Plan, Control, Evaluate

All of this costing analysis only pays off inside an ongoing loop, not as a one-time report:

PLAN
Set the break-even target & pricing
→
CONTROL
Track actual costs day to day
→
EVALUATE
Compare actual vs. plan, adjust
↻

For Rakesh, this looks like: Plan this month's break-even and pricing → Control by tracking daily ingredient costs against the plan → Evaluate at month-end whether milk price hikes quietly pushed his break-even point higher, then loop back into next month's plan with corrected numbers.
