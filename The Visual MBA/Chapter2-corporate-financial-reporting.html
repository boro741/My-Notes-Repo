Why this chapter matters

Accounting is the language of business

If you can't read a company's numbers, you're guessing about how it's actually doing — no different from trying to run a shop without ever counting the day's cash box. Every business, from a roadside chai tapri to a listed company, is explained by three financial statements:

💡 The 3 Statements Explained Simply
  • Balance Sheet — what the business owns and owes, right now.
  • Income Statement (P&L) — how much it made or lost over a period.
  • Cash Flow Statement — where actual cash moved in and out.

This chapter builds your understanding of the first two in detail, using one running example: Rakesh, who is starting a chai-aur-samosa tapri near a busy market.

Concept 1

Setting up shop — the Balance Sheet & Accounting Equation

Rakesh needs money to start. He takes a ₹5,000 loan from a local bank (that's a Liability — money owed to someone else). With that money, he buys a tapri cart, stove and utensils for ₹2,000 (an Asset), and keeps the remaining ₹3,000 as cash in hand (also an Asset).

The three building blocks

Assets — everything the business owns that has value (cash, the cart, stock of milk/sugar/samosas). Liabilities — everything it owes to outsiders (the bank loan). Equity — what's actually Rakesh's, after paying off every liability. Equity is often called "shareholders' money," but for a one-person tapri, it's simply Rakesh's own stake.

Here's the one rule that never breaks, for any business on earth:

Why it always balances Every rupee the business has came from somewhere — either borrowed (Liability) or contributed/earned (Equity). So the two sides of the equation are really just two different views of the exact same pile of money: one view shows what it turned into (Assets), the other shows who it belongs to (Liabilities + Equity). This is the whole logic behind double-entry bookkeeping — it's not a rule someone invented, it falls out automatically from tracking money honestly.
Concept 2

End of Day 1 — the balance sheet moves

Rakesh has a great first day — he sells ₹9,000 worth of chai and samosas. That profit (once expenses are accounted for) adds to his Equity, and the extra cash adds to his Assets. The equation still balances — it always will, because Equity is defined as whatever is left over after Liabilities are subtracted from Assets.

A balance sheet is a photo, not a video It only tells you the position at one instant — like a photo of Rakesh's cash box at 9 PM. To understand what actually happened during the day (how much he earned, spent, and kept), you need the Income Statement — think of that as the video recording of the whole day's business.

Concept 3

The Income Statement — where did the profit actually go?

The core formula: Revenue − Expenses = Net Profit. But "expenses" isn't one lump number — it happens in layers, and each layer tells you something different about the business.

Why EBIT gets its own line

EBIT (Earnings Before Interest & Taxes) shows how well the actual business is doing — making chai and selling it — completely separate from how it's financed (interest on the bank loan) or how much tax law takes away. This matters because interest and tax rates can change without Rakesh doing anything differently at his stall. EBIT is the number that isolates his operating skill from those outside factors.

Common mistake Treating Gross Profit as "take-home" profit. Gross Profit (₹7,000) only accounts for the direct cost of ingredients — rent, taxes, and loan interest haven't been paid yet. The real number Rakesh can put in his pocket is Net Profit (₹6,400), after every layer of expense.
Concept 4

Common-Size Statements — comparing apples to apples

Rupee figures alone don't tell you if a business is getting better — a bigger stall naturally has bigger numbers. So you divide every line by Sales, turning rupees into percentages. Now you can compare Rakesh's tapri this year vs last year, or even against a much bigger chai chain — the percentages are what's comparable, not the rupee size.

Reading this the right way Look at Gross Profit margin: it jumped from 33% last year to 78% this year. That's a huge improvement in how efficiently Rakesh is buying his ingredients relative to what he charges — maybe he found a cheaper wholesale supplier, or raised his chai price slightly. Common-size statements are what let you see a change like that instantly, instead of digging through rupee totals.

Concept 5

Pro Forma — forecasting next month

Pro forma simply means "what the future could look like." Say Rakesh expects sales to grow 20% next month, because he's adding a new corner near the office complex. To forecast the rest of the statement, you hold each expense's percentage of sales constant (from the common-size table above) and apply it to the new, bigger sales number.

Common mistake Scaling every line by the same growth rate. Only the costs that genuinely move with sales (ingredients, admin overhead tied to volume) should scale up. Taxes and loan interest here are held flat because they don't automatically change just because Rakesh sold more chai — interest depends on the loan amount, and tax depends on rules and profit levels, not directly on the sales growth rate.
Concept 6

Financial Ratios — quick health checks

Ratios compress a whole financial statement into one comparable number — useful for tracking Rakesh's tapri over time, or comparing it to another stall, or even a listed company (once you divide by the right things, size stops mattering).

Debt-to-Equity
Total Liabilities ÷ Equity
Measures: leverage — how much of the business is funded by borrowed money vs. Rakesh's own stake. High debt-to-equity = higher risk if sales dip, since loan payments don't wait for a bad month.
Current Ratio
Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Measures: liquidity — can Rakesh pay his near-term bills (this week's milk supplier, this month's loan EMI) with what he has on hand? Higher is safer, but too high can mean idle cash.
Return on Equity (ROE)
Net Income ÷ Shareholder Equity
Measures: how much profit Rakesh generates for every rupee of his own money invested — the ultimate "is my own capital working hard for me" number.
Net Profit Margin
Net Profit ÷ Net Sales
Measures: cost-control efficiency — of every rupee in sales, how much survives all expenses to become real profit.

Concept 7

The DuPont Framework — breaking ROE apart

Two businesses can have the exact same ROE for completely different — and very differently risky — reasons. The DuPont framework splits ROE into three drivers so you can see which one is doing the work:

Why this matters — a real comparison Rakesh's tapri could reach a healthy ROE mostly through a high profit margin (chai is genuinely cheap to make and sells well) with almost no debt. Meanwhile, an NBFC or finance company might hit the same ROE with a wafer-thin profit margin, almost entirely by cranking up financial leverage (borrowing heavily to fund large assets). Both show up as "good ROE" on paper — but Rakesh's version is a far safer business than one propped up mostly by debt. DuPont is what lets you tell those two stories apart.
Concept 8

How shares work — splitting ownership

Suppose Rakesh formally sets up his tapri as a small company with 100 total shares. He later takes on a business partner (say, to fund a second cart) and gives away 20 shares — so the partner now owns 20% of the business, and Rakesh owns the remaining 80%.

Working out one share's value
Net income (Year 1)₹6,400

Total shares100

This is the same idea behind any listed company's share price — it's just company value, sliced into equal pieces. The healthier the business's Assets and Net Income, the more each individual share is worth.

📌 Ledger Recap — formulas to remember

  • Accounting Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity
  • Income Statement: Revenue − Expenses = Net Profit
  • Gross Profit Margin: Gross Profit ÷ Sales
  • EBIT: Operating profit, before interest & taxes
  • Common-Size: Every line ÷ Sales, shown as %
  • Debt-to-Equity: Total Liabilities ÷ Equity
  • Current Ratio: Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
  • ROE: Net Income ÷ Equity
  • DuPont ROE: Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Leverage
  • Share Value: Company Worth ÷ Total Shares
