Lighting is a filmmaker's secret weapon, and most beginners skip it. It feels awkward at first. You're not sure what a light is supposed to do, so you just leave it alone.

It gets fun once you can look at a shot and feel that something is missing. So you add a lamp. Put a bit of light on the wall. Take a bit off the face. And suddenly it clicks. Crews on big movies will spend thirty minutes nudging a single light to get that feeling.

By the end you'll know what to buy, where to put it, and how to add lamps, haze and texture. Most chapters have a slider you can drag to see the effect yourself.

01Fundamentals

Three-point lighting (and why nobody uses it)

You've probably heard of three-point lighting. It's the first thing everyone teaches. You point three lights at one person and get a neat, flattering picture.

The key light is your main light. It goes off to one side at about 45°, so it puts some shadow on the face. That's why ring lights, the kind TikTokers use, are the least cinematic light you can buy. They sit around the camera, so there are no shadows and no shape. Fine for a dance video. Not for a film.

The fill light sits on the other side and gently lifts the shadows. Often it isn't even a light. It's just a white board that bounces a little of the key back onto the face. The hair light sits behind and higher up. It outlines the shoulders and hair so the person pops out from the background.

Here's the catch with three-point lighting: almost nobody actually uses it.

No filmmaker walks into a room and thinks, "where does my three-point setup go?" Do learn what each light does. But a stock setup gives you a stock-looking shot. Below, the left side is a clean, by-the-book three-point setup. The right side uses just one light. Drag the handle and see.

Buying advice

Please don't buy a ring light.

02Key light

Rembrandt lighting

When you place your key light, think of Rembrandt, the famous Dutch painter. His portraits have a little upside-down triangle of light on the cheek that's away from the lamp.

This look keeps shadows on the face but still shows off the eyes and cheekbones. You also get a soft shadow under the chin. It looks natural and flattering. It isn't a rule for every shot, but if you can spot that little triangle, your light is probably in a good spot.

03Camera placement

Shoot the shadow side

This tip costs nothing and instantly makes your shots look better.

Your key light is set. Now where does the camera go? Imagine a line down the middle of your subject. If the light is on one side, put the camera on the other side. Don't put it on the same side as the light.

Watch any Hollywood movie and try to spot a shot taken from the lit side of an actor's face. You'll hardly find any. The shadow side feels more dramatic and makes the face look round and real instead of flat.

Filming in a kitchen? Walk to the other side of your light. Outside, with the sun as your key? Walk to the other side of the sun. You're not making a silhouette, just a more cinematic look. It's a tiny move with a big payoff.

Try it in the simulator

Flip the camera between "Key side" and "Shadow side" and watch the Contrast box. The picture gets moodier on the shadow side, even though the light never moved.

04Motivation

Motivated light

Nothing breaks the spell faster than a bright light hitting someone's face for no reason.

Motivated light just means the audience can see where the light seems to come from: a lamp, a window, a screen, a neon sign, a TV. That makes the light feel natural. The thing in the shot doesn't have to do the real lighting. It only has to explain it.

On screen

David Fincher and Stanley Kubrick fill their frames with lamps. The camera moves around a lot, and they want the light to make sense from every angle, even though big studio lights above are doing the real work. In Martin Scorsese's Silence, the characters get harsh side light. A wide shot shows one window behind them with light landing on the wall, and that explains the side light.

So find something to put in the scene. Even a tiny bulb can explain a whole lighting setup. A window in the corner of the frame is enough, and it can even be fake.

05Quality of light

Soft light and hard light

Soft light fades gently, makes shadows smooth and skin look nice. Hard light makes sharp-edged, harsh shadows.

Both have their uses. When in doubt, go soft on faces, unless the scene needs drama.

The best trick for soft light: make the light source bigger. You don't need a pricier light, just a bigger softbox (a fabric box that spreads the light out). The closer it is to your subject, the bigger it looks, and the softer the light. Pros often park it just a hair outside the frame.

Film sets do the same thing with a big 8×8 fabric called a silk. It's fancier than a bedsheet, but the idea is identical. If your footage looks harsh, cheap or "video-y", the light is probably too hard about 90% of the time. Bring it closer and make it softer.

06Outdoors

Golden hour

Outside, the sun is the boss. The best times to shoot are the first hour after sunrise and the last hour before sunset.

Three shots, one day: 7 a.m., noon and 7 p.m. Easy to tell which ones look cinematic, right? The only thing that changed was the time. The iPhone 17 ad in the video was shot just as the sun went down, so the light was soft and even. At noon it would have looked awful.

During golden hour, try putting the sun behind your subject and shooting toward it. You get pretty glowing flares. At other times of day, pointing at the sun usually turns the sky into a white blob.

07Environment

The background and practicals

Nobody says "I can't wait to light the background!" That's exactly why it's the most overlooked part of lighting.

Most people light the person first and forget everything behind them. But the background needs to feel alive too. Light only the subject and your shot looks like someone standing in a spotlight on a stage.

The fix is almost always practicals: lights you can actually see in the shot. Lamps, candles, windows, LED strips, signs. They do three jobs. They explain where your light comes from. They give the background depth. And they add warmth and life.

On screen

Watch any movie and you'll spot lamps in hallways where nobody would put a lamp in real life. They're not there to make it brighter. Cinematographers know how a lamp changes the feel of a scene. Steven Spielberg has even been seen adding lamps and leaving them switched off, just so the set looks lived-in.

You can put lamps behind your subject, bounce a light off the wall behind them, or fake a window. Don't forget the background. It helps your light look good.

08Control

Shaping your light

Beginners spray light everywhere. Too dark? Add another light! Pros take the light they already have and shape it.

Shaping means not blasting everything with the same brightness. You block some light, cut some away, and choose where it lands. You decide what's soft, what's hard, where the shadows go, and where the viewer looks. Each tool below controls one thing about your light.

Softness

Softbox

Soft light that wraps nicely around a face. Bigger box, softer light.

Room glow

Lantern

Spreads light in all directions. Great for a gentle, natural glow in the room.

Stray light

Barn doors

Metal flaps on the front of a light. They keep the beam off the wall or ceiling.

Fill

Reflector or bounce

Bounces light back onto the subject, no extra lamp needed.

Brightness

Scrim

Makes the light dimmer without changing how soft it is. Like tinted car windows.

Blocking

Flag

A black panel that blocks light from where you don't want it. No barn doors? A flag does the same job.

Aim

Honeycomb grid

Comes with most softboxes. Narrows the beam so only your subject gets lit. Think barn doors for a softbox.

Shape

Projector and gobos

Projects shapes onto walls: window streaks, blinds, logos, textures.

Having a light is good. Shaping it is what tells the story. That's the gap between a shot that's merely lit and one that looks like a movie.

09Workflow

Set up the background first

7%That's how strong the key light was in the lesson's shot. At 100%, you're doing it wrong.

Light the room first. Then put your subject in it and slowly turn the key up from zero until they fit in.

The usual order

Subject first

  1. Put the person in place.
  2. Turn on every light.
  3. Turn the camera brightness down until the face looks right.
  4. Oops, the background is now black.
The better order

Room first

  1. Light the background. Turn on lamps. Add texture.
  2. Set the camera brightness for the background you want.
  3. Put the person in. Start the key at 0%.
  4. Slowly raise the key until they look like they belong.

The same trick fixes blown-out windows, where the window turns pure white behind your subject. Set the camera for the window first. Then bring in your key light and raise it until the person matches that background. Get the background looking right and blend the person into it, not the other way around.

10Texture

Texture and gobos

Texture means light that isn't flat and even across a wall. Streaks, beams, patterns, anything that breaks up the space behind your subject.

Real places are full of shadows and patterns from plants, windows and lamps. So you make your own. For a long time the author's shots had a lit subject and good brightness, but the background still felt dead. Then they bought a projector attachment with gobos, and now use it on almost every shot, from ads to simple talking-head videos.

A gobo is a little cutout pattern that sits in front of a light, usually inside a projector attachment. Slide in window blinds, tree branches or a random shape, and it shows up on the background. Keep it sharp for a dramatic streak. Blur it for a soft, subtle texture.

On screen

In the last season of Stranger Things, look at the hospital scenes. Almost every wall has a soft, blurry pattern on it. Someone did that on purpose. A flat wall looks like a cheap set. A textured wall looks real.

If you're buying one add-on, a gobo projector is one of the cheapest ways to make your footage look expensive.

11Atmosphere

Fog and haze

Add a little haze and a so-so shot suddenly looks like it came from a $20,000 cinema camera. Nothing else changed.

Haze does two things: it makes your light visible, and it softens it. A sharp light like a flashlight shows up as a beam. Softer lights get a gentle glow. The Gatorade ad shot on the $100 camera had haze all through the building, so every light had a beam. It works for product videos, short films and interviews. Even a little bit makes things look way more expensive.

Haze outdoors is harder. Rental machines cost thousands and need a crew. The video's trick: an insect fogger filled with mineral oil instead of bug spray. Instantly, an outdoor fog machine.

Safety

The video says never use this fogger indoors. It's very powerful. Outside only, follow the maker's instructions, and keep it away from people and pets.

12Shadows

Negative fill (a fancy word for shadow)

Fill light brightens the shadow side of a face. Negative fill does the opposite: it keeps the shadows deep.

Crews often put a white board next to the actor to bounce light back onto the face. For negative fill, swap it for a black flag or black cloth just outside the shot. It soaks up light, so nothing bounces off the wall onto the face. It works outdoors in the sun too, keeping shadows on the face. The effect is subtle, but it adds shape, even in a bright place.

Shadows are
not mistakes.

The video's author says modern movies confuse them. Cameras can now see in the dark so well that filmmakers have to choose what to hide, and "show everything" has somehow become the idea of cinematic. It isn't. Films from before 2010 have deep shadows, realistic light and carefully shaped faces. These days faces get blasted with light and shadows disappear. Then we wonder why the movie feels fake, flat or too clean. That's the author's opinion.

Too many creators panic when they see a shadow. They think something's wrong, so they add more LEDs and lamps and bounce boards until the shot is bright, even and boring. If your lighting looks flat, try taking light away instead of adding it. Behind-the-scenes clips of pro interviews and ads show negative fill everywhere. Let the shadows stay and make them look intentional.

13Gear

Gear at three budgets

Buy what you can afford and grow your kit slowly. The author's first lights were cheap photo softboxes and Home Depot lamps.

Think in three groups: lights, shaping tools (modifiers) and stands and holders (grip). For lights, the author uses and recommends Nanlite. Their view: quality close to Aputure for a lot less money, and nowhere near the roughly $15,000 an ARRI SkyPanel costs. Every shot in the video used Nanlite. Treat it as one person's pick.
