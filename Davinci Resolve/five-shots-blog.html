Why your footage doesn't add up to a story

You don't need to feel inspired every time you pick up a camera. In fact, it's completely normal for your mind to go blank. So you walk around, film whatever catches your eye, and hope it will somehow make sense later.

The problem shows up later, in the edit. You sit down with the footage and realise there's no story to tell — just a collection of beautiful shots. Footage that looks nice but doesn't actually say anything.

The fix isn't more talent, better timing, or waiting for creativity to strike. It's a system: five essential shots that can help you tell almost any story. Once you know them, you'll notice you already film most of them by accident — you just haven't been using them on purpose.

SHOT 01 / 05

The Establishing Shot — where are we?

You can't tell a story if nobody knows where it's happening. The wider the shot, the more scale it gives the story. It's what tells someone: this is a small island, this is a packed stadium, this is the middle of nowhere. This is always the first shot to look for.

What's an "establishing shot"? It's the very first, widest shot of a scene — the one that shows the whole location before the camera moves in close. It answers "where are we?" before the viewer starts asking "who?" or "what?".

SHOT 01 — WIDE FRAME, SMALL SUBJECT

If the place is the main character, make yourself — or your subject — small in the frame. It helps the viewer feel the scale.

If you're filming a coffee shop, start outside the building and let the atmosphere set in — the cars and motorbikes passing by. If you're hiking, don't start with your shoes; start with the mountain. If you're exploring a market, capture the whole atmosphere before you show a single stall.

Filming yourself in this shot

The hard part about including yourself in the establishing shot is that nobody's there to help with the framing. So block it out first: press record, walk to where you think you should stop, and check. Once it looks right, mark the spot — with your eyes, a rock, anything that reminds you where to stop. Then reset and film the real take.

And if you genuinely can't get the establishing shot you need — no drone, no way to physically get far enough back — stock footage can fill that gap perfectly. More on that later.

The Wide Action Shot — what's happening?

Once the audience knows where they are, it's time to show them what's happening. Still in a wide shot, but now the focus shifts from place to action.

A few specific, small actions — captured in the same wide shot — tell the viewer what's about to happen.

This shot should feel like the viewer is just observing, not like you're performing for the camera. A simple trick: put something in the foreground, between the camera and yourself, so it feels like the viewer is watching through something rather than being shown it directly.

What's "foreground framing"? It's placing an object — a branch, a cup, someone's shoulder — close to the lens so it partially blocks the shot. It adds depth and makes the viewer feel like a fly on the wall, not an audience being addressed.

SHOT 03 / 05

The Medium Shot — who's it happening to?

Now bring the camera a little closer. The question to ask here is: who or what is this moment happening to? The wide shot introduced you to the story. The medium shot connects you to the subject inside it.

CAMERA MOVES CLOSER, TO THE SUBJECT

Same distance as before, just closer — the camera and the person are now clearly in conversation.

Most people feel uncomfortable filming this shot, including seasoned creators — the camera is right there, and there's nowhere to hide from it. The fix isn't trying to "act natural." It's giving your hands a job before you hit record: pour something, fold something, tie your shoelace — anything.

Here's a trick that also makes editing easier: pick one or two actions you already did in the previous (wide) shot, and just do them again for this shot. It feels awkward and repetitive while filming — but in the edit, you'll thank yourself for pushing through, because now you have two angles of the same action to cut between.

SHOT 04 / 05

The Close-Up — the one detail

After the medium shot shows the person, this is where everything slows down. The close-up is simple: it's just one detail. This is the shot that makes people say a video "feels cinematic" — and it has nothing to do with camera gear. It's just attention.

THE APERTURE CLOSES IN ON ONE DETAIL

Just like a camera's aperture closing in, this shot narrows everything down to a single point of focus.

This is also the shot to have the most fun with — save it for last, because by this point it's basically just play. It's worth overshooting this part: the extras become b-roll you can reuse later.

What's "b-roll"? It's supplementary footage — cutaways, textures, small details — that isn't the main shot, but that you layer into the edit to add rhythm, cover a jump cut, or add visual interest.

SHOT 05 / 05

The POV Shot — put the viewer inside it

This shot pulls the viewer into the moment instead of letting them watch it from outside. It's the first shot where they stop feeling like an observer and start feeling placed inside the scene.

SHARP BACKGROUND, BLURRED FOREGROUND

Manual focus, locked on the background — the foreground (your shoulder, an object you're holding) stays softly blurred.

To get this, switch to manual focus and lock it onto whatever's in the background, so your own shoulder — or whatever's in the foreground — stays blurred. This works with almost any moment. The viewer just needs to feel like someone is there with them, inside the shot.

Manual focus, in plain terms: most cameras and phones auto-focus on whatever's in the center. Manual focus lets you "lock" the sharp point yourself — here, on the background — so the foreground stays soft instead of the camera constantly hunting for focus.

Putting the five shots together

Establishing → Wide Action → Medium → Close-Up → POV. In that order, almost any moment becomes a scene.

What's a "shot list"? It's simply a plan of the shots you intend to film for a scene, written out before you press record. Knowing these five roles in advance turns "what do I film next?" into a checklist.

Filming solo: filling the gaps you can't shoot

As a solo creator, you don't always have access to crazy locations or gear — most of the time it's just you and a tripod. But what you can always control is the edit. That's where a stock footage and music library becomes genuinely useful, not just a nice-to-have.

Filling in the missing Shot 1

Sometimes there's no drone, no way to physically get far enough back to show the scale an establishing shot needs. That's exactly when a stock footage library — the video mentions Storyblocks specifically — earns its keep: a wide aerial or landscape clip can stand in for the establishing shot you couldn't film yourself.

  • An AI color-and-framing matching tool can help a stock clip blend with your own footage, even when the original color or framing doesn't quite fit.
  • The library isn't just video — it also includes sound effects and motion graphic templates, all made by working filmmakers, artists, and musicians.
  • Most platforms make you search like Google: type the exact word, hope it matches. A conversational search instead lets you describe a feeling — "something that feels lonely," "something that feels like coming home," "the calm right before a storm" — and it understands you. The same works for music and sound effects.

That's really the whole point of any tool in this process — the shot list, the edit, the stock library. They all exist to support the story. You film what you can, and you find the rest where you can. But the story stays the most important part. Everything else is just there to serve it.

Play the system, not the mood.

It's okay if your mind goes completely blank before a shoot. You don't need inspiration to start — sometimes inspiration shows up after you start. These five shots are just the cheat sheet that gets you moving.
