Section One

The big picture

The color page looks like the helm of a spaceship, but you only need a few panels. The idea is simple: whatever shot is in the viewer is the shot you are grading. No dragging effects onto clips.

PartWhat it is
ViewerShows the shot you are working on (the one your playhead is over).
ThumbnailsEach thumbnail is one shot in your timeline. Click one to grade it.
Color palettesThe panels along the bottom. They are your controls for that shot.
Node graphLittle boxes joined by lines. Every correction you make lives in a node.
  1. Put your clips in a timeline (for practice: select the clips in the color folder, right-click, Create New Timeline Using Selected Clips).
  2. Click the Color tab at the bottom to open that timeline on the color page.
  3. Click a thumbnail, then use the palettes. Only that shot changes.

Your grade shows up on the Edit page too, with no rendering or round-tripping. You can export the edit with color applied.

💡 Gray vs rainbow numbers

A clip's number under its thumbnail is gray if you have not touched its color, and turns rainbow once you have. Great for spotting shots you missed.

Section Two

Color management (do this first)

🎬 Try it: log footage → correct

Flat, gray, low contrast. That is what log looks like out of the camera.

Many pro cameras shoot in log. Log squeezes more brightness and color information into the file by making the picture look flat, gray and dull. Think of a great tent packed in a bag: all the good stuff is there, but you must unpack it by following the instructions.

💡 What is Rec.709?

Rec.709 is the color space that looks right on normal screens (like this video). If your footage is already Rec.709, it needs no color management. Log footage does.

You could add contrast and saturation by eye, but that is slow and you are only guessing how bright things should be. Color management reverses the camera's own math instead.

  1. In the media pool, right-click your timeline → Timelines → Timeline Settings. Untick Use Project Settings and go to the Color tab.
  2. Change color science from DaVinci YRGB to DaVinci YRGB Color Managed.
  3. Slightly better quality: untick Automatic color management, then set Color processing mode to HDR and the working space to DaVinci Wide Gamut Intermediate. Click OK.
  4. Nothing changes yet. In the media pool, select all clips from the same camera, right-click → Input Color Space, and pick your camera and color science (e.g. Blackmagic Pocket 6K, Film Gen 4).
  5. Done. The footage now looks correct and you did not move a single slider.
  • Not sure of your camera's color science? Look in the camera menus (picture style) or Google it. You need to know it to do this properly.
  • Footage not shot in log, or unknown? Set Input Color Space to Same as Timeline or Rec.709 and Resolve leaves it alone.
  • iPhone 15/16 Pro shooters can pick Apple Log. Canon, Nikon, Sony, Leica, Samsung are in the list too.
  • Do not skip this step. It gives higher quality and saves lots of time.

🌗 Which wheel touches what?
LiftGammaGain

Tap a zone. Offset moves the whole bar.

Open the Primaries – Color Wheels palette (bottom left). Three kinds of control: the color wheel, the master wheel slider under it, and number boxes.

  • Color wheel: drag the center dot. The farther from center, the more color. The direction is which color (drag lower right = blue).
  • Master wheel: slide right for brighter, left for darker (or use the scroll wheel).
WheelAffects
OffsetThe whole image.
LiftMostly the dark parts (shadows).
GammaMostly the midtones.
GainMostly the bright parts (highlights).

💡 Easy contrast recipe

Gain master wheel → right (brights brighter). Lift master wheel → left (darks darker). That is contrast.

Number boxWhat it does
ContrastDarks darker, brights brighter.
PivotWhere Resolve splits dark from light. Left = more counts as bright. Right = more counts as dark.
TempLeft = cooler/blue, right = warmer.
TintBalances green vs magenta.
Shadows / HighlightsLike master wheels for the darker and brighter midtones.
SaturationHow strong all colors are.
HueChanges actual colors. Rarely used.
Color BoostLike saturation, but targets the less saturated colors.

Double-click any control to reset it. The reset button resets the panel. Right-click a node → Reset Node Grade resets that whole node.

💡 Homework

Take a shot and improve it using only primaries. Try a little contrast, a slightly bluer temp, a bit of offset, some saturation or color boost.

💡 Storytelling with tone

For a sunset look, lower exposure, add contrast, push gain toward orange, lower the pivot and drop saturation a touch.

Section Four

Curves

📈 Build an S-curve
0

Straight line = no change. Bend it into an S and the shot gets that filmic contrast.

The second most useful panel. The graph shows input brightness (bottom, left = black, right = white) against output brightness (up). A straight diagonal line means no change.

  • Pull a point up = that brightness gets brighter. Pull it down = darker.
  • Bottom-left = blacks (like lift). Top-right = whites (like gain). Drag the black point right, along the floor, to make more things pure black.
  • S-curve: brights up, darks down. This gives the classic filmic contrast. Every filmic look has some version of it.
  • Lift the blacks a little, crush the lower mids, pull highlights down and push upper mids up for a nice highlight roll-off.
Curve typeUse it to…
Hue vs HueChange a specific color into another (red house → purple).
Hue vs SatBoost or reduce saturation of one color (make trees gray).
Hue vs LumBrighten or darken one color (darken a backpack).
Lum vs SatControl color by brightness. Desaturate near-white and near-black so saturation stays in the mids.
Sat vs SatTame colors that are too saturated.
Sat vs LumChange brightness depending on how saturated a color is.

💡 Click the image

With a hue curve open, click or drag on the image. Resolve adds control points for the color you picked.

💡 Best way to change a color

Use a hue vs hue curve. It is much harder to break the image than with extreme wheel pushes.

Section Five

Scopes: trust data, not your eyes

🎯 Live vectorscope
40

Nice and natural. The dashed ring is the edge: stay inside it.

Your eyes get used to any color in about 10 seconds. Scopes never do. Find them in the lower-right Color Scopes panel.

Waveform: left-right = position, up-down = brightness

Every pixel is a dot. A bright lamp on the right shows up as a spike high on the right.

ScopeHow to read it
WaveformBrightness of things. Scale is 0 (pure black) to 1023 (pure white). Real images rarely touch both ends.
ParadeThree waveforms: red, green, blue. Equal levels = neutral (gray/white/black).
VectorscopeA color wheel. Center = no color. Farther out = more saturated. Direction = hue. Skin sits on the orange line.
Histogram / CIEAlso available in the dropdown.
  1. White balance with parade: find something that should be gray (pavement, a wall). Find it on the parade.
  2. If red or blue is higher than green, the image is tinted. Use offset until the three channels line up.
  3. Blue high and green high too means cyan. Balance until they match, or use temp and tint.
  • Keep saturation from touching the outer edge on the vectorscope. Aim for roughly the middle to outer part. Turn on extents to see the most extreme colors.
  • Too small and the image looks black and white, unless you want that.
  • Perfect neutral whites are not a rule. Most movies have a tint. Style matters more.

💡 Reset your eyes

Decide in the first few seconds of viewing a shot. Then hop between shots with the up/down arrows, or look away from the screen, then look back.

Section Six

Nodes, copying grades & the look

A node is a group of corrections. Nodes run in order, left to right, so order matters: black and white then pink gives pink; pink then black and white gives black and white.

Serial nodes: each one works on the last one's result
Exposure→Contrast→Warmth→Look
ActionHow
Add serial nodeRight-click → Add Node → Add Serial, or Alt+S
Add parallel nodeRight-click → Add Node → Add Parallel, or Alt+P
Turn one node on/offClick its number
Disable selected nodesCtrl+D
Rename a nodeRight-click → Node Label
Reset a clipRight-click empty node space → Reset All Grades and Nodes
Copy a gradeSelect target shot, then middle-click (scroll wheel press) the source thumbnail

Shift-select several shots to paste one grade onto all of them. If they already have grades, Resolve asks whether to replace the active grade.

Clip nodes vs timeline nodes

  • Clip nodes: each clip has its own tree. Use them for fixing and matching individual shots.
  • Timeline nodes: switch with the little dots above the node graph (the right dot). These apply to every clip, after each clip's own nodes. This is the place for your creative look.

Smart workflow

  1. Set up color management.
  2. Pick one good shot. Make sure exposure and white balance are decent.
  3. Build the look in the timeline nodes (a small S-curve, a slightly cooler temp, a touch of saturation).
  4. Go back to the clip nodes and tweak each shot to match, so all shots feel like the same world.
  5. Only then do fancy polish, if you have time.

💡 Keep the look gentle

A look that is heavy (like an extreme teal-and-orange) exaggerates differences between shots. Good shooting, lighting, set design and costumes get a film 80–90% of the way there. Grading is the salt on top. Study the color-managed footage and enhance the style already there.

💡 Check saturation on the vectorscope

If one color (like blue) shoots way out, use hue vs sat to pull just that color down.

Hero shot and stills

  1. Grade your best shot. Right-click the viewer → Grab Still. It saves to the Gallery (upper-left) as a preset with a thumbnail.
  2. Middle-click a still to apply its grade to any shot.
  3. Right-click a still → Play Still for a split-screen comparison with the current shot.
  4. Compare every shot to this one still. If you only match neighbors, colors drift and the first and last shots end up different.

Decide fast if a match is close enough. You can return to it later. Adjust in clip nodes, not timeline nodes.

Section Seven

Secondaries: windows, tracker, qualifier

🔵 Soft circle window
70
35

No cut-out. A big, soft circle only limits where the correction happens.

A primary correction affects the whole image. A secondary affects only part of it: an area (window) or a specific color or brightness (qualifier or curves).

ToolHow to use it
Windows palettePick a shape (circle, gradient…). Corrections in that node now only happen inside the shape. The image is not cut out.
SoftenPull the red dots to feather the edge. A big soft circle does most jobs (brighten a face, darken a side, vignette).
Add another windowClick the plus circle. Multiple windows add together in one node.
Gradient windowApplies the correction and fades it out along a line. Good for darkening one side.
OverlaySwitch to another palette or toggle overlays to hide the outlines.
Key tabShows the selection you have made.
Tracker tabSelect a window, click the track back-and-forth button. It locks on and follows the eye, lips, or face.

💡 Parallel or serial?

Whole-image fixes: serial nodes. Several local fixes (darken side, brighten eyes, tone lips): parallel nodes. Each one starts from the same original image, which helps when selecting by color.

Qualifier (pick by color)

  1. Add a parallel node. Go to the Qualifier tab (the fourth tab) and pick the eyedropper.
  2. Drag over the skin. Turn on Highlight mode to see what is selected.
  3. Adjust the sliders (turn off saturation or luminance if they do not help) and softness.
  4. Clean up in Matte Finesse: raise Pre-filter a bit, use Clean Black / Clean White to fill holes.
  5. Add a circle window to limit it further, then switch off highlight mode.
  6. Use Mid Detail (pulled left) for gentle skin smoothing. Don't overdo it.

💡 Warning

Extreme changes on a qualifier selection show the ragged edges. Keep beauty work subtle, and change colors with hue vs hue curves instead. Some automatic selections exist in the paid version.

Do not start here. Look and matching first. Details later.

Section Eight

Log wheels, HDR wheels & panels

Beginners can ignore these. Primaries do 99% of what you need.

PaletteWhat it is
Log wheelsSplits the image into shadows, midtones and highlights, with range sliders to say where each begins and ends. The name does not mean it is only for log footage.
HDR wheelsSame idea, but six zones: specular, highlights, light, shadow, dark, black. Each has range and softness. Click the sun icon to see what a zone affects.

Use them for very targeted fixes, for example darkening just a bright window without touching anything else. Lower the highlight range until the window counts as a highlight.

Color panels (hardware)

PanelPrice (approx.)
Micro Color Panel$559. Trackballs for lift, gamma, gain, rings for master wheels. Works with iPad.
Mini PanelAbout $2,300
Advanced PanelAbout $30,000

Nothing can only be done on a panel. It is just faster and more fun. Worth it if you grade over about a thousand shots a week. For a video every couple of weeks with about 80 shots, you do not need one.
