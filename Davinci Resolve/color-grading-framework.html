Chapter One

The big idea: go from broad to narrow

Here is the one idea that makes much easier: start big, then go small.

First, you fix the whole picture. You set how bright it is, how strong the colors are, and what mood it has. Only when the whole shot looks good do you zoom in on smaller things, like one face or one object. In DaVinci Resolve, these smaller jobs sit later in your chain of .

Some people call this the top-down approach. Others call it broad to narrow. Same thing.

💡
Quick tip: what is color grading?

It means changing the colors, brightness and mood of a video after you have filmed it. DaVinci Resolve is an editing app that is famous for its color tools (and it has a free version).

Think about painting your house before Diwali. You paint all the big walls first. Only after that do you touch up the door frames and window edges. Nobody paints every door first and then tries to paint the wall around it. Color grading works the same way.

🎯 Why this saves you a headache

If you start with small jobs (like cutting people out so you can change only the background), you will later have to flip that cut-out around. That flip is what causes trouble. Going broad to narrow means you never have to think about the background separately. Let's see exactly what goes wrong in the next chapter.

Chapter Two

The "bad thing": edge fringing

Maybe you remember a warning that "something bad" happens when you cut people out and change only the background. That bad thing has a name: edge fringing, also called a .

Here is what happens. You draw a around the people so you can boost the color of the background only. But fine hair, motion blur and soft edges never get cut out cleanly. You end up with an odd glowing outline or a dark border around the person.

It is like cutting yourself out of a photo with scissors and pasting it on a new sheet of paper. The edges look "cut out", and the eye spots it straight away.

❌ Cut-out way

Mask the people, boost only the background

You can see a hard line around the person.

✅ Face-tweak way

Boost the whole frame, then soften only the face

The change fades in slowly. Nobody notices.
🧠
Why hair and blur cause trouble

A single strand of hair is thin, so the pixels around it are part hair and part background. A mask can only say "inside" or "outside". Those mixed pixels get treated the wrong way, and that leaves the glowing or dark edge.

Why the face-tweak way looks invisible

When you a mask inward on a face to bring the color down a little, the eye accepts it easily. Skin naturally reacts to the light in the room, so a small change on a face feels natural.

But when you cut a hard hole in the background and push color up right around a person, the viewer immediately notices the boundary line. Same tool, very different result.

Chapter Three

Flip your workflow: room first, faces later

The fix is simple. Turn your workflow upside down. Grade the whole room first, then calm down the faces afterwards.

1
Grade the entire room first (a global node) Push the , and overall color mood across the whole frame, early in your chain. Let the background look rich and vibrant, even if the faces get a bit too orange, red or oversaturated for now.
2
Tame the faces downstream (secondary nodes) Add nodes (later in the chain) with a to each face. In these nodes you slightly lower the saturation or adjust the , lift or drop the exposure so faces pop out from the room, and soften the window edges heavily.

❌ The hard way

Small jobs first

  1. Cut the people out with a mask
  2. the mask
  3. Boost the background
  4. Fight with edges and outlines
Risk: edge fringing

✅ The easy way

Big jobs first

  1. Boost the whole frame
  2. Add a Power Window on each face
  3. Soften the window edges
  4. Adjust the skin gently
No inverting. No outlines.

Working downstream like this removes the need to invert masks, wrestle with , or worry about edge fringing at all.

✅
The takeaway

Do the big, easy change on everything. Then fix only the small parts that came out wrong. Fixing faces is much easier than cutting them out.

Chapter Seven

Three golden rules

Whatever node tree you build, follow these three rules. They keep your work clean and easy to fix.

Rule 1: Always use Offset first

bend specific parts of the picture's brightness curve, and that can skew colors. is different: it raises or lowers every pixel equally. That keeps the original balance the camera sensor recorded. Think of Offset as one master dimmer switch for the whole room.

OffsetThe whole line slides. Nothing gets bent.
Lift / Gamma / GainParts of the line bend. Colors can shift.

Rule 2: One task per node

Never mix a tracked face mask and a master white balance fix in the same node. Keep each job in its own node. Then you can switch a single node off and on to check what it really does: press Ctrl + D on Windows, or Cmd + D on Mac.

Rule 3: Label every node

Right-click a node and choose Node Label. Give it a short name. When you open the shot again weeks later, you will know what each node does at a glance.

Why toggling matters

Switching a node off shows you the picture without it. If the shot looks better with the node off, you have just found a bad adjustment. This only works if the node holds one job.

Chapter Eight

Phase 4: texture & grain

This is the part that confuses most beginners. Why would anyone add grain on purpose? Let's make it simple.

Think of a tiny pinch of salt in your food, or the tadka on top of a dal. It goes in at the very end, in a small amount, and it ties the whole dish together. Or think of a photo printed on textured paper compared to a glossy phone screen.

Modern digital cameras capture images that are so clean, sharp and flat that people can look slightly plastic or synthetic. is just a layer of tiny, microscopic dots scattered across the whole video, added at the very end.

Three reasons people add grain

🎬
It makes your video look like a real movie Movies shot on traditional film naturally have texture. A tiny touch of grain removes the cheap, sterile "digital webcam" look and makes the video feel cinematic.
🧱
It stops YouTube from ruining your walls Film a plain flat wall and upload it, and YouTube compresses your file. Smooth walls can break into ugly, blocky stripes. That is called . Subtle grain gives the compression tiny details to work with, so the wall stays smooth.
🩹
It hides your editing seams You isolated faces and changed the background separately, so mask edges can look a little cut-out. One even layer of grain over the whole frame sits on top of everything. It works like a light blending filter that ties the shot together.
⚙️
The technical reason behind reason 2

Screens with color and web (like YouTube's H.264 or AV1) cannot show very subtle gradients smoothly. Grain works as : the microscopic random dots break up flat color areas, so the encoder renders the gradient smoothly instead of in blocky bands.

Try it yourself

Press the three buttons below. First you see a smooth wall. Then you see what heavy compression can do to it. Then you add grain and watch the stripes melt away.

Original: smooth wall

This demo is exaggerated on purpose so you can see the idea clearly. Real banding is subtler, and real grain should be much gentler.

The three texture tools in Resolve

🎞️

Film Grain

A effect that copies the tiny chemical crystals of 16mm or 35mm film. Keep the grain size small and the strength low.

🔍

Midtone Detail

Found in the Primary Wheels palette. Lower it slightly to soften harsh skin blemishes without blurring fine hair. Raise it to add crisp texture to clothing and hair.

🌅

Halation

A soft, warm glow around high-contrast edges, where bright lights meet dark boundaries. It copies how light scatters through the layers of analog film.
